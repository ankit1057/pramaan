The story

Meera and her little brother Arjun are flipping a bottle cap to decide who bats first. Cap up, Meera bats. Cap down, Arjun bats.

Arjun loses four times running. He is furious. "Flip again," he says, "cap down is due now. It has to be my turn."

Meera picks up the cap and turns it over in her hand. "Show me the part of this cap that remembers," she says. "Which bit of it knows it went up four times?"

Arjun looks. It is a bottle cap. It has a ridged edge and a bit of red paint. There is nowhere for a memory to live. The cap cannot know, so it cannot owe him anything.

Then Meera does something else. She takes four mangoes and two guavas and puts them in a cloth bag, and pulls fruit out without looking, keeping each one. "Now," she says, "if I pull out three mangoes in a row, is a guava more likely next?"

Arjun thinks, and says yes. And this time he is right.

Here is the surprise: the bag remembers and the cap does not. The bag gets emptier as you take from it. The cap is brand new on every single flip. Telling those two situations apart is most of what probability is.

Say it simply

Probability puts a number between 0 and 1 on how often something happens when you repeat an experiment many times. Zero means never, one means always, and a half means "as often as not".

There are two ways to get that number. You can count the possibilities, if they are all equally likely: three of the six faces of a die are even, so the probability of an even roll is one half. Or you can measure it: roll the die ten thousand times and look at the fraction that came up even.

The one-line takeaway: the counted probability is what the theory predicts, the measured frequency is what reality delivers, and the whole subject lives in the gap between them — a gap that shrinks as you collect more data, but shrinks slowly.

Say it precisely

Imagine you have a magic bag holding three red balls and one blue ball.

  1. The Basics of Guessing: - Experiment: Pulling a ball out of the bag without looking. - Outcomes: The colors you could get. Here, you could get a red ball or a blue ball. - Sample Space: The complete list of everything in the bag: [Red, Red, Red, Blue]. - Event: What you want to happen. Like saying, "I want to pull a blue ball!"

  2. The Magic Fraction (Probability): To figure out your chances of winning, make a simple fraction! - Put the number of winning balls on top (one Blue ball). - Put the total number of all balls on the bottom (four balls total). - Your chance is one out of four!

  3. The Impossible and The Certain: - Impossible (Zero): What is the chance of pulling a green ball? Zero! There are no green balls. It is impossible. - Certain (One): What is the chance of pulling either a red or a blue ball? One! It is a total guarantee because that is all there is in the bag. - Chances are always between zero and one. You can never have a negative one chance or a two chance.

  4. The Opposite Trick (Complement): If the chance of picking a blue ball is one out of four, what is the chance of NOT picking a blue ball? It is just the rest of the fraction! One whole minus one fourth equals three fourths. It is usually much faster to calculate the chance of losing and subtract it from a total certainty!

Why it's true

Why does a flipped coin never remember? (The Gambler's Fallacy) Imagine you flip a coin and it lands on 'Heads' five times in a row. You might think, "It MUST be Tails next time! It owes me a Tails!" But look closely at the coin. Does it have a brain? Does it have a tiny notebook where it writes down its past flips? No! The coin is completely mindless. Every single time you flip it, it is a brand-new fifty-fifty chance. It does not know what happened five seconds ago.

Wait, doesn't it all balance out eventually? Yes, but not by "canceling out" the past! Imagine you flip a coin ten times and get eight Heads and two Tails. You have six extra Heads! Now, imagine you flip that exact same coin a million times. You will probably get very close to five hundred thousand Heads and five hundred thousand Tails. Those six extra Heads from the very beginning are still there, but mixed into a million flips, they are so incredibly tiny you cannot even notice them anymore! The coin did not magically "fix" the mistake; it just drowned the tiny mistake in a giant ocean of new flips.

Oracle lab

The oracle computes the same probability two genuinely independent ways. The first enumerates the entire sample space: it generates every sequence of heads and tails of length n, works out the probability of each individual sequence, and adds up the ones that have exactly k heads. The second walks a tree diagram by recursion: at each stage it branches into head and tail, multiplies by that branch's probability, and accumulates the probability that arrives at a successful leaf.

They share no helper. The enumerator builds complete sequences and filters them; the recursion never builds a sequence at all. They agree only if both the counting and the multiply-along-a-path rule are right. The mutant below adds along the path instead of multiplying, which is the single most common error when a learner first meets a tree diagram.

Experiment lab
The theory says the empirical frequency settles down on the theoretical probability, and it says how fast: the typical distance between the measured probability and the true probability shrinks in proportion to one over the square root of n, not one over n. Multiplying the number of trials by 100 should therefore cut the typical error by about 10, not by 100. That is a prediction precise enough to be wrong, so we measure it.

            Misconceptions
              

            • "After five heads, tails is more likely." This is the gambler's fallacy. A fair coin has no mechanism that stores the past, so the next flip is 0.5 whatever came before. What the law of large numbers promises is that the proportion settles down, by dilution, not that an existing imbalance gets repaid.
              • 

            • "Probability means what will happen in this one trial." It is a statement about a long run of repetitions, or about a degree of belief. A 70 percent chance of rain does not make a dry day evidence that the forecast was wrong.
              • 

            • "Equally likely is the default." It is an assumption that must be earned by the physical set-up. The two outcomes "I win the lottery" and "I do not" are not equally likely, so you cannot conclude the probability is one half.
              • 

            • "An event with probability zero is impossible." For finite sample spaces that holds, but when outcomes form a continuum every individual outcome has probability zero while something still happens. Probability zero means negligible, not forbidden.
              • 

            • "Drawing with and without replacement are nearly the same." They differ precisely when the population is small. Taking 3 mangoes from a bag of 6 changes the odds enormously; taking 3 from a lorry-load barely moves them.
              • 

            • "More trials make the count of heads get closer to half." The proportion converges, but the expected gap in the raw count actually grows like the square root of the number of flips.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            The counting counting formula applies only to finite sample spaces whose outcomes are genuinely equally likely. Drop either condition and it gives nonsense. For continuous outcomes — a waiting time, a measurement — you need densities and integration instead of counting, and the notion of "the probability of exactly 3.7 seconds" stops being useful.
            

            Independence is an assumption about the world that is easy to state and easy to get wrong. Repeated trials on the same hardware, the same day, or the same user are usually correlated, so multiplying probabilities as if they were independent understates the chance of a joint failure, sometimes by orders of magnitude.
            

            Simulation has its own limits. A software generator seeded identically produces identical streams, which is exactly what we want for a reproducible test but means the "randomness" is a fiction we control. A bad generator, or reusing one stream for two purposes that should be independent, will quietly produce correlated results that look fine.
            

            Finally, everything here is unconditional. Once evidence arrives — a test came back positive, the first draw was red — you need conditional probability and Bayes' theorem, which is M27.
            In your real work
            Every retry policy, cache-hit estimate and flaky-test diagnosis is this module. "The test fails one run in twenty" is an empirical probability; "three retries makes it fine" is the complement rule, one minus zero point zero five cubed, and it is only valid if the failures are independent — which for a flaky test sharing one machine they usually are not. Reasoning about tail risk from a handful of observations, and knowing that a measured rate from 20 trials is nearly uninformative, is standard L5 interview and design-review ground.
            Flash cards
            What three things define a random experiment in probability?
            A procedure whose single result is unpredictable, a known set of possible outcomes called the sample space, and events defined as subsets of that sample space.
            Why must every probability lie between 0 and 1?
            Because a favourable count cannot be negative and cannot exceed the total count, so the ratio is pinned between 0 and 1.
            State the complement rule and say why it is useful.
            P(not E) = 1 - P(E). It turns a hard "at least one" count into an easy "none at all" product.
            What is the gambler's fallacy, in one sentence?
            Believing that an independent mechanism owes you a compensating result because of what it did before.
            Does the law of large numbers say the count of heads approaches half the flips?
            No. It says the proportion approaches one half. The expected gap in the raw count actually grows, roughly like the square root of the number of flips.
            What are the two rules for reading a tree diagram?
            Multiply the probabilities along a path, and add the probabilities of the separate paths that satisfy the event.
            When does the past genuinely change the next probability?
            When the mechanism has memory, such as drawing without replacement, where each draw changes what remains.
            If one trial succeeds with probability p, what is the chance of at least one success in n independent trials?
            One minus the probability of no successes, which is 1 - (1 - p) to the power n.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why a coin has no memory but a bag of fruit does, you can derive the complement rule and use it to answer an "at least one" question without counting cases, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can say precisely what the law of large numbers promises and what it does not.
