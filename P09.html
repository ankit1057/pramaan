The story

In May 1974, Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn published their historic paper "A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication" in the IEEE Transactions on Communications. Prior to their breakthrough, computer networks were closed, proprietary islands that relied on dedicated circuit switching—monopolized telephone lines reserved end-to-end for a single conversation. Cerf and Kahn introduced the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) and Internet Protocol (IP), proposing an open, decentralized architecture where autonomous networks could interconnect seamlessly. Data was carved into self-contained packets that hopped independently across intermediate gateways, dynamically routing around damaged links without centralized supervision.

Twelve years later in October 1986, the young Internet suffered its first catastrophic crisis: the Internet Congestion Collapse. The data throughput across the transcontinental NSFNET backbone dropped by a factor of one thousand, plummeting from 32,000 bits per second to a pathetic 40 bits per second. Packets flooded network routers faster than outgoing links could transmit them, filling router buffers, triggering massive drops, and causing hosts to resend duplicates in an exponential feedback loop. UC Berkeley researcher Van Jacobson rescued the global Internet by inventing TCP congestion control. By formalizing Slow Start, Additive Increase Multiplicative Decrease (AIMD), and dynamic round-trip time estimation, Jacobson transformed TCP into a cooperative, self-stabilizing distributed algorithm that allowed the global Internet to scale to billions of connected devices.

Say it simply

The Internet is a vast, decentralized web of connected machines that talk by passing digital envelopes called packets. Instead of reserving a continuous physical line between you and a website, your computer chops data into small numbered packets, stamps them with destination IP addresses, and hands them to routers. TCP ensures that packets arrive intact and in order, using a 3-way handshake to connect and a sliding window to regulate speed. If the network becomes congested, TCP automatically slows down via AIMD and ramps back up gently, ensuring fair sharing across millions of users.

Say it precisely

Imagine the Internet is the world's biggest postal service!

  1. The Four Layers of the Post Office: - Application: Writing the letter (like typing a website name). - Transport: Packing the letter into numbered boxes so none get lost. - Internet: Putting the GPS address (IP Address) on the boxes so the mail trucks know where to go. - Physical: The actual trucks, airplanes, and WiFi invisible lasers that carry the boxes!

  2. The Traffic Jam Rule (Congestion Control): If you send 1,000 boxes at once, the mail truck will explode! So, your computer plays a safe game: - Start Slow: It sends 1 box. If it gets a "Thank you!" note back, it sends 2 boxes. Then 4, then 8! - Cruising: When it's going fast enough, it only adds 1 extra box at a time. - Uh oh! (Loss): If a box gets dropped, the computer immediately cuts the number of boxes it sends in half. This instantly clears up the traffic jam for everyone!

  3. How Fast Can You Throw? (BDP): Imagine throwing baseballs to a friend far away. If it takes 10 seconds for a ball to reach them, and you can throw 5 balls a second, there will be 50 balls flying through the air at the same time! If your friend only brought a tiny basket that holds 10 balls, you have to throw slower, even though your arm is super strong.

  4. Upgrading the Delivery Trucks (HTTP): - Old Trucks (HTTP/1): One box at a time. If the first box gets stuck, the whole truck stops (Head-of-Line blocking). - New Trucks (HTTP/3): It sends hundreds of boxes on different tiny motorcycles. If one motorcycle crashes, all the other boxes still arrive safely!

Why it's true

A long time ago, phones used "Circuit Switching." When you called your grandma, the telephone company literally locked a continuous, unbroken copper wire stretching all the way from your house to her house. If you stopped talking to chew a cookie, that wire sat totally empty, and nobody else was allowed to use it! Also, if a tree fell on the wire, the call immediately dropped.

The Internet is much smarter. It uses "Packet Switching." Instead of locking down a whole road, your computer chops your movie into millions of tiny puzzle pieces (packets). Each piece gets an address sticker and is tossed onto the highway.

Because they are independent, if a tree falls on the highway, the puzzle pieces just take a detour down a side street! And because the road isn't locked, thousands of people can share the same highway at the exact same time without waiting in line. The computer at the very end just collects all the puzzle pieces and glues them back together into a movie!

Oracle lab

The TCP AIMD congestion control verification oracle simulates how a sending endpoint dynamically adjusts its congestion window (cwnd) across slow start, congestion avoidance, packet loss, and fast recovery. The interactive oracle verifies exponential growth during slow start, linear increments during congestion avoidance, and multiplicative halving upon packet loss detection. This simulation accurately captures Van Jacobson's self-stabilizing transport algorithm that prevents Internet congestion collapse across diverse network links.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "UDP is inherently unreliable and therefore inferior to TCP." UDP trades guaranteed delivery for minimal latency and zero connection overhead; it powers real-time multiplayer gaming, live video streaming, DNS lookups, and modern HTTP/3 QUIC transport.
            • 

          • "Bandwidth and latency are interchangeable measures of network speed." Bandwidth is the pipe width (bits per second); latency is the propagation delay (milliseconds). High bandwidth cannot overcome speed-of-light propagation latency on round trips.
            • 

          • "HTTP/2 solves all Head-of-Line (HoL) blocking problems." HTTP/2 multiplexes streams over a single TCP connection; a single lost packet on that TCP connection stalls all multiplexed streams at the transport layer. HTTP/3 solves this using QUIC over UDP.
            • 

          • "NAT (Network Address Translation) was designed as a security firewall." NAT was created as an emergency stopgap to mitigate IPv4 address exhaustion by sharing one public IP across multiple private LAN addresses; security benefits were purely accidental.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Computer network protocols break down under bufferbloat when unmanaged router queues grow excessively large, introducing seconds of queueing latency that ruin interactive voice and video calls. Furthermore, mobile wireless links violate traditional TCP assumptions: transient signal fades cause packet drops that TCP mistakenly interprets as network congestion, cutting throughput unnecessarily.
          In your real work
          Network architecture and socket programming govern mobile application engineering (optimizing HTTP/3 connections in Flutter apps), distributed microservices (Envoy proxy gRPC load balancing), and global CDN edge routing (Cloudflare, Fastly) where BGP Anycast routes user DNS requests to the nearest data center.
          Flash cards
          What is the difference between TCP and UDP?
          TCP provides reliable, ordered, connection-oriented byte streams with congestion control; UDP provides lightweight, unordered, datagram-oriented transmission.
          What is the Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP)?
          The maximum amount of data in flight required to fully saturate a link: Bandwidth * RTT.
          What is Additive Increase Multiplicative Decrease (AIMD)?
          The congestion control algorithm where cwnd increases linearly by 1 MSS per RTT and halves multiplicatively upon packet loss.
          What causes Bufferbloat?
          Overly large packet buffers in routers that fill up and introduce seconds of latency without improving network throughput.
          How does HTTP/3 differ from HTTP/2?
          HTTP/3 replaces TCP with QUIC over UDP, eliminating transport Head-of-Line blocking and enabling 0-RTT handshakes.
          What is the role of DNS?
          Translates human-readable domain names into 32-bit IPv4 or 128-bit IPv6 network addresses.
