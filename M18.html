The story

Five cousins are lined up for a photograph at a wedding: Anu, Bir, Chetan, Dia and Esha.

The photographer is fussy. He tries them in one order, frowns, and swaps two. Then another order. The children get bored and start asking how many times he could possibly do this before he runs out of arrangements.

Anu works it out. Five choices for who stands first. Whoever that is, four are left for second place. Then three, then two, then one. Five times four times three times two times one. A hundred and twenty photographs.

Then Dadi comes out with three identical laddoos and says three of the five may have one.

"A hundred and twenty again?" says Bir hopefully.

Anu starts listing. Anu-Bir-Chetan. Anu-Chetan-Bir. Bir-Anu-Chetan... and stops, because these are all the same three children. The laddoos are identical. Nobody can tell which one they got.

So she counts how many times each group of three gets written down. Three children can be ordered in six ways. Every single group is being counted six times over.

A hundred and twenty divided by six. Ten.

Here is the surprise: the hard count and the easy count are the same count, divided by the number of distinctions you have decided not to care about. Almost every counting problem you will ever meet is this one move — count it the easy way with everything distinguishable, then divide out the sameness.

Say it simply

Counting carefully is a real skill, and the whole subject rests on two rules.

Multiplication principle: if a choice has m options and an independent second choice has n, the pair has m times n options.

Division principle: if your count sees each genuine outcome exactly k times, divide by k.

From these, everything follows. A permutation is an arrangement where order matters. A combination is a selection where it does not — and it is just the permutation count divided by the orderings you are ignoring, exactly as Anu discovered.

The binomial theorem then notices something lovely: expanding (a+b)^n means choosing, from each of n brackets, whether to take a or b. So the coefficients are combinations. Algebra and counting turn out to be the same subject written twice.

Say it precisely

Sometimes we want to figure out how many ways we can arrange things, or choose things, without actually having to list every single one out and count them with our fingers. When the order matters, like people sitting in chairs in a row, we call that a permutation. If you swap who sits where, it counts as a totally new arrangement! But when the order does not matter at all, like picking friends for your team, we call that a combination. If you pick the same friends but just call their names in a different order, it is still the exact same team! We also have a neat triangle trick, called Pascal's triangle, where you can find how many ways to choose teams just by adding the two numbers right above it. And if you want to know what happens if everyone accidentally takes the wrong coat at a party, we call that a derangement.

Why it's true

Result 1: Permutations. Think of playing musical chairs. You have ten kids and three chairs. For the first chair, you have ten kids to pick from. For the second chair, nine kids are left. For the third, eight kids are left. To find the total number of ways they can sit, you just multiply ten times nine times eight. You are basically taking the total arrangements of everyone, but cancelling out the arrangements of the poor kids who did not get a chair!

Result 2: Combinations. Imagine taking a photo of a team of kids. Every team can be posed in different left-to-right orders. So each unordered team corresponds to many different ordered photographs. The ordered count therefore counts each team many times. To find just the number of unique teams, we take the total number of photographs and divide it by the number of ways a single team can be arranged. This shrinks the huge number of photos down to just the unique teams!

Result 3: The binomial theorem, by counting. Imagine having mystery boxes, each containing an apple and a banana. You want to pick one fruit from every box. Expanding a math expression is exactly like choosing, from each box independently, either the apple or the banana, and seeing what snack bag you end up with. How many ways produce an exact snack bag with three bananas? You just need to choose which three boxes give you a banana: that is exactly the number of combinations!

Result 4: The pigeonhole principle. Suppose you have ten pigeons and nine pigeonholes. Imagine every pigeonhole had at most one pigeon. Then the total number of pigeons would be at most nine. But we have ten pigeons, which is a contradiction! So at least one hole must contain two or more pigeons. The principle is almost insultingly simple and yet proves non-obvious things: among any thirteen people, two must share a birth month because there are only twelve months!

Result 5: Derangements. Let us count how many ways a party goes wrong, where at least one person takes their own coat. We figure out the arrangements where person A gets their coat, then person B, and so on. But if we just add those up, we double-count the times when both A and B get their own coats! So we have to subtract the overlaps. But wait, then we subtracted too much for when A, B, and C all get their coats! So we have to add that back. This back-and-forth adding and subtracting is called inclusion and exclusion.

Oracle lab

The oracle counts everything twice: once by brute-force enumeration, generating the actual arrangements and selections and counting them, and once by formula, doing arithmetic with factorials. Enumeration knows no formulas; the formulas never build an arrangement. If a factorial were misplaced, or a division forgotten, the two would disagree.

This is the honest way to check a counting formula, and it is exactly what you should do when a counting argument in real work feels slippery: shrink the problem until you can enumerate it, and check. Experiment lab
Two predictions.


First, derangements. Theory says the proportion of permutations with no fixed point tends to 1 / e, approximately 0.3679, and remarkably fast. So: if everyone at a party of n people takes a coat at random, the chance nobody gets their own barely depends on n at all once n is past about 5. We compute the sequence three independent ways — brute-force enumeration, the recurrence, and the inclusion-exclusion formula — and watch the ratio.


Second, the binomial series. For x between minus one and one and a non-integer exponent the series is infinite. Theory predicts it converges to one plus x to the power n inside that radius and diverges outside it. We measure both halves.

Where reality disagrees: the derangement ratio converges so fast that by n=8 it already matches about 37 percent to about six decimal places — which is why the "nobody gets their own coat" probability is essentially the same for 10 people and for 10,000. The binomial series is the opposite story: at x is 0.9 it converges, but slowly, and at x is 1.5 it does not converge at all even though square root of one plus x is a perfectly ordinary number there. The series has a radius of convergence, and outside it the formula is simply not a valid representation of the function. Writing down a series is not the same as the series being usable, and that distinction is the whole content of M33.

            Misconceptions
              

            • "Permutation and combination are two formulas to memorise." They are one count and a division. If you can say what you are over-counting and by how much, you never need to recall which formula is which.
              • 

            • "zero factorial is zero." It is 1. There is exactly one arrangement of nothing, and every boundary case of every formula here depends on it.
              • 

            • "Stars and bars needs the boxes to be identical." The opposite: the boxes must be distinct and the items identical. Swap which side is identical and the count is completely different — that is a partition problem with no simple closed form.
              • 

            • "The binomial theorem needs a whole-number exponent." The finite version does. The series version works for any real exponent, but only for x between minus one and one, and that restriction is not negotiable.
              • 

            • "Pigeonhole is too obvious to be useful." It proves that two people in London have the same number of hairs on their heads, and it is the standard tool for showing a hash collision or a repeated state must exist. Obviousness and power are unrelated.
              • 

            • "The chance nobody gets their own coat must drop as the party grows." It converges to about 37 percent almost immediately, and is essentially the same for 10 guests and for a million.
              • 

            • "Inclusion-exclusion is just subtract the overlap." For two sets, yes. For k sets there are two to the power k minus 1 terms with alternating signs, and stopping after the pairwise subtractions is a classic undercount.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Closed-form counting runs out quickly. Counting the ways to partition n identical items into identical boxes has no simple formula at all — the partition function p of n grows subexponentially and its asymptotics are a deep result of Hardy and Ramanujan. Counting labelled graphs, or permutations avoiding a pattern, lands in the same territory.
            

            The formulas also assume exact arithmetic. combinations of 100 things taken 50 at a time is about 1 followed by 29 zeroes, far beyond a 64-bit integer, so in any fixed-width language these calculations overflow silently. Python's arbitrary-precision integers hide the problem; Dart, Java and C++ do not. Computing combinations as combinations formula also overflows far earlier than necessary, since the answer may be small while the intermediate factorials are astronomical. The fix is to multiply and divide alternately, keeping the running value small — the same spirit as the stable quadratic formula in M13.
            

            The binomial series diverges outside |x|<1, and no number of terms repairs that. Near the boundary convergence is slow enough to be useless in practice.
            

            Finally, independence is an assumption the multiplication principle quietly requires. If the second choice depends on the first, m times n is wrong, and the correction is conditional counting — which is M27.
            In your real work
            This is where complexity estimates come from. "Try every subset" is two to the power n, "try every ordering" is n factorial, and knowing that 20 factorial exceeds 1 followed by 18 zeroes while one million is only a million tells you instantly which brute force is feasible. The pigeonhole principle is the argument behind every hash-collision discussion, including why a 64-bit hash over four billion keys is not safe, and the birthday bound is this module plus M10. Inclusion-exclusion is what makes a query over overlapping filters count correctly. Pascal's identity is the recurrence behind the classic dynamic-programming table, and the overflow caveat above is a real bug people ship whenever they compute a binomial coefficient in a fixed-width integer type.
            Flash cards
            What is the single idea that turns a permutation count into a combination count?
            The division principle: the ordered count sees each unordered selection exactly r! times, so divide by r!.
            Why is 0! defined as 1?
            Because there is exactly one arrangement of nothing, and because the permutation and combination formulas require it at their boundaries.
            Give the combinatorial proof of Pascal's identity.
            Count the r-subsets of an n-set by whether they contain element n. If they do, the rest is an (r-1)-subset of n-1; if not, it is an r-subset of n-1. The cases are disjoint and exhaustive.
            Why are the binomial coefficients combinations?
            Expanding (a+b)^n means picking a or b from each of n brackets. The coefficient of a^(n-r) b^r counts which r brackets gave the b, which is C(n,r).
            State the pigeonhole principle in its sharper form.
            Placing n items into m boxes forces some box to hold at least the ceiling of n/m.
            How many ways to put n identical items into k distinct boxes, empties allowed?
            C(n+k-1, k-1), by placing k-1 bars among n stars.
            What is a derangement, and what does D(n)/n! tend to?
            A permutation with no fixed point. The ratio tends to 1/e, about 0.3679, and converges very fast.
            When is the binomial series for (1+x)^n valid for non-integer n?
            Only for |x| < 1. Outside that radius the series diverges even though the function is perfectly well defined.
            Why can computing C(n,r) as n!/(r!(n-r)!) overflow unnecessarily?
            Because the intermediate factorials are enormous even when the answer is small. Multiplying and dividing alternately keeps the running value near the final size.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why the laddoo count is the photo count divided by six, you can derive the permutation and combination formulas from the multiplication and division principles rather than recall them, you can give a combinatorial proof of Pascal's identity without algebra, you can recognise a stars-and-bars problem and say which side must be identical, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can estimate whether a brute force over subsets or over orderings will finish before you do.
