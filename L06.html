The story

In 2013, Tomas Mikolov and his team at Google published word2vec, astonishing the computational linguistics community. By training a shallow two-layer network to predict neighboring words from a target word (Skip-gram), the resulting continuous vector embeddings captured rich semantic geometry. Vector arithmetic famously yielded $\mathbf{v}_{\text{King}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{Man}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{Woman}} \approx \mathbf{v}_{\text{Queen}}$. But whole-word tokenization suffered from a fatal flaw: vocabulary explosion. Languages with rich morphology, typos, or compound words produced millions of out-of-vocabulary (OOV) tokens represented as useless [UNK] tokens. In 2016, Rico Sennrich realized that a decades-old data compression algorithm—Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)—could iteratively merge frequent pairs of characters into subwords. Today, byte-level BPE forms the universal input layer of every modern large language model, breaking text down into dense numeric tokens that bridge raw human language and high-dimensional vector spaces.

Say it simply

Computers cannot directly read text words or letters; they only understand numbers. Tokenization splits sentences into smaller chunks called tokens—which can be words, syllables, or single bytes. Byte-Pair Encoding automatically discovers the most common subword pieces by repeatedly merging frequent character pairs. Each token is then mapped to a list of numbers called an embedding vector. Words used in similar contexts end up with vectors that point in similar directions in multi-dimensional space, allowing neural networks to calculate meaning using geometry and cosine similarity.

Say it precisely

Let $\mathcal{V}$ be a finite vocabulary of subword tokens with size $|\mathcal{V}| = V$. A tokenizer is an invertible mapping $\mathcal{T}: \Sigma^* \to \mathcal{V}^*$ that segments a string of characters or raw bytes into a sequence of token IDs $(t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N)$ where $t_i \in \{0, \dots, V-1\}$.

An embedding matrix $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}$ maps each discrete token ID $t_i$ to a dense continuous vector $\mathbf{e}_i = \mathbf{E}[t_i] \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The geometric similarity between two embeddings is measured by cosine similarity: $$\operatorname{cos\_sim}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2}$$

In next-token prediction, a language model outputs a probability distribution $P(t_k \mid t_1, \dots, t_{k-1})$. The quality of the model over an evaluation sequence of length $N$ is measured by Perplexity (PPL): $$\operatorname{PPL} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N \ln P(t_k \mid t_1, \dots, t_{k-1})\right) = \left(\prod_{k=1}^N \frac{1}{P(t_k \mid t_1, \dots, t_{k-1})}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}$$ Perplexity represents the effective branching factor: a perplexity of $K$ means the model is as uncertain as choosing uniformly at random among $K$ possible tokens at each step.

Why it's true

The foundation of vector embeddings rests on the Distributional Hypothesis formulated by linguist J.R. Firth in 1957: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." If two words appear in virtually identical surrounding contexts (such as 'feline' and 'cat'), their semantic roles are interchangeable, and their vector representations should be geometrically close.

Word2vec operationalizes this principle through the Skip-gram objective with negative sampling. For a center word $w_t$ and context window $c$, it maximizes: $$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t} \sum_{-c \le j \le c, j \neq 0} \left[ \ln \sigma(\mathbf{v}'_{w_{t+j}}^\top \mathbf{v}_{w_t}) + \sum_{k=1}^K \mathbb{E}_{w_n \sim P_n}[\ln \sigma(-\mathbf{v}'_{w_n}^\top \mathbf{v}_{w_t})] \right]$$ where $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{v}'$ are target and context embedding matrices, and $P_n$ is a unigram noise distribution raised to the $3/4$ power.

However, whole-word vocabularies cannot generalize to unseen words. Character-level tokenization eliminates OOV tokens but blows up sequence length, making attention quadratically expensive. Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) finds the optimal Pareto frontier: 1. Start with a base vocabulary of single characters or 256 byte values. 2. Count all adjacent symbol pairs in the corpus. 3. Merge the most frequent pair $(c_1, c_2) \to c_{\text{new}}$, adding $c_{\text{new}}$ to the vocabulary. 4. Repeat until reaching the desired vocabulary size $V$.

To evaluate probabilistic language models against standard non-neural baselines, we use $n$-gram models. An $n$-gram model makes a Markov assumption of order $n-1$: $$P(w_k \mid w_1, \dots, w_{k-1}) \approx P(w_k \mid w_{k-n+1}, \dots, w_{k-1}) = \frac{C(w_{k-n+1}, \dots, w_k)}{C(w_{k-n+1}, \dots, w_{k-1})}$$ Because unseen $n$-grams produce zero probability and infinite perplexity, smoothing techniques like Laplace (add-1) or Kneser-Ney smoothing are essential.

Implement BPE training over a corpus text:
train_bpe(corpus_text, num_merges):
1. Split corpus_text into whitespace-separated words.
2. Represent each word as a tuple of single-character strings appended with end-of-word '</w>'.
3. Track frequencies of each word tuple.
4. For each merge iteration up to num_merges:
   - Count frequencies of all adjacent character pairs across all word tuples.
   - If no pairs exist, break early.
   - Find the most frequent pair $(c_1, c_2)$. (Break ties using Python's default order).
   - Create merged token $c_{\text{new}} = c_1 + c_2$.
   - Record merge rule ((c_1, c_2), c_new).
   - Update all word tuples by replacing occurrences of $(c_1, c_2)$ with $c_{\text{new}}$.
5. Return tuple (vocab_list, merge_rules) where vocab_list is a sorted list of unique subword tokens.

Requirements:
1. Split train_text and test_text by whitespace.
2. Count unigrams $C(w)$ and bigrams $C(w_i, w_{i+1})$ in train_text.
3. Let $V$ be the number of unique unigrams in train_text.
4. For each bigram $(w_i, w_{i+1})$ in test_text:
   - Compute smoothed conditional probability with Laplace (add-1) smoothing:

5. Compute Perplexity:
   $$\operatorname{PPL} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \ln P(w_{i+1} \mid w_i)\right)$$
   where $M$ is the number of bigram transitions in test_text.
6. Return float perplexity (return 1.0 if $M == 0$).

          Misconceptions
            

          • "Tokenizers understand the semantic meaning of the words they produce." Tokenizers are purely statistical string segmentation algorithms; semantic meaning emerges solely from the weights and embeddings of the downstream neural network.
            • 

          • "BPE tokenization eliminates all unknown tokens under any input." Standard BPE can still encounter unknown characters if unseen in the base alphabet; byte-level BPE solves this completely by initializing its base vocabulary with all 256 raw byte values.
            • 

          • "Cosine similarity of 1.0 means two vectors are mathematically identical." Cosine similarity only measures angular alignment: $\mathbf{u}$ and $2\mathbf{u}$ have a cosine similarity of exactly 1.0 despite having different Euclidean lengths.
            • 

          • "Lower perplexity guarantees that a language model outputs truthful facts." Perplexity strictly measures statistical predictability of the next token; a model can generate hallucinated nonsense with very low perplexity if the false text is grammatically fluent.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Subword tokenizers break down when confronted with whitespace-free languages or unusual character concatenations that produce catastrophic token segmentation splits, and word embeddings fail when polysemous words (e.g. 'bank' as a financial institution vs a river edge) are forced into a single static context-free vector across different linguistic contexts. In addition, rare domain-specific acronyms can fragment into inefficient single-byte sequences that bloat attention memory.
          In your real work
          Every production language model — from GPT-4 to LLaMA — starts with byte-level BPE tokenisation and an embedding lookup. In a mobile edge deployment the subword vocabulary is often pruned from 128k to 32k tokens, cutting hundreds of megabytes from the resident memory footprint without measurably degrading accuracy on the target domain.
          Flash cards
          What is Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)?
          An iterative data-driven subword tokenization algorithm that merges the most frequent adjacent character pairs into new vocabulary tokens.
          Why is byte-level BPE superior to character-level BPE?
          Byte-level BPE uses all 256 possible bytes as its base vocabulary, ensuring zero out-of-vocabulary (OOV) tokens for any arbitrary input string.
          What is the Distributional Hypothesis in linguistics?
          The principle that words appearing in similar linguistic contexts tend to share similar semantic meanings ('you shall know a word by the company it keeps').
          What does language model Perplexity measure?
          The exponentiated cross-entropy loss, representing the effective number of equally likely tokens the model is choosing among at each step.
          How does vector arithmetic solve word analogies like King - Man + Woman?
          By translating the vector along semantic difference directions (such as gender or tense) in a learned geometric embedding space.
          Why do static word2vec embeddings struggle with polysemy?
          Because static embeddings assign exactly one vector per word, conflating distinct word senses into an unweighted geometric average.
