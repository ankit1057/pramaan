The story

Priya is training a model that will not train. The loss goes up.

She halves the learning rate. The loss still goes up, slower. She halves it again — now it goes down, but so slowly that a run which should take an hour is projected at nine days. Somewhere between "explodes" and "glacial" there is a right answer, and she is finding it by bisection, one expensive run at a time.

Her colleague says what everyone says: learning rate is a hyperparameter, you tune it, that's the job.

But watch what happens when she tries it on a problem simple enough to analyse — a quadratic bowl, two variables, no noise. The same thing happens. Above a certain step size the iterates fly apart; below it they converge. And the threshold is not vague. It sits at exactly $\frac{2}{L}$, where $L$ is the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian. Cross it by one percent and the method diverges; stay under it and it cannot.

The second surprise is worse. Staying under the threshold guarantees convergence but says nothing about speed, and the speed is governed by a different number: the ratio of the largest eigenvalue to the smallest. Priya's bowl is a hundred times steeper in one direction than another, and gradient descent spends its time bouncing across the steep direction while creeping along the shallow one. No step size fixes that, because one step size has to serve both directions.

That is the whole subject. Which problems can be solved reliably (convex ones), how fast (set by conditioning), and what to do when one scalar step size is not enough (momentum, adaptive methods, or second-order information).

Say it simply

Convexity is the property that makes optimisation tractable. A convex function has no local minimum that is not global, so a method that goes downhill cannot get stuck in the wrong place.

Gradient descent repeatedly steps against the gradient. It works when the step is small enough — specifically below $\frac{2}{L}$ for an $L$-smooth function — and its speed is set by the condition number $\kappa=\frac{L}{\mu}$.

Everything else is a response to that. Momentum damps the oscillation across steep directions. Adam gives each coordinate its own effective step size. Newton's method uses curvature and removes the conditioning dependence entirely, at the cost of a Hessian. Line search picks the step by measuring instead of guessing.

For constrained problems the KKT conditions generalise "the gradient is zero", and duality gives a certificate: a number you can compute that proves how close to optimal you are.

Say it precisely

Convexity

A set $C$ is convex when $x,y\in C$ implies $\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\in C$ for all $\lambda\in[0,1]$ — the segment between any two points stays inside.

A function $f$ is convex when its domain is convex and

$$f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\le\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)$$

the chord lies above the graph. Equivalent characterisations for differentiable $f$:

  • First order: $f(y)\ge f(x)+\nabla f(x)^T(y-x)$ — the tangent plane is a global underestimator.
  • Second order: $\nabla^2f(x)\succeq0$, the Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere.

$f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex when $f-\frac{\mu}{2}\|x\|^2$ is convex, equivalently $\nabla^2f\succeq\mu I$. It is $L$-smooth when $\nabla f$ is $L$-Lipschitz, equivalently $\nabla^2f\preceq LI$.

The key consequence: for a convex $f$, $\nabla f(x^\*)=0$ implies $x^\*$ is a global minimum. For non-convex $f$ it implies nothing of the sort.

Gradient descent

$$x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k)$$

Assumptions Step Rate
convex, $L$-smooth $\alpha=\frac1L$ $f(x_k)-f^\*\le\frac{L\|x_0-x^\*\|^2}{2k}$, i.e. $O(1/k)$
$\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth $\alpha=\frac1L$ $\|x_k-x^\*\|\le\left(1-\frac1\kappa\right)^k\|x_0-x^\*\|$
same $\alpha=\frac{2}{\mu+L}$ $\left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^k$

with $\kappa=\frac{L}{\mu}$. Divergence for $\alpha>\frac2L$ is not a heuristic: on a quadratic the iteration is linear with factor $1-\alpha\lambda$ per eigendirection, and $|1-\alpha L|>1$ exactly when $\alpha>\frac2L$.

Linear ("geometric") convergence multiplies the error by a constant each step — the error's digits grow linearly. The $O(1/k)$ rate for merely-convex problems is far worse: ten times the accuracy costs ten times the iterations.

Momentum

Heavy ball (Polyak):

$$v_{k+1}=\beta v_k-\alpha\nabla f(x_k), \qquad x_{k+1}=x_k+v_{k+1}$$

Nesterov evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point:

$$y_k = x_k+\beta(x_k-x_{k-1}), \qquad x_{k+1}=y_k-\alpha\nabla f(y_k)$$

The payoff is the $\sqrt\kappa$ rate: momentum achieves $\left(\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}\right)^k$ where plain descent gets $\left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^k$. For $\kappa=100$ that is $0.82$ against $0.98$ per step — roughly a tenfold reduction in iterations. Nesterov's method is optimal: no first-order method can beat $O\!\left(\frac{1}{k^2}\right)$ on convex $L$-smooth problems.

Adaptive methods

RMSProp keeps a running average of squared gradients and divides by its root:

$$s_k=\rho s_{k-1}+(1-\rho)g_k^2, \qquad x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{s_k}+\epsilon}g_k$$

Adam adds momentum and bias correction:

$$m_k=\beta_1m_{k-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_k, \qquad v_k=\beta_2v_{k-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_k^2$$ $$\hat m_k=\frac{m_k}{1-\beta_1^k}, \qquad \hat v_k=\frac{v_k}{1-\beta_2^k}, \qquad x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{\alpha\hat m_k}{\sqrt{\hat v_k}+\epsilon}$$

The bias correction matters because $m_0=v_0=0$: both estimates start biased towards zero. The net effect on the step is not obvious, because the two biases pull in opposite directions — a small $m$ shrinks the step, a small $v$ shrinks the denominator and so inflates it. Working out the ratio at step $k$:

$$\frac{\text{uncorrected step}}{\text{corrected step}} = \frac{\sqrt{1-\beta_2^k}}{1-\beta_1^k}$$

At $k=1$ with the usual $\beta_1=0.9,\beta_2=0.999$ this is $\frac{\sqrt{0.001}}{0.1}=3.16$: the uncorrected first steps are about three times too LARGE, not too small, because the under-estimated denominator wins. With the correction, the first step has magnitude exactly $\alpha$ — which is the design intent, since $\frac{\hat m_1}{\sqrt{\hat v_1}}=\frac{g}{|g|}=\pm1$. Adam's step size is meant to be a trust region, and bias correction is what makes it one from the very first step.

Stochastic gradient descent

With $f=\frac1n\sum f_i$, SGD uses $\nabla f_i$ for a random $i$ (or a mini-batch average). Each step costs $O(1)$ instead of $O(n)$, and the gradient is unbiased: $E[\nabla f_i]=\nabla f$.

The price is variance. With a constant step SGD converges to a noise ball of radius $O(\alpha\sigma^2)$ around the optimum rather than to the optimum; convergence requires a decreasing step with $\sum\alpha_k=\infty$ and $\sum\alpha_k^2<\infty$. A mini-batch of size $b$ cuts the variance by $b$, hence the standard deviation by $\sqrt b$ — M34's $\frac{1}{\sqrt n}$ again, and the reason batch size has diminishing returns.

Newton and line search

$$x_{k+1}=x_k-\left(\nabla^2f(x_k)\right)^{-1}\nabla f(x_k)$$

Newton's method is affine invariant: rescaling the variables does not change its path, so the condition number does not appear in its rate. Near a minimum it converges quadratically, like M35's root finder. Costs: $O(d^3)$ per step for the solve, $O(d^2)$ memory, and a Hessian that must be positive definite.

Backtracking line search (Armijo) replaces guessing with measuring: start from $\alpha=1$ and halve until

$$f(x-\alpha g)\le f(x)-c\,\alpha\|g\|^2$$

with $c\in(0,1)$, typically $10^{-4}$. It needs no knowledge of $L$.

Constrained optimisation and duality

For $\min f(x)$ subject to $g_i(x)\le0$ and $h_j(x)=0$, the Lagrangian is

$$\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda,\nu)=f(x)+\sum\lambda_ig_i(x)+\sum\nu_jh_j(x)$$

The KKT conditions at an optimum (under a constraint qualification) are

  1. Stationarity: $\nabla f+\sum\lambda_i\nabla g_i+\sum\nu_j\nabla h_j=0$
  2. Primal feasibility: $g_i\le0$, $h_j=0$
  3. Dual feasibility: $\lambda_i\ge0$
  4. Complementary slackness: $\lambda_ig_i=0$ — an inactive constraint has zero multiplier

For convex problems KKT is sufficient as well as necessary.

Weak duality. The dual function $d(\lambda,\nu)=\inf_x\mathcal{L}$ satisfies $d(\lambda,\nu)\le p^\*$ for every feasible $(\lambda,\nu)$ — always, convex or not. For a linear program $\min c^Tx$ s.t. $Ax\ge b,x\ge0$, the dual is $\max b^Ty$ s.t. $A^Ty\le c,y\ge0$, and weak duality reads $c^Tx\ge b^Ty$. Strong duality ($d^\*=p^\*$) holds for LPs always, and for convex problems under Slater's condition. The gap $c^Tx-b^Ty$ is a computable certificate of suboptimality.

A pinch of history

Cauchy proposed gradient descent in 1847. Dantzig invented the simplex method in 1947 and LP duality followed from von Neumann's game-theoretic insight the same year. Karush wrote his conditions in a 1939 master's thesis that went unnoticed until Kuhn and Tucker rediscovered them in 1951. Polyak introduced the heavy ball in 1964; Nesterov's accelerated method and its optimality proof came in 1983. Khachiyan showed LP is in P in 1979 with the ellipsoid method, and Karmarkar made interior-point methods practical in 1984. Robbins and Monro published stochastic approximation in 1951, thirty years before anyone trained a neural network with it. Adam is due to Kingma and Ba in 2015.

Why it's true

Result 1: for convex $f$, a stationary point is a global minimum. The first-order condition says $f(y)\ge f(x)+\nabla f(x)^T(y-x)$ for all $y$. If $\nabla f(x)=0$ the inequality reads $f(y)\ge f(x)$ for every $y$. Done — in one line, and it is the entire reason convexity is the dividing line of the subject.

Result 2: the $\frac{2}{L}$ threshold. Take $f(x)=\frac12x^TAx$ with $A$ symmetric positive definite, eigenvalues $\lambda_i$. Then $\nabla f=Ax$ and

$$x_{k+1}=x_k-\alpha Ax_k=(I-\alpha A)x_k$$

In the eigenbasis each coordinate evolves independently as $(1-\alpha\lambda_i)^k$. Convergence requires $|1-\alpha\lambda_i|<1$ for every $i$, i.e. $0<\alpha<\frac{2}{\lambda_i}$. The binding constraint is the largest eigenvalue:

$$0<\alpha<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}=\frac{2}{L}$$

This is exact, not asymptotic. At $\alpha=\frac2L$ the largest mode oscillates with constant amplitude forever; above it, it grows geometrically. Priya's explosion was one eigendirection.

Result 3: why conditioning sets the speed. With $\alpha=\frac1L$ the per-step contraction in direction $i$ is $\left|1-\frac{\lambda_i}{L}\right|$. The slowest is the smallest eigenvalue:

$$1-\frac{\mu}{L}=1-\frac1\kappa$$

To reduce the error by $e$ needs about $\kappa$ iterations. The step size cannot help: raising $\alpha$ to speed up the shallow direction destabilises the steep one, because one scalar must serve every eigendirection at once. That tension is precisely what momentum, adaptive methods and Newton each attack differently.

Result 4: the descent lemma, and why $\frac1L$ is the natural step. $L$-smoothness gives

$$f(y)\le f(x)+\nabla f(x)^T(y-x)+\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^2$$

Substituting $y=x-\alpha\nabla f(x)$:

$$f(y)\le f(x)-\alpha\|\nabla f\|^2+\frac{L\alpha^2}{2}\|\nabla f\|^2 = f(x)-\alpha\left(1-\frac{L\alpha}{2}\right)\|\nabla f\|^2$$

The bracket is positive exactly when $\alpha<\frac2L$ — the same threshold, now from the function-value side — and the decrease $\alpha\left(1-\frac{L\alpha}{2}\right)$ is maximised at $\alpha=\frac1L$, giving $f(y)\le f(x)-\frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f\|^2$.

Result 5: weak duality in three lines. For any feasible $x$ (so $g_i(x)\le0$, $h_j(x)=0$) and any $\lambda\ge0$:

$$\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda,\nu)=f(x)+\underbrace{\sum\lambda_ig_i(x)}_{\le0}+\underbrace{\sum\nu_jh_j(x)}_{=0}\le f(x)$$

Taking the infimum over all $x$ on the left only decreases it, so $d(\lambda,\nu)\le f(x)$ for every feasible $x$, hence $d(\lambda,\nu)\le p^\*$. No convexity was used anywhere. This is what makes a dual feasible point a proof of a lower bound, and the duality gap a number you can report.

Oracle lab

The oracle optimises problems whose answers are known in closed form, so every iterate can be measured against the truth rather than against a convergence heuristic.

For a quadratic $\frac12x^TAx-b^Tx$ the minimiser is $A^{-1}b$, found by exact elimination. For the constrained problem the KKT system is linear and solved directly. For the LP, weak duality is checked as an inequality that must hold for every feasible pair.

The mutant drops Adam's bias correction — a real and frequently-made omission that leaves the first steps a thousand times too small.

Experiment lab
Four predictions.


First, the threshold is sharp. Theory says divergence at $\alpha>\frac2L$ exactly. We sweep across it and look for a cliff, not a slope.


Second, iterations scale with $\kappa$. Plain descent should need $O(\kappa)$ iterations and momentum $O(\sqrt\kappa)$, so multiplying $\kappa$ by 100 should cost 100× for one and 10× for the other.


Third, SGD with a constant step does not converge — it reaches a noise ball whose radius scales with the step size.


Fourth, non-convexity is a different world. Gradient descent on a function with several minima lands in whichever basin it started in, and no step size changes that.

Where reality disagrees: the sweep across $\frac2L$ is the part worth staring at. At $1.99/L$ the iterate reaches $10^{-6}$; at exactly $2.0/L$ it flips between $+1$ and $-1$ forever, neither converging nor diverging; at $2.01/L$ it reaches $10^{30}$. A half-percent change in one hyperparameter separates convergence from catastrophe, and the location of the cliff is computable in advance from the Hessian. Priya's nine-day run and her explosion were on either side of a number she could have predicted.


The conditioning table is the one with practical consequences. At $\kappa=1000$, plain descent needed over ten times the iterations of momentum, and the normalised columns confirm the exponents: $O(\kappa)$ against $O(\sqrt\kappa)$. This is why ill-conditioned problems are the ones people reach for Adam on — and why normalising inputs, which reduces $\kappa$ directly, is often worth more than any optimiser change.


The non-convex panel is the honest limit. Four starts, two destinations, both with vanishing gradients, one strictly worse — and sweeping the step size over a factor of 20 did not move the outcome. Every theorem above this line assumed convexity, and none of them applies here.

def test_the_stability_threshold_is_exactly_two_over_L():
    L = 20.0
    grad = quadratic_grad([1.0, L], [0.0, 0.0])
    limit = max_stable_step(L)
    # just inside, it converges; just outside, it blows up
    assert converges(grad, [1.0, 1.0], 0.99 * limit) is True
    assert converges(grad, [1.0, 1.0], 0.999 * limit) is True
    assert converges(grad, [1.0, 1.0], 1.05 * limit) is False
    assert converges(grad, [1.0, 1.0], 1.1 * limit) is False
    # The threshold is exact, not approximate. At 1.001 * limit the growth
    # factor is only 1.002, so it takes about 10000 steps to become visible -
    # give it the budget and it diverges just the same.
    assert converges(grad, [1.0, 1.0], 1.001 * limit, iters=20000) is False
    # the threshold is set by the LARGEST eigenvalue, not the smallest
    assert limit == 2.0 / L
    assert converges(grad, [1.0, 1.0], 2.0 / 1.0) is False, \
        "using the small eigenvalue would give a step that diverges"
    # a better-conditioned problem tolerates a bigger step
    easy = quadratic_grad([1.0, 1.0], [0.0, 0.0])
    assert converges(easy, [1.0, 1.0], 1.9) is True
    assert converges(easy, [1.0, 1.0], 2.1) is False

def test_adam_and_rmsprop_find_the_optimum():
    grad = lambda x: [1.0 * x[0] - 3.0, 20.0 * x[1] - 5.0]
    x = adam(grad, [0.0, 0.0], 0.5, 400)
    assert abs(x[0] - 3.0) < 1e-3, x
    assert abs(x[1] - 0.25) < 1e-3, x
    # A CONSTANT step leaves RMSProp in a limit cycle whose width scales with
    # the step: at 0.1 it settles 0.05 away from the optimum and stays there.
    loose = rmsprop(grad, [0.0, 0.0], 0.1, 0.9, 2000)
    assert abs(loose[1] - 0.25) > 1e-3, "a large constant step cannot converge exactly"
    r = rmsprop(grad, [0.0, 0.0], 0.01, 0.9, 2000)
    assert abs(r[0] - 3.0) < 1e-3, r
    assert abs(r[1] - 0.25) < 1e-3, r
    # The corrected first step has magnitude EXACTLY the step size, since
    # m_hat / sqrt(v_hat) = g / |g| = 1. Starting at the origin with a negative
    # gradient in both coordinates, one step must land on (step, step).
    one = adam(grad, [0.0, 0.0], 0.5, 1, correct=True)
    assert abs(one[0] - 0.5) < 1e-6, one
    assert abs(one[1] - 0.5) < 1e-6, one
    # Without correction it overshoots by sqrt(1-b2)/(1-b1) = 3.162 - the
    # early steps are too LARGE, not too small.
    one_u = adam(grad, [0.0, 0.0], 0.5, 1, correct=False)
    assert abs(one_u[0] / one[0] - 3.1622776) < 1e-4, one_u
    # and over a few steps the corrected run is closer to the optimum
    few = 20
    with_c = adam(grad, [0.0, 0.0], 0.5, few, correct=True)
    without = adam(grad, [0.0, 0.0], 0.5, few, correct=False)
    d_with = max(abs(with_c[0] - 3.0), abs(with_c[1] - 0.25))

    assert d_with < d_without, (d_with, d_without)
    for bad in (lambda: adam(grad, [0.0, 0.0], 0.5, 10, b1=1.0),
                lambda: rmsprop(grad, [0.0, 0.0], 0.1, 1.5, 10),

            Misconceptions
              

            • "A smaller learning rate is always safer." Safer from divergence, yes; but below $\frac1L$ you are simply paying more iterations for the same guarantee, and Priya's nine-day projection was the cost.
              • 

            • "Convergence and speed are the same question." $\alpha<\frac2L$ decides whether; $\kappa$ decides how fast. The two are controlled by different eigenvalues and no single step size serves both.
              • 

            • "Momentum just means bigger steps." It changes the dependence on conditioning from $\kappa$ to $\sqrt\kappa$ — a different exponent, not a constant factor. At $\kappa=10^4$ that is 100× fewer iterations.
              • 

            • "Adam is always better than SGD." Adam adapts per-coordinate scaling, which helps on badly-scaled problems. On well-conditioned ones well-tuned SGD with momentum often generalises better, and Adam's convergence proof in the original paper was later found to be flawed.
              • 

            • "Bias correction is a technicality." Without it the first step is about $3.16$ times larger than intended, because the second-moment estimate starts at zero and so under-estimates the denominator. The common guess — that the steps start too small — has the sign backwards; it is an early overshoot, and it is why Adam without correction wants a warmup.
              • 

            • "A zero gradient means a minimum." It means a stationary point: minimum, maximum or saddle. In high dimensions saddles vastly outnumber local minima, which is the real obstacle in deep learning — not local minima, as folklore has it.
              • 

            • "Newton's method is strictly better because it is second order." It costs $O(d^3)$ per step and $O(d^2)$ memory, needs a positive definite Hessian, and converges quadratically only near the optimum. For $d=10^9$ it is not a candidate.
              • 

            • "Duality is abstract theory." The duality gap is a number you compute and report. It is how a solver knows — and can prove — that it is within 0.1% of optimal without knowing the optimum.
              • 

            • "Non-convex means hopeless." It means no global guarantee. Gradient methods still work well in practice on neural networks; what you lose is the theorem, not the result.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Every rate above assumes $L$ is known. In practice it is not, and estimating it costs as much as the optimisation. This is the honest case for line search and for adaptive methods — they measure what the theory assumes.
            

            $L$-smoothness itself often fails. ReLU networks are not differentiable everywhere, and the loss landscape's local curvature varies by orders of magnitude across the trajectory, so a single global $L$ is a fiction. Gradient clipping is the practical patch.
            

            Strong convexity almost never holds in machine learning: overparameterised models have flat directions and whole manifolds of minimisers, so $\mu=0$ and the linear rate degrades to $O(1/k)$.
            

            For constrained problems, KKT requires a constraint qualification (Slater's condition or LICQ). Without one the multipliers may not exist at the optimum, and a solver that assumes them will report nonsense. Strong duality can also fail for non-convex problems — the gap is then genuinely positive and no algorithm closes it.
            

            SGD's convergence theory assumes unbiased gradients and bounded variance. Both are violated by gradient clipping (which biases) and by heavy-tailed gradient noise (which breaks the variance bound) — and M34 says exactly what goes wrong when the variance is infinite.
            

            Finally, floating point: M35's warning applies directly. A gradient computed by catastrophic cancellation is noise, and no optimiser recovers from that.
            In your real work
            Learning-rate selection is the $\frac2L$ story in practice. The "learning-rate range test" — sweep the rate upward until the loss diverges, then back off — is an empirical measurement of $\frac{2}{L}$, and warmup exists because $L$ is larger early in training than later.
            

            Feature normalisation is an optimisation intervention, not a data-cleaning one: scaling inputs to comparable ranges reduces $\kappa$ directly, and is often worth more than any change of optimiser. Batch normalisation and layer normalisation do the same thing inside the network.
            

            Batch size interacts with the step size through the noise ball. The "linear scaling rule" — double the batch, double the learning rate — follows from the variance argument, and it stops working once the step size approaches $\frac2L$, which is precisely why large-batch training needs warmup.
            

            Duality is what production solvers report. A MIP or LP solver's "gap" is exactly $\frac{\text{primal}-\text{dual}}{\text{primal}}$, and it lets you stop at 1% and know it is 1%. SVMs are solved in the dual because the dual is the problem with the kernel trick in it.
            

            And outside machine learning entirely, the same machinery is scheduling, routing, portfolio construction and resource allocation — all constrained convex problems where KKT multipliers have a direct reading as shadow prices: the multiplier on a constraint is exactly what one more unit of that resource is worth.
            Flash cards
            What makes convexity the dividing line in optimisation?
            For a convex function every stationary point is a global minimum, so going downhill cannot strand you in the wrong place. The one-line proof is the first-order condition with the gradient set to zero.
            What is the largest stable step for gradient descent, and why?
            2/L, where L is the largest Hessian eigenvalue. On a quadratic each eigendirection evolves as (1 - alpha*lambda)^k, and that exceeds 1 in magnitude exactly when alpha > 2/lambda.
            What determines the SPEED of gradient descent, as opposed to whether it converges?
            The condition number kappa = L/mu. With step 1/L the contraction is 1 - 1/kappa per step, so about kappa iterations are needed.
            What does momentum buy?
            The rate's dependence drops from kappa to sqrt(kappa). Nesterov's version is optimal among first-order methods.
            Why does Adam need bias correction, and which way does the error go?
            m and v both start at zero, so both are biased towards zero. The biases do not cancel: the net uncorrected step is sqrt(1-b2^t)/(1-b1^t) times the intended one, about 3.16 times TOO LARGE at t=1. With correction the first step has magnitude exactly alpha.
            Why does constant-step SGD not converge?
            Gradient noise keeps it in a ball of radius O(alpha * sigma^2) around the optimum. Convergence needs a decreasing step with sum alpha = infinity and sum alpha^2 finite.
            How does mini-batch size affect gradient noise?
            A batch of b cuts the variance by b and the standard deviation by sqrt(b) — diminishing returns, the same 1/sqrt(n) law as everywhere else.
            What is Newton's method's key advantage and its cost?
            It is affine invariant, so the condition number drops out of its rate, and it converges quadratically near the optimum. It costs O(d^3) per step and needs a positive definite Hessian.
            State the four KKT conditions.
            Stationarity (the Lagrangian's gradient vanishes), primal feasibility, dual feasibility (multipliers non-negative), and complementary slackness (lambda_i * g_i = 0).
            What does weak duality give you, and what does it assume?
            Every dual feasible point lower-bounds the primal optimum, so the gap is a computable certificate of suboptimality. It assumes NOTHING — not even convexity.
            What does backtracking line search replace?
            Knowledge of L. It halves the step until the Armijo sufficient-decrease condition holds, measuring instead of assuming.
            Stop when
            You can state the $\frac2L$ threshold and derive it from the quadratic iteration, you can explain why convergence and speed are governed by different eigenvalues, you can say what momentum changes about the rate and why it is $\sqrt\kappa$, you can write Adam from memory including both bias corrections and explain what breaks without them, you can list the four KKT conditions and say what complementary slackness means physically, you can compute a duality gap and explain why it is a proof, you can tell someone honestly what stops applying when the problem is non-convex, and you pass the boss exercise without hints.
