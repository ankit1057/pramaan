The story

A clever man comes to the king's court with a chessboard. "Your Majesty, I ask a small reward. One grain of rice on the first square. Two on the second. Four on the third. Keep doubling, square by square, to the sixty-fourth."

The king is delighted. Rice! Grains! He has elephants and palaces, and this man wants crumbs. He agrees at once.

The counting begins. The first row uses a spoonful. The second row, a bowl. By the end of the third row the servants bring a sack. By the fifth row they are bringing carts.

The king stops the counting and asks the court mathematician to finish it on paper. She works for a while and then says, very quietly, that the last square alone needs more rice than the kingdom has ever grown, and that the whole board needs more rice than exists in the world.

Here is the surprise, and it is not that the numbers got big. It is where they got big. Nothing alarming happens for half the board. The king makes his decision based on a spoonful, and the spoonful is honest — it really is what the first squares cost. Doubling hides its size until very suddenly it does not.

The same shape governs a cup of tea cooling, a loan compounding, and a list in your program quietly growing.

Say it simply

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers. A series is what you get when you add a sequence up.

Two patterns cover an enormous amount of ground. In an arithmetic progression you add a fixed step each time: 3, 7, 11, 15. In a geometric progression you multiply by a fixed ratio: 3, 6, 12, 24. Arithmetic growth is a straight line; geometric growth is the chessboard.

For both, there is a closed form — a formula that jumps straight to the n-th term or to the total without walking through every step. Finding such a formula is the whole game, because walking takes n steps and a formula takes one.

The one-line takeaway: a geometric series with ratio below 1 adds up to a finite total even with infinitely many terms, and that single fact explains everything from Zeno's paradox to why appending to a list is cheap.

Say it precisely

The Ladder of Numbers (Arithmetic)

Think of an arithmetic progression like climbing a ladder. You start at the very first rung, and every single step you take goes up by the exact same amount.

If you want to know your height on the tenth step, you do not need to climb them all. You just say, "I started at the bottom, and I took nine steps of the exact same size!"

If you wanted to add up all the heights of the rungs you stepped on, you can pair the shortest rung with the tallest rung, the second shortest with the second tallest, and so on. They all balance out perfectly! So the total is just the number of rungs multiplied by the average height of the first and last rung.

The Magic Cloning Machine (Geometric)

A geometric progression is like a magical cloning machine. You start with one magic bean, and every day the machine multiplies what you have by a fixed rate, like doubling it!

On the tenth day, your beans have multiplied nine times.

But what if the machine shrinks things instead of growing them? Like cutting a pizza in half, then taking half of that, and so on. If you keep doing this forever, you do not get an infinite amount of pizza. The pieces get so tiny that all together, they just make up a normal finite amount!

Telescoping

Imagine a pirate telescope that collapses into itself. A telescoping sum is a math puzzle where almost everything cancels out.

Imagine adding and subtracting like this: you get a whole pizza, give away half, get half back, give a third away... The middle disappears completely, leaving only the very first piece and the very last piece!

Melting and Reshaping (Averages)

Imagine you have some rectangular blocks, and you want to know their average size. - Arithmetic Mean is like melting them down and sharing the material equally. - Geometric Mean is like reshaping them to form a perfect square.

The rule says the Arithmetic Mean is always bigger than or equal to the Geometric Mean!

Why it's true

Why does adding infinite shrinking pieces make a finite shape? Imagine you have a single square piece of paper. You color half of it red. Then, you look at the blank half, and color half of that blue (which is a quarter of the whole paper). Then you color half of the leftover piece green. If you keep doing this forever, you will eventually color the entire piece of paper, but you will never, ever spill color off the edges! The pieces get so incredibly tiny that they perfectly fill up the one single square of paper. This shows that adding an infinite number of shrinking things can equal exactly one!

Why does the pirate telescope puzzle work? Imagine you are walking up and down stairs. You take two steps up, then one step down. Then you take three steps up, then two steps down. If you do this a hundred times, you don't need to add up every single tiny step. You can just look at where you started and where you finally stopped! In math, when we add a big list of numbers where every piece perfectly cancels out the piece right next to it, the whole messy middle completely vanishes. Only the very first starting step and the very last ending step are left. This is why we call it a collapsing telescope!

Oracle lab

The oracle checks every closed form in this module against the one thing that cannot be wrong about a sum: actually adding the terms up, one at a time, in a loop.

The closed forms jump to the answer in a single expression; the loops know nothing except how to accumulate. They share no helper. If a formula has an off-by-one, a wrong denominator or a misplaced factor, the loop will disagree at some n and the comparison will catch it. The mutant below divides by 3 instead of 6 for the sum of squares, n * (n + 1) * (2 * n + 1) / 3 — a plausible slip, since a 6 and a 3 are easy to confuse when you half-remember the formula.

Experiment lab
Two predictions, both measurable.


The alternating series pi / 4 = 1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - 1/7 + ... converges, but theory says it converges dreadfully slowly: for an alternating series whose terms shrink to zero, the error after n terms is smaller than the first omitted term, and here that is about 1 / (2 * n). So to gain one more correct decimal digit you need ten times as many terms. We predict the error after n terms is close to 1 / (4 * n) for the estimate of pi itself (since we multiply the series by 4, and the bound 1 / (2 * n) on pi / 4 becomes 2 / n on pi — the midpoint behaviour makes the true error about half the bound). We simply measure it.


Second, amortised doubling. Theory says n appends into a doubling array cost fewer than 2n element copies in total. We simulate the array and count every copy.

Where reality disagrees: the Madhava error does not fall on a smooth 1 / n curve; it oscillates, because an alternating series overshoots and undershoots the limit in turn. That is why the assertions above use a band rather than a point. The practical lesson is the one Madhava himself drew: this series is a beautiful proof that pi has a simple closed form, and a terrible way to actually compute it. He supplied correction terms for exactly that reason, and modern computation uses entirely different series that gain many digits per term.

            Misconceptions
              

            • "An infinite sum must be infinite." Only if the terms fail to shrink fast enough. 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + ... is infinitely many positive numbers adding to exactly 2. This is the resolution of Zeno's paradox.
              • 

            • "If the terms go to zero, the sum converges." False, and the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ... is the counterexample: its terms go to zero and it still diverges to infinity. Shrinking terms are necessary, not sufficient.
              • 

            • "0.999 repeating is slightly less than 1." It is a geometric series with a = 0.9 and r = 0.1, whose sum is exactly 0.9 / 0.9 = 1. They are two names for one number.
              • 

            • "Doubling an array is wasteful; grow it by a fixed chunk instead." Fixed chunks make the total copying quadratic, so each append costs order n time on average. The "waste" of doubling is exactly what buys constant amortised time.
              • 

            • "Amortised O(1) means every append is fast." It means the average over a sequence is constant. Individual appends that trigger a growth take linear time in n, which matters very much if you have a frame deadline.
              • 

            • "A shape with finite area has finite perimeter." The Koch snowflake has an area strictly below 8/5 of its starting triangle and a perimeter that grows by 4/3 each step forever, without bound.
              • 

            • "Madhava's series is a good way to compute pi." It is a beautiful theorem and a hopeless algorithm: the error falls like 1 / n, so a thousand terms buys about three decimal places.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            The closed forms assume the pattern continues forever and exactly. Real sequences — measurements, prices, response times — are only approximately arithmetic or geometric, and extrapolating a GP far beyond the data is how people predict absurdities.
            

            The infinite geometric sum needs |r| < 1 with no exceptions. At r = 1 the series is a + a + a + ...; at r = -1 it is a - a + a - ..., whose partial sums oscillate between a and 0 and never settle. Writing 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + ... = 1/2 by substituting into a / (1 - r) is the classic fallacy — it is a summation method, not the ordinary sum, and the formula is simply not applicable.
            

            In floating point, summing a long series accumulates rounding error, and adding from the largest term to the smallest is worse than the other way round, because the small terms vanish beneath the running total. The Madhava demo gets away with it because it only needs a few digits; serious summation uses Kahan's algorithm, which is in M35.
            

            The amortised argument assumes copying costs are proportional to the number of elements and that memory is always available. Neither holds unconditionally: reallocation may fail, and a large copy may blow a latency budget even if the average is fine.
            In your real work
            The amortised doubling result is the specification of List in Dart, ArrayList in Java, vector in C++ and list in Python, and the growth factors differ deliberately — roughly 1.5 to 2 — trading memory against copying. The latency caveat above is the practically important one: an append that triggers a reallocation of a large list is not free, and doing it during a frame is a real source of jank. List.filled or an explicit initial capacity exists precisely to avoid this. Geometric series also set exponential backoff for retries, where the sum tells you the total wait before giving up, and compound interest is the same formula with a different name.
            Flash cards
            Give the sum of an arithmetic progression and the one-line reason it is true.
            n/2 times (first + last). Writing the sum forwards and backwards and adding makes every column identical.
            When does an infinite geometric series converge, and to what?
            Exactly when the magnitude of the ratio is below 1, and then the sum is a/(1-r).
            The terms of a series tend to zero. Does it converge?
            Not necessarily. The harmonic series is the standard counterexample: its terms tend to zero and it diverges.
            Why is appending to a doubling array O(1) amortised?
            Because the total copies over n appends form a geometric series bounded by 2n, so the average per append is constant. Most of the cost is in the final doubling.
            What goes wrong if an array grows by a fixed number of slots instead of a fixed factor?
            The total copying becomes 1+2+3+... which is quadratic, so each append costs linear time on average.
            What is a telescoping sum?
            One where each term splits into pieces that cancel against neighbouring terms, leaving only the two ends.
            State AM-GM and its equality condition.
            The arithmetic mean of non-negative numbers is at least their geometric mean, with equality exactly when all the numbers are equal.
            What is remarkable about the Koch snowflake's perimeter and area?
            The perimeter grows by a factor of 4/3 each iteration and is unbounded, while the area converges to 8/5 of the starting triangle.
            Who found the series pi/4 = 1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - ... and what did he also understand about it?
            Madhava of Sangamagrama, around 1400, in the Kerala school. He also gave correction terms, showing he knew how slowly it converges.
