The story

In a busy seaside shipyard during the age of steamships, a flashy sales merchant arrives with a shiny brass steam gauge. He stands on a wooden crate before the harbormaster and shouts: "This extraordinary valve increases boiler fuel efficiency by forty percent, reduces pressure vibrations to zero, and has been thoroughly certified by top scholars in the capital!"

The harbormaster folds his arms and turns to his chief engineer, an unsmiling woman named Clara who carries a soot-stained leather notebook and a stopwatch. "Clara," asks the harbormaster, "is the merchant's claim true?"

Clara does not argue. She does not look at the merchant's embossed certificates, and she does not listen to his persuasive speeches. Instead, she walks over to the test boiler in dockyard three. She attaches the valve, shovels one hundred pounds of anthracite coal into the firebox, turns the valve, and checks the boiler's steam output against the water weight meter.

Forty-five minutes later, Clara returns with coal dust on her knuckles. "I bolted the valve to boiler three at two o'clock. At two-forty, the water output was three hundred gallons—the exact same output as our standard iron valve. The forty percent efficiency claim is unsupported. The merchant's claim is an unverified assertion. My measurement is a verified observation. Coal does not care about persuasive arguments."

Say it simply

Claims are cheap; evidence is expensive. In scientific engineering and automated agent auditing, we maintain a strict evidence hierarchy: real execution and observation outranks test runner output, test runner output outranks static source reading, and source reading outranks unverified assertions. Every empirical claim must be adjudicated as SUPPORTED, PARTIAL, FALSE, or UNDETERMINED. Commands run in pipelines must be audited using pipefail, and resume claims must be verified against reproducible measurement artifacts.

Say it precisely

Imagine you are a detective trying to solve a tricky mystery, and someone tells you, "I know exactly what happened!" As a smart detective, you know you cannot just believe them because they sound very confident. You need real, solid clues. Some clues are much better than others!

For example, a rumor you heard on the playground is a very weak clue. Finding a footprint is a stronger clue. But catching the person on video doing it is the strongest, absolute best clue! When checking if a claim is true, we must sort our clues from weakest to strongest. If someone says "This car is super fast!", we don't just look at the picture of the car (weak clue); we actually put the car on a race track and use a stopwatch to measure its speed ourselves (super strong clue). We call a claim "TRUE" only when the strongest possible clues prove it, and "FALSE" if our actual test proves it wrong.

Why it's true

Why do we need this strict rule of checking clues from weakest to strongest? Because both people and clever computers are really good at sounding smart, even when they are completely wrong! If someone speaks with a loud, confident voice, it is easy to accidentally believe them without checking the real facts. Without a strict rule to demand strong clues, a made-up guess is treated the exact same as a real scientific test.

In building big computer programs, this mistake causes giant disasters. For example, a robot might report "I finished the job!" just because it finished its last step, hiding the fact that it completely crashed and burned on the first step. Or someone might brag "I made the game run a hundred times faster!" when they only tested it on a completely empty screen. By demanding strong, physical proof—like actually running the code and measuring it with tools—we stop ourselves from being tricked by fancy words, fake claims, and sneaky mistakes. It separates the real builders from the storytellers!

    Write audit_performance_claim(baseline, observed, claimed_improvement_pct, metric_type='latency') to audit technical claims against empirical measurements:
Inputs:
- baseline: Positive float baseline metric value.
- observed: Positive float observed metric value.
- claimed_improvement_pct: Claimed percentage improvement as float (e.g. 40.0 for 40 percent improvement).
- metric_type: 'latency' (lower is better) or 'throughput' (higher is better).
    

    Rules:
1. True improvement percentage:
   - For 'latency': \text{actual_pct} = \frac{\text{baseline} - \text{observed}}{\text{baseline}} \times 100\ percent
   - For 'throughput': \text{actual_pct} = \frac{\text{observed} - \text{baseline}}{\text{baseline}} \times 100\ percent
2. Compute discrepancy \Delta = \text{claimed} - \text{actual_pct}.
3. Verdict:
   - 'ACCURATE': if |\Delta| \le 1.0\ percent
   - 'OVERSTATED': if \Delta > 1.0\ percent and \text{actual_pct} > 0
   - 'UNDERSTATED': if \Delta < -1.0\ percent and \text{actual_pct} > 0
   - 'FALSE_REGRESSION': if \text{actual_pct} \le 0 (the metric actually got worse or did not change!)
Return dictionary:
{'actual_pct': round(actual_pct, 2), 'discrepancy': round(discrepancy, 2), 'verdict': verdict}.
Raise ValueError if baseline <= 0 or observed <= 0 or invalid metric_type.

      Core
      Compute Brier score and confidence calibration curve
      
not tried
      

      Write brier_score_calibration(predictions, outcomes, num_bins=5):
Inputs:
- predictions: List of float subjective probabilities p_i \in [0.0, 1.0].
- outcomes: List of binary integers o_i \in {0, 1} indicating whether the claimed event actually occurred.
- num_bins: Number of equal-width probability bins over [0, 1].
      

      Requirements:
1. Validate inputs: predictions and outcomes must have identical non-zero length. All p_i \in [0, 1] and o_i \in {0, 1}. Raise ValueError on mismatch or invalid values.
2. Compute overall Brier score:
   B = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (p_i - o_i)^2
3. Partition predictions into num_bins bins (bin k covers [k/b, (k+1)/b], with right boundary inclusive for the last bin).
4. For each bin containing at least one prediction, compute:
   - mean_predicted: average p_i in bin
   - empirical_frequency: average o_i in bin
   - count: number of predictions in bin
Return dictionary:
{'brier_score': round(B, 4), 'bins': list_of_bin_dicts}.

    preds = [0.8, 0.8]
    outs = [1, 0] # Mean predicted 0.8, frequency 0.5
    # Brier: ((0.8 - 1)^2 + (0.8 - 0)^2) / 2 = (0.04 + 0.64) / 2 = 0.34
    res = brier_score_calibration(preds, outs, num_bins=5)
    assert res['brier_score'] == 0.34

        Boss
        Multi-tier claim auditor with evidence reconciliation
        
not tried
        

        Implement audit_claims_system(claims, evidence_records):
Inputs:
- claims: List of claim dicts: {'id': str, 'metric': str, 'claimed_val': float, 'direction': 'higher'|'lower'}.
- evidence_records: List of evidence dicts: {'claim_id': str, 'tier': 'assertion'|'source_reading'|'test_output'|'ran_observed', 'observed_val': float, 'exit_code': int}.
        

        Evidence Hierarchy:
1. Discard any evidence record where exit_code != 0 (unsuccessful execution cannot support a claim).
2. For each claim, find the highest tier available:
   - Rank: 'ran_observed' (4) > 'test_output' (3) > 'source_reading' (2) > 'assertion' (1).
3. Verdict determination:
   - If highest tier \le 2 (assertion or source_reading): return 'UNDETERMINED'.
   - If highest tier \ge 3 (test_output or ran_observed):
     - For 'higher': if observed >= claimed * 0.95, return 'SUPPORTED'; elif observed > 0, return 'PARTIAL'; else 'FALSE'.
     - For 'lower': if observed <= claimed * 1.05, return 'SUPPORTED'; elif observed < baseline (or within 2x claimed), return 'PARTIAL'; else 'FALSE'.
Return a dictionary:
{'claim_verdicts': {claim_id: verdict}, 'audited_count': len(claims), 'tier_counts': {tier: count}}.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "If a developer or paper claims a 5x speedup, we should assume good faith." Good faith does not prevent cognitive bias, selective benchmarking, or silent failures; only executable artifacts count.
            • 

          • "A passing CI pipe means all commands succeeded." Without pipefail, an upstream failure in cmd1 | cmd2 is masked by cmd2, giving a false-positive passing build.
            • 

          • "Reading the code is just as good as running the code." Static source inspection cannot verify dynamic cache behavior, memory contention, or compiler optimizations.
            • 

          • "Calibrated confidence means always being 99 percent confident." Calibration means matching stated confidence to empirical reality: when you say 70 percent, 7 out of 10 should be true.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          The evidence hierarchy breaks down when hardware environments differ fundamentally between execution and target deployment (e.g. benchmarking on an x86_64 cloud workstation with 128GB RAM vs running on an ARM cortex-A53 edge device with thermal throttling). In cross-platform mobile environments, evidence is valid only when gathered on the target hardware architecture under thermal equilibrium.
          In your real work
          In serious engineering review the zero-handwaving rule applies: every bug fix and optimisation must carry the exact terminal log, the exit code of the command rather than of a pipe, before-and-after p99 latency telemetry, and a reproduction script. A claim without executable evidence is rejected at review.
          Flash cards
          What is the evidence hierarchy in software engineering?
          Direct execution and observation > test suite runner output > static source code reading > unverified assertions.
          Why is set -o pipefail critical in CI scripts?
          It prevents succeeding pipe commands from masking the non-zero exit codes of upstream crashed processes.
          What is the difference between a SUPPORTED and an UNDETERMINED claim?
          SUPPORTED has direct empirical execution evidence matching within tolerance; UNDETERMINED lacks execution or test verification.
          What does Brier score measure?
          The mean squared error between subjective confidence probabilities and actual binary outcomes, quantifying calibration.
          What is the Straw Man fallacy in technical reviews?
          Refuting an exaggerated, distorted caricature of an alternative proposal rather than addressing its actual merits.
          What is a reproducibility package?
          A complete bundle containing source code, pinned dependency manifests, dataset seeds, and execution scripts enabling 1-click replication.
          Stop when
          You can audit technical claims against empirical baselines, explain why pipefail prevents silent CI masking, and calculate Brier calibration scores.
