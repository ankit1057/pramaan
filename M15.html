The story

The temple gopuram in Asha's town is very tall and absolutely nobody is allowed to climb it.

Her grandfather hands her a stick exactly one metre long and tells her to stand it upright in the sand. It casts a shadow. Asha measures the shadow: half a metre.

"Now measure the temple's shadow," he says.

She paces it out. Sixty metres.

"So how tall is the temple?"

Asha stares. Then she sees it. Her stick is one metre tall and throws a half-metre shadow, so a shadow is half as long as the thing that made it. That is not a fact about her stick. The sun is so far away that its rays arrive parallel, so the temple's shadow is made the same way. Sixty metres of shadow means one hundred and twenty metres of temple.

She never touched the temple. She measured a stick and some sand.

Here is the surprise: the ratio of height to shadow does not care how big the triangle is. A stick, a tree and a mountain standing in the same sunlight all share one number. That number depends on one thing only — the angle of the sun. Give the angle a name, write down its ratio once, and you can measure anything you can see the top of.

Say it simply

Trigonometry attaches numbers to angles. In a right-angled triangle, pick one of the non-right angles, call it $\theta$, and form three ratios of sides: sine (opposite over hypotenuse), cosine (adjacent over hypotenuse) and tangent (opposite over adjacent).

The whole subject rests on one observation from the story: these ratios depend only on $\theta$, not on the size of the triangle, because all right triangles with the same angle are similar.

That is enough to measure heights and distances you cannot reach. But the real payoff comes when you let $\theta$ run all the way round a circle. Sine and cosine stop being about triangles and become the mathematics of anything that repeats: a wave, a wheel, a vibrating string, a signal, an alternating current. Everything in the back half of this module is about that shift.

Say it precisely

Ratios in a right triangle

For an acute angle $\theta$ in a right triangle, with sides opposite, adjacent and hypotenuse:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}, \quad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$$

and their reciprocals $\csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}$, $\sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}$, $\cot\theta = \frac{1}{\tan\theta}$.

Standard values, derived below from two specific triangles:

$\theta$ $0°$ $30°$ $45°$ $60°$ $90°$
$\sin\theta$ $0$ $\frac12$ $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ $1$
$\cos\theta$ $1$ $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ $\frac12$ $0$
$\tan\theta$ $0$ $\frac{1}{\sqrt3}$ $1$ $\sqrt3$ undefined

Complementary angles. The two acute angles of a right triangle add to $90°$, and each one's opposite side is the other's adjacent side. So

$$\sin(90° - \theta) = \cos\theta, \quad \tan(90°-\theta) = \cot\theta, \quad \sec(90°-\theta) = \csc\theta$$

This is where the "co" in cosine, cotangent and cosecant comes from: they are the ratios of the complementary angle.

The Pythagorean identities

$$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1, \qquad 1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta, \qquad 1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta$$

Radians and the unit circle

One radian is the angle subtending an arc equal to the radius, so a full turn is $2\pi$ radians and

$$180° = \pi \text{ radians}, \qquad \theta_{\text{rad}} = \theta_{\deg} \times \frac{\pi}{180}$$

Radians are not a convenience: the derivative formulas $\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$ and the limit $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ hold only in radians.

On the unit circle, the point at angle $\theta$ measured anticlockwise from the positive $x$-axis is exactly $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. This extends both functions to every real angle, positive or negative, and the Pythagorean identity becomes the statement that the point lies on a circle of radius 1.

Domains, ranges and periods:

Function Domain Range Period
$\sin$, $\cos$ all reals $[-1, 1]$ $2\pi$
$\tan$, $\cot$ excluding where the denominator vanishes all reals $\pi$
$\sec$, $\csc$ excluding where the denominator vanishes $(-\infty,-1] \cup [1,\infty)$ $2\pi$

The addition formulas and everything that follows

$$\sin(A \pm B) = \sin A\cos B \pm \cos A \sin B$$ $$\cos(A \pm B) = \cos A \cos B \mp \sin A \sin B$$ $$\tan(A\pm B) = \frac{\tan A \pm \tan B}{1 \mp \tan A \tan B}$$

Note the flipped sign in the cosine formula: $\cos(A+B)$ has a minus.

Double angle (set $B = A$):

$$\sin 2A = 2\sin A\cos A, \quad \cos 2A = \cos^2 A - \sin^2 A = 1 - 2\sin^2 A = 2\cos^2 A - 1, \quad \tan 2A = \frac{2\tan A}{1-\tan^2 A}$$

Triple angle:

$$\sin 3A = 3\sin A - 4\sin^3 A, \quad \cos 3A = 4\cos^3 A - 3\cos A, \quad \tan 3A = \frac{3\tan A - \tan^3 A}{1 - 3\tan^2 A}$$

Product to sum, and sum to product:

$$2\sin A\cos B = \sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B), \qquad 2\cos A\cos B = \cos(A+B)+\cos(A-B)$$ $$\sin C + \sin D = 2\sin\frac{C+D}{2}\cos\frac{C-D}{2}, \qquad \cos C + \cos D = 2\cos\frac{C+D}{2}\cos\frac{C-D}{2}$$

Solving any triangle

For a triangle with sides $a, b, c$ opposite angles $A, B, C$, and circumradius $R$:

$$\textbf{Sine rule:}\quad \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R$$ $$\textbf{Cosine rule:}\quad c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$$

The cosine rule contains Pythagoras: when $C = 90°$, $\cos C = 0$ and it reduces to $c^2 = a^2+b^2$.

General solutions and inverse functions

$$\sin\theta = \sin\alpha \iff \theta = n\pi + (-1)^n \alpha, \qquad \cos\theta = \cos\alpha \iff \theta = 2n\pi \pm \alpha, \qquad \tan\theta = \tan\alpha \iff \theta = n\pi + \alpha$$

for every integer $n$. Because sine and cosine repeat, they are not injective and have no inverse until we restrict them. The principal value branches are

$$\sin^{-1}: [-1,1] \to \left[-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right], \qquad \cos^{-1}: [-1,1] \to [0, \pi], \qquad \tan^{-1}: \mathbb{R} \to \left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$$

and from these follow

$$\sin^{-1}x + \cos^{-1}x = \frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad \tan^{-1}x + \cot^{-1}x = \frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad \sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1}x, \qquad \cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}x$$

A pinch of history

Indian astronomers made the decisive move from chords to half-chords. Hellenistic astronomy, in Hipparchus and later Ptolemy's Almagest, tabulated the chord of an angle. The Surya Siddhanta and then Aryabhata, in the Aryabhatiya of 499 CE, tabulated the half-chord instead — which is exactly the modern sine — and Aryabhata gave a table of sine differences at intervals of $3°45'$. He called it jya (bowstring); transmitted into Arabic as jiba, written without vowels as jb, it was later misread as jaib, meaning "fold" or "bay", and translated into Latin as sinus. The English word sine is the fossil of that mistranslation.

Brahmagupta (628 CE) gave an interpolation formula for sine values. Bhaskara II (1150) stated the addition formulas for sine. Madhava and the Kerala school, around 1400, went further and obtained the infinite power series for sine and cosine, together with accurate sine tables — work that reappears in Europe only with Newton and his successors.

Why it's true

Result 1: the ratios depend only on the angle. Two right triangles sharing an acute angle $\theta$ also share their right angle, so their third angles agree too. They are therefore similar, and similar triangles have proportional sides. So opposite-over-hypotenuse is the same number in both. This is the content of Asha's stick, and everything else depends on it.

Result 2: the standard values, from two triangles.

  1. The $45°$ case. Take a square of side 1 and cut it along a diagonal. The two acute angles are equal and sum to $90°$, so each is $45°$. The legs are 1 and 1; Pythagoras gives the hypotenuse $\sqrt2$. Hence $\sin 45° = \cos 45° = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and $\tan 45° = 1$.
  2. The $30°$–$60°$ case. Take an equilateral triangle of side 2 and drop a perpendicular from one vertex. It bisects both the opposite side and the apex angle, giving a right triangle with hypotenuse 2, base 1 and apex angle $30°$. Pythagoras gives the height $\sqrt{4-1} = \sqrt3$. So $\sin 30° = \frac12$, $\cos 30° = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$, and swapping which angle you look at gives the $60°$ row.

Result 3: $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta = 1$. In a right triangle with hypotenuse $h$, opposite $o$ and adjacent $a$, we have $\sin^2+\cos^2 = \frac{o^2}{h^2}+\frac{a^2}{h^2} = \frac{o^2+a^2}{h^2}$, which Pythagoras makes $\frac{h^2}{h^2}=1$. It is Pythagoras divided by $h^2$ — nothing more. Dividing the identity through by $\cos^2\theta$ gives $1+\tan^2 = \sec^2$, and by $\sin^2\theta$ gives $1+\cot^2 = \csc^2$.

Result 4: the addition formula, by rotation. This is the cleanest proof and the one worth knowing.

  1. Rotating the plane anticlockwise by $\theta$ sends the point $(1,0)$ to $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and the point $(0,1)$ to $(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$.
  2. So rotation by $\theta$ is the linear map with matrix $R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$.
  3. The non-obvious step: rotating by $B$ and then by $A$ is the same as rotating once by $A+B$. Geometrically obvious, algebraically powerful — it says $R(A)R(B) = R(A+B)$.
  4. Multiply the two matrices. The top-left entry of $R(A)R(B)$ is $\cos A\cos B - \sin A \sin B$; the bottom-left is $\sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$.
  5. The corresponding entries of $R(A+B)$ are $\cos(A+B)$ and $\sin(A+B)$.
  6. Equate them. Both addition formulas drop out at once, including the minus sign in the cosine, which is now clearly not a typo but a consequence of the matrix.

Setting $B=A$ gives the double-angle formulas; applying the formula to $2A + A$ and simplifying with the Pythagorean identity gives the triple-angle formulas.

Result 5: the sine rule. Drop the altitude $h$ from vertex $C$ to side $AB$. In the two right triangles it creates, $h = b\sin A$ and $h = a\sin B$. Equating, $b \sin A = a \sin B$, so $\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B}$. Repeating with a different altitude gives the third ratio.

Result 6: the cosine rule. Place $C$ at the origin with side $a$ along the positive $x$-axis, so $B=(a,0)$ and $A = (b\cos C, b\sin C)$. Then

$$c^2 = (b\cos C - a)^2 + (b\sin C)^2 = b^2\cos^2 C - 2ab\cos C + a^2 + b^2\sin^2 C = a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C,$$

using $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$ at the last step.

Oracle lab

The oracle checks the identity machinery two genuinely independent ways.

The first route composes: given only $\sin A$, $\cos A$, $\sin B$ and $\cos B$, it builds $\sin(A+B)$, $\cos(A+B)$, the double-angle and the triple-angle values purely algebraically, never touching the combined angle.

The second route evaluates: it forms the combined angle $A+B$ (or $2A$, or $3A$) as a number and asks the library for its sine and cosine directly.

One path is algebra on four numbers; the other is a power-series evaluation of a different argument. They have no step in common. If any formula carried a wrong sign or a wrong coefficient, the two would part company. The mutant below writes $\cos(A+B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B$ — the single most common sign error in all of trigonometry, and the one the matrix proof exists to prevent.

Experiment lab
Two predictions to measure against reality.


First, the sine and cosine rules. They claim to hold for every triangle, not just right-angled ones. So we build random triangles from three random points, measure the sides with the distance formula and the angles with the dot product, and then check both rules — including that all three sine ratios equal the same $2R$.


Second, the small-angle approximation. Theory says $\sin x \approx x$ for small $x$ in radians, with an error given by the next term of the Taylor series, $\frac{x^3}{6}$. That predicts the error shrinks by a factor of 1000 whenever $x$ shrinks by 10. We measure it.

Where reality disagrees: the triangle demo discards triangles with a very short side, and that is not laziness. A nearly degenerate triangle has an angle close to $0$ or $\pi$ where $\arccos$ is extremely sensitive — a rounding error of $10^{-16}$ in the cosine becomes an error of around $10^{-8}$ in the angle, because the derivative of $\arccos$ blows up at the ends. The mathematics is exact; the measurement of an angle from a nearly-flat triangle is ill-conditioned. This is why atan2 is preferred to acos in real geometry code, and it is the same conditioning theme as M12 and M13.

            Misconceptions
              

            • "$\sin^{-1}x$ means $\frac{1}{\sin x}$." It means the inverse function, arcsine. The reciprocal is $\csc x$. The notation is genuinely bad and the ambiguity is why $\arcsin$ is preferred in code.
              • 

            • "Sine and cosine are about triangles." They start there, but on the unit circle they are defined for every real angle, including obtuse, negative and enormous ones, where no triangle exists. That extension is the whole reason they describe waves.
              • 

            • "$\cos(A+B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B$." That is $\cos(A-B)$. The sum formula has a minus. If you can reconstruct the rotation matrix you never have to remember which.
              • 

            • "It does not matter whether I work in degrees or radians." It matters enormously. $\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$ and $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1$ are false in degrees, and every library function takes radians.
              • 

            • "The sine rule always solves a triangle." Given two sides and a non-included angle it can be ambiguous — the SSA case — because $\arcsin$ returns an acute angle and the obtuse supplement may be equally valid. The cosine rule has no such ambiguity, which is why the boss exercise uses it twice.
              • 

            • "$\sin^2\theta$ is a new function." It is shorthand for $(\sin\theta)^2$. But $\sin^{-1}$ breaks this pattern completely, which is exactly the trap in the first bullet.
              • 

            • "The small-angle approximation is a rough rule of thumb." It is an exact Taylor statement with a known error of about $\frac{x^3}{6}$, which at $x = 0.01$ means about $1.7\times10^{-7}$ — far better than most measurements.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            The right-triangle definitions only cover angles strictly between $0$ and $90°$. Tangent is undefined at $90°$ and its neighbours, and near there it is numerically violent: a tiny change in the angle produces an enormous change in the tangent, so computing a height from an elevation close to vertical is hopeless in practice even though the formula is fine on paper.
            

            Recovering an angle from a cosine is ill-conditioned near $0$ and $\pi$, as the experiment showed, because $\arccos$ has an infinite derivative at $\pm1$. Production geometry code uses atan2(y, x), which takes the two coordinates separately, gets the quadrant right, and stays well conditioned everywhere.
            

            Trigonometric functions of very large arguments are also unreliable: computing $\sin(10^{20})$ requires knowing $10^{20}$ modulo $2\pi$, and the input itself does not carry enough bits to pin that down. The answer a library returns is the correct sine of the number you gave it, which may be far from the number you meant.
            

            Finally, all of this is plane trigonometry. On a sphere the angles of a triangle sum to more than $\pi$, the excess being proportional to the area, and you need spherical trigonometry — which is where the subject came from, since Aryabhata and Ptolemy were doing astronomy, not surveying.
            In your real work
            Rotation, the proof technique in Result 4, is the implementation: a 2-D rotation matrix is how any UI framework rotates a view, and Transform.rotate is that matrix. Sine and cosine drive every oscillating animation, and an easing curve is usually a scaled piece of one. The unit circle is how a circular progress indicator maps a fraction to a point. atan2 is what turns a drag vector into an angle for a rotating control, and the reason to prefer it over acos is the conditioning argument above, not style. Further out, the addition formula is the algebraic heart of the FFT in M39, where it becomes multiplication of complex numbers on the unit circle.
            Flash cards
            Why do the trigonometric ratios depend only on the angle and not the triangle's size?
            Because two right triangles sharing an acute angle are similar, and similar triangles have proportional sides.
            Derive sin 30 and cos 30 from a picture.
            Drop a perpendicular in an equilateral triangle of side 2. It gives a right triangle with hypotenuse 2, base 1 and height root 3, so sin 30 = 1/2 and cos 30 = root 3 over 2.
            Why is sin^2 + cos^2 = 1 not a separate fact to memorise?
            It is Pythagoras for the triangle, divided through by the square of the hypotenuse.
            State the addition formulas and the trick for getting the signs right.
            sin(A+B) = sinA cosB + cosA sinB and cos(A+B) = cosA cosB - sinA sinB. They are the entries of the product of two rotation matrices, which fixes the minus sign.
            What is a radian, and why not just use degrees?
            The angle subtending an arc equal to the radius. Radians are required for the derivative of sine to be cosine and for sin(x)/x to tend to 1.
            Give the principal-value ranges of arcsin, arccos and arctan.
            [-pi/2, pi/2], [0, pi], and the open interval (-pi/2, pi/2).
            Why does arcsin(x) + arccos(x) always equal pi/2?
            Because the two angles are complementary: if sin A = x then cos(pi/2 - A) = x, and both results lie in their principal branches.
            When is the sine rule ambiguous, and what do you use instead?
            In the SSA case, where arcsin cannot distinguish an angle from its supplement. Use the cosine rule, which has a unique solution.
            How accurate is sin x = x, and how does the error scale?
            The error is about x^3/6, so dividing the angle by 10 divides the error by 1000.
            Why is atan2 preferred to acos in geometry code?
            acos has an infinite derivative at plus or minus 1, so it is ill-conditioned for angles near 0 and pi, while atan2 is well conditioned and resolves the quadrant.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child how to measure a temple with a stick, you can derive the standard values from a square and an equilateral triangle rather than recall a table, you can prove the addition formulas from the rotation matrix and get the signs right every time, you can solve any triangle from SSS or SAS and say why you reach for the cosine rule rather than the sine rule, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can explain why atan2 exists.
