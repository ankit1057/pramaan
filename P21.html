The story

In 1967, at the AFIPS Spring Joint Computer Conference, computer architect Gene Amdahl presented a sobering mathematical argument that shocked the high-performance computing community. Amdahl demonstrated that no matter how many parallel processors you throw at a computing task, the speedup is fundamentally strictly limited by the fraction of the algorithm that must run serially. If just five percent of an image processing pipeline or database query is inherently sequential, the absolute maximum theoretical speedup on an infinite number of parallel processor cores is a mere twenty times. For twenty-one years, "Amdahl's law" was viewed as a hard barrier to massively parallel supercomputing.

That barrier was dramatically shattered in 1988 by John Gustafson and his research team at Sandia National Laboratories. Gustafson demonstrated a 1,024-fold speedup on a 1,024-processor nCUBE supercomputer by demonstrating that in real-world science and engineering, people do not run a fixed-size problem faster on larger supercomputers; instead, they scale the problem size to utilize the expanded hardware memory and computing power. This insight, formalized as Gustafson's law (scaled speedup), catalyzed the modern high-performance computing revolution. In 1990, Guy Blelloch published Vector Models for Data-Parallel Computing, establishing work-efficient parallel prefix sums (scans) as foundational primitives. In the 2000s, CPU clock frequency scaling hit the thermal "power wall," forcing hardware manufacturers to transition from faster single cores to multicore processors with SIMD vector extensions, while NVIDIA pioneered the Single Instruction, Multiple Threads (SIMT) GPU architecture. Today, parallel computing powers everything from mobile Flutter rasterizers rendering 120fps UI frames across multi-core ARM SoCs to massive clusters training trillion-parameter transformer foundation models.

Say it simply

Parallel computing is the practice of splitting a large computational problem into smaller independent subproblems that execute simultaneously across multiple processing units, such as CPU cores, SIMD vector registers, or thousands of GPU threads. Rather than executing instructions one after another in serial order, parallel systems exploit data parallelism (applying the exact same operation to millions of array elements at once) or task parallelism (executing different functions concurrently). Fundamental physical and mathematical laws govern parallel speedup: Amdahl's law demonstrates that sequential bottlenecks limit speedup for fixed-size problems, while Gustafson's law shows that scaling problem size unlocks linear speedup. At the hardware level, performance is often constrained not by raw arithmetic capability, but by memory bandwidth—the rate at which data can be transported from RAM to processing units.

Say it precisely

Imagine you have a huge pile of toys to clean up. If you clean them by yourself, it takes an hour. If you invite 10 friends, it goes much faster! But there are rules to how much your friends can help.

  1. The Toaster Rule (Amdahl's Law): Suppose you are making a sandwich. Your friends can help you grab chips and pour juice all at the exact same time! But you still have to wait for the bread to toast. Only one piece of bread fits in the toaster. It doesn't matter if you have a million friends waiting in the kitchen—you still have to wait 2 minutes for the toaster! This rule says that the "waiting" part puts a strict speed limit on how fast you can finish.

  2. The Giant Party Rule (Gustafson's Law): But wait! If you invite a million friends over, you don't just make one sandwich. You buy a giant factory with a million toasters and make a million sandwiches at the same time! This rule says that if you get more helpers, you don't just do a small chore faster—you do a gigantic chore instead!

  3. Chores and Waiting (Work and Span): - Work: This is the total time it would take ONE kid to do every single chore in the house, one by one. - Span: Some chores have to wait for others. You can't put on your shoes before your socks! Even if you have 100 helpers, you still have to wait for the socks to go on before the shoes. "Span" is the longest chain of chores where you are forced to wait.

  4. The Buddy System (Parallel Scan): Imagine 8 kids need to add up all their candy. If they pass a bucket down the line, it takes a long time. Instead, they use the buddy system! Kid 1 and 2 add theirs, while Kid 3 and 4 add theirs at the exact same time. Then the pairs add theirs together. By teaming up, everyone gets the answer much faster!

  5. The Cookie Delivery Limit (Roofline Model): Imagine you can eat 100 cookies a minute. You are a super-fast eater! But your mom is in the kitchen, and she only brings you 1 cookie every hour. It doesn't matter how fast your mouth is; you are limited by how fast the cookies are carried to the table! Computers are exactly the same: they are often limited by how fast they can move data, not how fast they can do math.

Why it's true

For a long time, computers were just getting faster and faster, like a kid who learns to run faster every year. But eventually, the computer got so hot it would melt if it ran any faster! So instead of making one super-fast runner, computer scientists started putting hundreds of normal-speed runners inside the computer to work together.

But making runners work together is super hard! If 10 kids try to paint the same wall at the same time, they bump heads, spill paint, and make a huge mess. Sometimes, having 10 kids paint a wall is actually slower than just having one kid do it quietly!

We need to understand these rules so we know exactly when to use helpers (like checking millions of pixels in a video game at the same time) and when it's better to just do things one by one (like putting on socks and shoes). When you get the buddy system just right, your computer can paint beautiful 3D video games or learn from giant books in the blink of an eye!

          Misconceptions
            

          • "Adding twice as many CPU cores will always make a parallel program run twice as fast." Amdahl's law proves that speedup is bounded by the sequential fraction of the program. If only 10% of a program cannot be parallelized, the maximum possible speedup on an infinite number of cores is 10x, and doubling cores yields diminishing returns.
            • 

          • "Thread pools should always create as many threads as possible to maximize throughput." Spawning more worker threads than physical CPU hardware cores leads to CPU thrashing, excessive context switching overhead, and cache invalidation. Optimal compute-bound thread pool sizing is typically equal to the number of physical CPU cores.
            • 

          • "GPU SIMT architecture executes each thread completely independently just like CPU cores." In modern GPUs, threads execute in lockstep groups called warps (typically 32 threads). If threads within the same warp take different branches of an if-else statement (branch divergence), both branches are executed serially with threads masked out, destroying parallel efficiency.
            • 

          • "A computation with linear work and linear span is a great candidate for parallelization." Parallelism is defined as total work divided by the critical-path span. If the critical-path span equals total work, every single step must wait for the step before it, meaning zero concurrency or speedup can be achieved regardless of how many processor cores are available.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Parallel computing principles fail or encounter severe performance penalties in specific systems scenarios:
1. Memory-Bound Kernels and the Roofline Ceiling: In simple streaming operations like vector addition (scaling and adding two arrays), each float requires reading 16 bytes and writing 8 bytes for only 2 math operations (an extremely low arithmetic intensity of less than 0.1 operations per byte). The kernel hits the memory bandwidth wall immediately, leaving 95% of GPU compute cores idle.
2. Fine-Grained Task Overhead in Work-Stealing Pools: If the cost of spawning a task or stealing it from a worker queue (roughly one microsecond) exceeds the execution time of the task itself (e.g. adding two numbers), parallel scheduling overhead dwarfs execution time, making parallel code orders of magnitude slower than a simple sequential loop.
3. Cache False Sharing: When two threads running on different CPU cores concurrently modify independent variables that happen to reside on the same 64-byte L1 CPU cache line, hardware cache coherency protocols (MESI) constantly invalidate the cache lines across cores, crippling parallel throughput.
          In your real work
          Parallel computing techniques are applied across modern high-performance engineering:
- Flutter and Skia/Impeller Graphics Engines: Render pipelines partition scene graphs into independent display lists, evaluating geometry and dispatching tessellation kernels concurrently across multi-core ARM big.LITTLE CPUs before rasterizing on mobile GPUs.
- Deep Learning Training Frameworks (PyTorch/JAX): Data-parallel and pipeline-parallel distributed training divides batches across thousands of GPUs, relying on Blelloch-style all-reduce tree algorithms implemented in NVIDIA NCCL to synchronize gradients.
- Game Engine Physics and Particle Systems: Modern game engines (Unreal, Unity DOTS) use SIMD vectorization and lock-free work-stealing job systems to simulate millions of particles and ragdoll physics across all available CPU cores.
          Flash cards
          What does Amdahl's Law state?
          The maximum speedup of a program on parallel processors is strictly bounded by the reciprocal of its sequential execution fraction: S(P) <= 1/s as P -> infinity.
          What is the key difference between Amdahl's Law and Gustafson's Law?
          Amdahl assumes a fixed problem size (speedup asymptotes to 1/s), while Gustafson assumes problem size scales with processor count (speedup scales linearly: s + P(1-s)).
          What is the difference between Work and Span?
          Work (T1) is the total sequential execution time of all operations. Span (T_inf) is the critical path length of the computation DAG. Maximum parallelism is T1 / T_inf.
          What is the difference between SIMD and SIMT?
          SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data) uses wide CPU vector registers (AVX/NEON) to execute one opcode over vector lanes; SIMT (Single Instruction, Multiple Threads) runs lockstep warps on GPUs where independent thread branches can cause divergence.
          How does Blelloch's parallel prefix scan achieve work-efficiency?
          By executing in two tree phases: an up-sweep reduction tree that computes partial sums, and a down-sweep distribution tree, achieving O(n) work and O(log n) span.
          What is the Roofline Model in computer architecture?
          A performance model showing whether an algorithm's throughput is bounded by peak compute floating-point operations (GFLOPS) or by memory bandwidth (GB/s * operational intensity).
          Stop when
          You can calculate Amdahl and Gustafson speedup limits, analyze computation DAG work and span with Brent's theorem, implement Blelloch work-efficient parallel prefix scans, and simulate multi-worker work-stealing thread schedulers.
