The story

Rohan's grandfather draws a treasure map on the back of an envelope.

"From the well, walk three paces east. Then turn a quarter-turn to the left and walk four paces. Dig."

Rohan walks it and finds a tin of coins. Then he gets interested in the turning. He walks three east, turns a quarter-turn — now he is facing north. Another quarter-turn, facing west. Another, south. One more and he is facing east again. Four quarter-turns and he is back where he started.

His grandfather asks him something strange. "What number would you have to multiply by, to make a quarter-turn?"

Rohan knows that multiplying by -1 flips you right round — a half-turn. East becomes west. So a quarter-turn done twice should be a half-turn. Whatever the quarter-turn number is, multiplying by it twice must give -1.

He thinks. No ordinary number does that. Two positives give a positive. Two negatives also give a positive. There is nowhere for -1 to come from.

"Then the number is not on your line," says his grandfather. "Your line only goes east and west. Make it a page."

Here is the surprise: the number that squares to -1 was never a fantasy. It is the quarter-turn, and the only reason it looked impossible is that Rohan was insisting on a one-dimensional world. Give numbers a second dimension and the impossible thing is just the instruction "turn left".

Say it simply

We define one new number, i, with the single property i squared is minus 1. Everything else follows from ordinary algebra.

A complex number z = a + bi is then a pair of real numbers: a across and b up. Plotted that way, on the Argand plane, complex numbers are points, or arrows from the origin.

And now the payoff. Adding complex numbers slides arrows. Multiplying them rotates and scales: multiply the lengths, add the angles. So multiplying by i, which has length 1 and angle 90°, is exactly Rohan's quarter-turn.

The one-line takeaway: complex numbers are not a patch for broken quadratics. They are the algebra of rotation, which is why they turn up wherever something spins, oscillates or repeats — and why the Fourier transform is built from them.

Say it precisely

A complex number just combines walking and turning: walk some steps forward, turn left, and walk some steps sideways. The first part is your normal forward or backward steps. The second part is your sideways steps. Adding them is easy. If you do one walk and then another walk, you just add all your forward steps together, and all your sideways steps together.

Multiplying is a bit like mixing them together, and every time you see two left turns, it makes you face backwards! So a double sideways step becomes a backward step.

The conjugate is like your mirror reflection: you take the same forward steps, but you step sideways to the right instead of the left. The modulus is just the direct distance from the start to where you ended up, like measuring a shortcut with a straight tape measure. If you multiply a number by its mirror reflection, you get a pure forward number. We use this neat trick to do division.

Just like a spinning top, sideways powers keep repeating every four turns: One turn left, face backwards, face right, and back to the start! Instead of walking and turning, we can think like a pirate drawing a treasure map. To get to a spot, turn by a specific angle and walk straight out for a distance.

There is a magical math shortcut called Euler's formula. It lets us write our pirate instructions super simply. Now, multiplying two complex numbers is beautifully simple: just multiply the distances, and add the angles together. De Moivre's theorem says if you want to multiply a number by itself many times, just multiply the angle that many times.

Why it's true

Result 1: Multiplication. Let us write two numbers using our pirate map instructions. If we just multiply them the long way with algebra, we get a messy mix of cosines and sines. The magical step is that this messy mix turns out to be the exact same formula for adding two angles from regular geometry! So the math proves what our eyes see: multiplying them is exactly the same as adding their angles together! This shows that multiplying complex numbers is just rotating and stretching.

Result 2: De Moivre's theorem. This follows a domino effect. The first domino is just the number itself. For the next dominos, if it works for multiplying some amount of times, what happens if we multiply one more time? By the first result, we just add the angles again. So multiplying one more time just means adding the angle one more time. The dominos all fall down perfectly, proving it works for any number of times.

Result 3: Roots of unity. Think of the roots as a team of kids standing in a perfect circle pulling on a rope in the center. Because they are equally spaced, the pull from one side is perfectly balanced by the pulls from the other sides. The center of mass of perfectly spaced points on a circle is exactly the middle, which is zero. The rope does not move.

Result 4: Square roots. Imagine a rule that says if you put on your left shoe and then your right shoe, it is the same as putting them on together. This rule works great for shoes. But if you try to use it for putting on your socks and then your shoes, it breaks! The rule for combining square roots was built only for positive numbers. If you try to force it onto negative numbers, it breaks the universe. The mistake is just using a rule outside of the sandbox where it was allowed to play.

Oracle lab

The oracle multiplies complex numbers two genuinely independent ways.

The first works in rectangular form: expand (a plus bi) times (c plus di) with the real arithmetic rule (ac-bd) + (ad+bc)i. It never computes an angle.

The second works in polar form: convert both numbers to modulus and argument, multiply the moduli, add the arguments, and convert back. It never touches the rectangular rule.

If either the sign in ac minus bd or the claim that arguments add were wrong, the two would disagree immediately. The demo also checks De Moivre against repeated multiplication, and Euler's formula against the power series for e to the power x, cosine of x and sine of x — a third independent route. The mutant uses ac plus bd in the real part, forgetting that i squared is minus 1.

Experiment lab
Theory makes several sharp, checkable claims about the n-th roots of unity: there are exactly n of them, they all sit on the unit circle, consecutive ones are separated by exactly \frac{2\pi}{n} in angle, and for n \ge 2 they sum to exactly zero. We compute them and measure all four.


Then the bridge to Fourier: we build the discrete Fourier transform purely out of roots of unity, run it on a signal that is a sum of two known cosines, and predict which frequency bins will light up. If the claim that "a complex exponential carries an oscillation" is right, the transform will find the frequencies we put in, and nothing else.

Where reality disagrees: notice the factor of two in the Fourier half. A real cosine of amplitude 3 produces a magnitude of 1.5, not 3, because a real cosine is the sum of two complex exponentials rotating in opposite directions — \cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2} — so its energy is split between bin k and bin N-k. People meeting a DFT for the first time almost always expect the amplitude back and are confused by the half. It is not an artefact; it is what "a real signal has a symmetric spectrum" means. The demo also chose frequencies that fit a whole number of cycles into the window. When they do not, the energy smears across neighbouring bins — spectral leakage — and that is M39's problem, not this module's.

def test_conjugate_and_modulus_identities():
    import random
    rng = random.Random(1806)
    for _ in range(300):
        z = (rng.uniform(-10, 10), rng.uniform(-10, 10))
        w = (rng.uniform(-10, 10), rng.uniform(-10, 10))
        # z * conj(z) is the squared modulus, and is real
        prod = mul(z, conjugate(z))
        assert abs(prod[1]) < 1e-9
        assert abs(prod[0] - modulus(z) ** 2) < 1e-9
        # modulus is multiplicative
        assert abs(modulus(mul(z, w)) - modulus(z) * modulus(w)) < 1e-9
        # triangle inequality
        assert modulus(add(z, w)) <= modulus(z) + modulus(w) + 1e-9

            Misconceptions
              

            • "Imaginary numbers are not real, so they describe nothing." The name is Descartes' insult and it has done lasting damage. They describe rotation, and they are as concrete as a quarter-turn. They were invented not for quadratics but to get real answers out of cubics.
              • 

            • "square root of negative one times square root of negative one equals one." The identity \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}=\sqrt{ab} holds for non-negative reals only. Square roots are two-valued over the complex numbers and no branch choice preserves the rule.
              • 

            • "The imaginary part of 3 plus 4i is 4i." It is 4. The imaginary part is a real number, which is why the imaginary part of z can be compared and plotted.
              • 

            • "the argument of z is arctan of b over a." That loses the quadrant: 1 plus i and minus 1 minus i give the same ratio and opposite arguments. Use atan2(b, a).
              • 

            • "Complex numbers can be ordered like reals." There is no ordering compatible with the arithmetic. Asking whether i is greater than 0 is meaningless; only moduli compare.
              • 

            • "Every complex number has one n-th root." It has exactly n, equally spaced in angle. A library returning only the principal one is making a choice, not stating a fact.
              • 

            • "A complex exponential is an exotic object." e to the power i theta is just the point at angle theta on the unit circle. Nothing more.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            The complex numbers are algebraically closed, which is their great virtue — every polynomial factors completely — but they pay for it by losing the order. You cannot sort them, there is no notion of positive, and inequalities simply do not transfer. Anything built on ordering, such as the Intermediate Value Theorem, has no complex analogue.
            

            Multi-valued functions are the recurring hazard. log of z and the nth root of z and the argument of z are all genuinely multi-valued, and software picks a principal branch with a cut, usually along the negative real axis. Crossing that cut makes the result jump discontinuously, so identities such as log of zw equals log of z plus log of w fail by a multiple of two pi i. This is a real source of bugs in signal and control code.
            

            Numerically, the modulus the distance formula overflows when a or b is large even though the answer is representable; good libraries use a scaled formula, which is why hypot exists. And the rectangular square-root formula in the boss exercise loses precision when a is large and negative, because the distance plus a is a subtraction of nearly equal numbers — the catastrophic cancellation of M13, met again.
            

            Finally, extending further has a cost. The quaternions give up commutativity, the octonions give up associativity, and a theorem of Hurwitz says there is nothing else: the reals, the complex numbers, the quaternions and the octonions are the only normed division algebras over the reals.
            In your real work
            Rotation is the direct application: a 2-D rotation is multiplication by e to the power i theta, and that is literally how you rotate a point, a view or a gesture vector. Quaternions, the 3-D cousin, are what every 3-D engine uses for orientation precisely because they avoid gimbal lock.
            

            Everything periodic is complex arithmetic underneath. Audio, image compression and radio all run through the FFT, which is roots of unity plus divide-and-conquer. An IIR or FIR filter's behaviour is read off the positions of its poles and zeros in the complex plane, and "the filter is stable" means "all poles inside the unit circle" — a statement about moduli. Control systems say the same thing with different words. When M39 arrives, none of it will be new; it will be this module applied.
            Flash cards
            What is the geometric meaning of multiplying by a complex number?
            Scale by its modulus and rotate by its argument. Moduli multiply and arguments add.
            Why is the real part of (a+bi)(c+di) equal to ac - bd?
            Expanding gives a bd term multiplied by i squared, which is -1, so it subtracts.
            Why does the minus sign in complex multiplication match the minus sign in cos(A+B)?
            Because they are the same fact: complex multiplication is the rotation matrix, and the addition formulas are its entries.
            State Euler's formula and what it says geometrically.
            e^(i theta) = cos theta + i sin theta: the point at angle theta on the unit circle.
            How many n-th roots does a non-zero complex number have, and how are they arranged?
            Exactly n, equally spaced by 2*pi/n in angle, all with the same modulus.
            Why do the n-th roots of unity sum to zero for n at least 2?
            They are a geometric progression with ratio omega, and the GP sum is (omega^n - 1)/(omega - 1), whose numerator vanishes.
            Why must you use atan2 rather than atan(b/a) for the argument?
            Because atan loses the quadrant: two numbers in opposite quadrants give the same ratio b/a.
            Why is sqrt(a)*sqrt(b) = sqrt(ab) not valid for complex numbers?
            The square root is two-valued, and no choice of branch preserves the identity everywhere. It is proved only for non-negative reals.
            What is the connection between complex numbers and the Fourier transform?
            The DFT multiplies the data by powers of a root of unity, so one complex exponential carries an oscillation's amplitude and phase together.
            A real cosine of amplitude 3 is transformed. Why does the bin show 1.5?
            Because a real cosine is the sum of two counter-rotating complex exponentials, so its energy splits between bins k and N-k.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why the quarter-turn number cannot live on a line, you can derive the multiplication rule and see the addition formulas inside it, you can convert fluently between rectangular and polar form and say why atan2 is required, you can find all n roots of any complex number and explain why the sum of the roots of unity is zero, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can say in one sentence why the Fourier transform is built from roots of unity.
