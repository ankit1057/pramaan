The story

A doctor tells Kavita that her test came back positive. The disease is rare — about one person in a hundred has it. The test is good: it catches ninety-nine out of a hundred people who have the disease, and it wrongly flags only five healthy people in a hundred.

Kavita is terrified. The test is ninety-nine per cent accurate and it says she has it.

Her uncle, who sells insurance and thinks in numbers all day, takes a sheet of paper and does something very simple. He does not argue. He just imagines ten thousand people.

Of the ten thousand, a hundred have the disease and nine thousand nine hundred do not.

Of the hundred who have it, the test catches ninety-nine.

Of the nine thousand nine hundred who do not, the test wrongly flags five per cent — four hundred and ninety-five people.

So the people holding a positive result are ninety-nine who are ill and four hundred and ninety-five who are not. Five hundred and ninety-four envelopes, and only ninety-nine of them contain bad news.

Kavita's chance of actually having the disease is ninety-nine out of five hundred and ninety-four. About one in six.

Here is the surprise: nothing about the test was bad. The test really is ninety-nine per cent sensitive. What Kavita forgot — what almost everybody forgets — is that there are so many more healthy people that even a small error rate among them produces more false alarms than the disease produces true ones. The question "how good is the test" and the question "what does my result mean" have completely different answers, and the gap between them is this module.

Say it simply

Conditional probability asks: given that B happened, how likely is A? Written P(A|B), it narrows the world to the cases where B is true and asks what fraction of those also have A.

The crucial point is that P(A|B) and P(B|A) are different numbers. "The test is positive given you are ill" is 0.99. "You are ill given the test is positive" is 0.17. Confusing the two is the single most consequential error in applied probability, and it has a name: the base rate fallacy.

Bayes' theorem is the machinery for turning one into the other. It is just the multiplication rule rearranged, but it is the formal version of the uncle's ten thousand people.

A random variable then attaches a number to each outcome, so you can talk about its average (expectation) and its spread (variance) — and the descriptive statistics of M09 reappear, now as properties of a model rather than of data.

Say it precisely

Think of conditional probability like zooming in on a map. If you want to find a specific toy store (Event A) in your city, the chances might seem small if you look at the whole world. But if someone tells you the store is definitely in your neighborhood (Event B), you zoom your map into just your neighborhood. Now, the chance of finding the store is much higher because you are only looking inside a smaller area. You ignore everything outside your neighborhood.

Bayes' theorem is like playing a detective game where you change your mind when you find new clues. Imagine you think your dog ate your missing sandwich (your initial guess or prior). Then you find a clue: breadcrumbs leading to the dog's bed. The theorem is just a logical way to update how sure you are based on that new clue. If cats never eat sandwiches and dogs always do, the crumb clue makes you very sure it was the dog!

A binomial setup is like flipping a trick coin many times. You count how many times it lands on heads. The rule needs three things: you must know exactly how many flips you will make, the coin must not change its weight between flips, and one flip's result cannot affect the next flip. If you flip it ten times, the expected number of heads is just ten times the chance of getting a head on a single flip.

Why it's true

The zooming-in rule for conditional probability makes perfect sense if you think about sharing a pizza. If you have a giant pizza and only a tiny slice has pepperoni, your chance of picking a pepperoni slice blindfolded is low. But if someone hands you a specific smaller box and says "your slice is in here," and that box is mostly pepperoni, your chances just went up. The smaller box is your new world.

When we say two things are independent, it means knowing about one tells you absolutely nothing about the other. If I tell you I had eggs for breakfast, it does not help you guess if it will rain tomorrow. Their chances do not mix. If they do not mix, you can easily calculate the chance of both happening by just combining their individual chances directly.

Bayes' theorem works because it balances two important things: how common something is to begin with, and how strong the new clue is. If a disease is super rare, even a very good medical test will give a lot of false alarms. Why? Because the test is checking thousands of healthy people, and even a tiny error rate will accidentally flag many healthy people. The theorem correctly calculates that a positive test does not automatically mean you are sick, because healthy people simply outnumber sick people by a massive amount.

The rule for counting successes works by tracking every possible path. If you want exactly two heads in three coin flips, you could get Head-Head-Tail, Head-Tail-Head, or Tail-Head-Head. The rule calculates the chance of one specific path, and then simply multiplies it by the number of possible paths that give the same result. Because each flip is completely separate from the others, their individual chances just multiply together like building blocks.

Three predictions.

def test_independence_on_two_dice():
    space = {(i, j) for i in range(1, 7) for j in range(1, 7)}
    first_even = {(i, j) for i, j in space if i % 2 == 0}
    second_six = {(i, j) for i, j in space if j == 6}
    # the two dice are independent
    assert are_independent(first_even, second_six, space) is True
    assert abs(conditional(first_even, second_six, space)
               - (len(first_even) / len(space))) < 1e-12
    # the sum being 7 is NOT independent of the first die being even
    sum_seven = {(i, j) for i, j in space if i + j == 7}
    assert are_independent(first_even, sum_seven, space) is True   # a famous coincidence
    sum_eight = {(i, j) for i, j in space if i + j == 8}
    assert are_independent(first_even, sum_eight, space) is False
    assert multiplication_rule_holds(first_even, sum_eight, space) is True


def test_exclusive_is_the_opposite_of_independent():
    space = set(range(1, 7))
    low = {1, 2}
    high = {5, 6}
    assert are_mutually_exclusive(low, high) is True
    assert are_independent(low, high, space) is False
    assert exclusive_implies_dependent(low, high, space) is True
    # knowing one happened tells you the other certainly did not
    assert abs(conditional(low, high, space)) < 1e-12
    # overlapping events are not mutually exclusive
    assert are_mutually_exclusive({1, 2, 3}, {3, 4}) is False
    assert exclusive_implies_dependent({1, 2, 3}, {3, 4}, space) is False

    # but cutting the false-positive rate helps enormously

    assert better_fpr > 10 * poor

            Misconceptions
              

            • "The probability of A given B and the probability of B given A are much the same." They can differ by an order of magnitude, as Kavita's 0.99 versus 0.17 shows. This confusion — the prosecutor's fallacy in court, the base rate fallacy in medicine — is the most costly error in applied probability.
              • 

            • "A 99% accurate test means a positive result is 99% likely to be right." Only if the condition is common. For a rare condition most positives are false, however good the test, because there are so many more candidates to be wrong about.
              • 

            • "Mutually exclusive means independent." They are near-opposites. Disjoint events with positive probability are maximally dependent: if one happened, the other definitely did not.
              • 

            • "Variance adds, like expectation." Expectation adds always. Variance adds only under independence, and positive correlation inflates it — the experiment measured the variance of X plus X is four times the variance of X, not 2.
              • 

            • "The expected value of a squared variable equals the square of its expected value." Never, unless the variance is zero. The gap between them is the variance.
              • 

            • "Improving the test is the way to fix a high false-alarm rate." For a rare condition, raising sensitivity from 0.90 to 1.00 barely moves the posterior; cutting the false-positive rate moves it enormously. The exercise measures exactly that.
              • 

            • "The binomial applies to any sequence of yes/no outcomes." It needs a fixed number of trials, a constant probability and independence. Sampling without replacement violates the last two, and the right model there is hypergeometric.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Conditioning on a probability-zero event is undefined, and for continuous variables every single outcome has probability zero — so "the probability that the waiting time is exactly 3 seconds given that it is at least 2" needs densities and a limiting definition, which is M34's job. The naive formula divides by zero.
            

            Bayes' theorem is only as good as its prior. Garbage in, garbage out: a confidently wrong prior produces a confidently wrong posterior, and when no honest prior is available the whole Bayesian apparatus is doing less work than it appears to. Equally, the likelihoods must come from somewhere; quoting a test's sensitivity from the manufacturer's study on a different population is a real and common error.
            

            Independence is an assumption about the world, not a mathematical convenience, and it is routinely false in exactly the situations where it matters most. Correlated failures — servers sharing a rack, loans sharing an economy, tests sharing a laboratory — make joint disasters far more likely than the independent calculation suggests. That mis-modelling was central to the 2008 financial crisis and to many outage post-mortems.
            

            Finally, the whole module assumes a well-defined sample space agreed in advance. Real problems often have ambiguous ones — Bertrand's paradox gives three different "correct" answers to a single innocuous question about a random chord — and no amount of correct arithmetic rescues an ill-posed question.
            In your real work
            This is the mathematics of every alert and every classifier. An anomaly detector with a 1% false-positive rate, run against a mostly-healthy fleet, produces a flood of false alarms — and the team learns to ignore it. That is Kavita's test, and the fix is the one the exercise measures: reduce the false-positive rate, because improving the detection rate barely helps when the base rate is low.
            

            Precision and recall are conditional probabilities in different directions: recall is the probability of being flagged given that an item is bad, and precision is the probability of being bad given that an item is flagged. Quoting one when asked about the other is the same error in different clothes, and it is why a model with 99% accuracy on a 1%-positive dataset may be worthless.
            

            Independence assumptions underpin retry logic, redundancy and SLO arithmetic. "Three replicas each with 99.9% availability gives nine nines" is true only if the failures are independent — and replicas in one availability zone, or depending on one config service, are not. Expectation's indifference to dependence is what makes it safe to reason about average load by summing; variance's sensitivity to it is why tail behaviour needs much more care.
            Flash cards
            What does conditioning on B do to the sample space?
            It narrows it to B, and renormalises by dividing by P(B) so the new probabilities sum to 1.
            Why are P(A|B) and P(B|A) different?
            They divide the same joint probability by different things. When P(A) and P(B) differ greatly, so do the two conditionals.
            State Bayes' theorem and name its four parts.
            Posterior equals prior times likelihood divided by evidence, where the evidence is the total probability of the observation.
            A test is 99% sensitive with a 5% false-positive rate, applied to a 1%-prevalent disease. What is P(disease | positive)?
            About 1 in 6, because 99 true positives are swamped by 495 false ones out of 10,000 people.
            Are mutually exclusive events independent?
            No, the opposite. Disjoint events of positive probability are maximally dependent, since one occurring rules out the other.
            Which adds without conditions, expectation or variance?
            Expectation always adds. Variance adds only when the variables are independent.
            Give the mean and variance of a binomial, and say where independence is needed.
            np and np(1-p). The mean follows from linearity alone; the variance needs the trials to be independent.
            What is the relation between Var(X) and E[X^2]?
            Var(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2, so the two square-quantities differ by exactly the variance.
            For a rare condition, is it better to improve sensitivity or reduce the false-positive rate?
            Reduce the false-positive rate. The posterior is governed by prevalence times sensitivity against the false-positive rate, and sensitivity is already near its ceiling.
            How do precision and recall relate to conditional probability?
            Recall is P(flagged | bad) and precision is P(bad | flagged) — the two directions of the same conditional, and not interchangeable.
            Stop when
            You can explain the ten-thousand-people argument to a child, you can derive Bayes' theorem from the multiplication rule in two lines, you never confuse P(A|B) with P(B|A) and can say why they differ, you can state exactly where independence is needed in deriving the binomial moments and where it is not, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can explain to a colleague why their 99%-accurate alert is firing mostly false alarms.
