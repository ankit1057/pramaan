The story

In a grand coastal astronomical observatory overlooking rocky cliffs, a young student rushes into the library holding a newly printed research treatise from an overseas academy. The treatise claims that on the winter solstice, a brilliant new blue star appeared directly inside the constellation of the Scorpion, outshining every celestial body in the southern sky!

The student shouts with awe: "Professor! A newly discovered star! The treatise has fifty pages of equations and twelve engraved illustrations! It will revolutionize all our navigational charts!"

The old observatory director adjusts his spectacles and does not look at the illustrations. Instead, he asks four quiet questions: "Did the authors publish their telescope aperture and lens curvature? Did they log the atmospheric humidity on that night? Did they record their raw angular measurements? Did they provide the mathematical derivation for their atmospheric refraction correction?"

The student hesitates: "They... they said their observations were confirmed by the academy's senior astronomer!"

The director walks to the brass telescope, points it to the exact sky coordinates, and invites the student to look through the eyepiece. The sky is pitch black. "A scientific paper," the director explains, "is not sacred scripture to be believed. It is an engineering blueprint. When an author publishes a claim, they are handing you a recipe and challenging you to bake the bread in your own oven. If their recipe cannot produce bread in our kitchen, they have not discovered a star—they have only described a speck of dust on their own telescope lens."

Say it simply

Reading scientific and technical papers is an active auditing skill, not passive consumption. Keshav's three-pass method structures reading: pass one surveys the big picture, pass two grasps the core content and evidence, and pass three virtually re-implements the work to find hidden assumptions. Replicating results requires auditing statistical calculations, distinguishing between repeatability, reproducibility, and replicability (ACM standards), and synthesizing multiple studies through meta-analysis to evaluate effect sizes and heterogeneity.

Say it precisely

Imagine you are reading a magical recipe book that claims it can make a cake that tastes like pure joy. As a smart reader, you know you shouldn't just trust the picture of the cake. You need to know if the recipe actually works in real life!

When scientists share their work, we use three different levels to test if their recipe is actually real: 1. Repeatability: If the original baker makes the cake again in their own kitchen, does it still taste good? 2. Reproducibility: If you go into the baker's kitchen and use their exact bowls, spoons, and ingredients, can you make the same cake? 3. Replicability: The ultimate test! If you buy your own bowls and your own ingredients, and bake it in your completely different kitchen at home, does it still work?

If a magic trick only works when the magician does it in a special room with special lighting, it's not a very good trick. A claim is only considered true science if anyone, anywhere, can follow the instructions and get the exact same awesome result every single time!

Why it's true

Why do we need such strict tests for checking scientific papers and reports? Because, shockingly, more than half of the fancy experiments you read about in magazines or online cannot actually be repeated! When other scientists try to build the exact same robot or write the exact same code, it totally breaks. Why? Sometimes the original inventor forgot to write down a crucial step, or they used a very specific tool that no one else has.

Also, scientists are humans, and humans love to show off their big wins while hiding their mistakes. If someone tries to jump over a huge puddle ten times and falls in nine times, but makes it once, they might just take a picture of the one successful jump and say, "Look how amazing I am!" This is called publication bias.

To defend yourself from fake claims, you must become an expert reader. First, do a quick ten-minute scan of the big titles to see what the story is about. Second, spend an hour looking closely at their charts and pictures to see if anything looks weird or impossible. Third, completely rebuild their project in your own imagination step-by-step to see if it makes sense. Finally, when you want to know the absolute truth, you collect results from many different people trying the experiment and combine them together to find the real, honest answer.

      GRIM test for reported mean plausibility on discrete Likert scales
      
not tried
      

      Write grim_test_checker(reported_mean, sample_size, scale_min=1, scale_max=7, decimals=2):
The GRIM (Granularity-Related Inconsistency of Means) test verifies whether a reported mean of discrete integer responses could mathematically exist.
Mathematical Rule:
If N participants answer on an integer scale from S_{\min} to S_{\max}, the sum of their responses must be an exact integer T \in [N \cdot S_{\min}, N \cdot S_{\max}].
Therefore, the true mean must be \bar{X} = \frac{T}{N}.
For a reported mean rounded to D decimal places, it is GRIM-consistent if and only if there exists an integer T such that:
\left|\text{round}\left(\frac{T}{N}, D\right) - \text{reported_mean}\right| == 0
Requirements:
1. sample_size must be positive integer; reported_mean must lie within [scale_min, scale_max]. Raise ValueError on violation.
2. Check whether any valid integer sum T \in [N \cdot S_{\min}, N \cdot S_{\max}] produces the reported mean when rounded to D decimal places.
3. Return dictionary:
   {'is_grim_consistent': bool, 'possible_integer_sums': [matching_T_values], 'granularity': round(1.0 / sample_size, 4)}.

not tried
        

        Implement fixed_effects_meta_analysis(studies):
Inputs:
- studies: Non-empty list of study dicts: {'id': str, 'effect_size': float, 'std_error': float}.
        

        Requirements:
1. Validate inputs: All std_error must be > 0. Length of studies must be \ge 2. Raise ValueError on violation.
2. For each study i:
   - Weight w_i = \frac{1}{\text{std_error}i^2}
3. Pooled effect size:
   \hat{\theta} = \frac{\sum w_i \cdot \text{effect_size}_i}{\sum w_i}
4. Pooled standard error:
   SE(\hat{\theta}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum w_i}}
5. 95 percent Confidence Interval:
   [\hat{\theta} - 1.96 \cdot SE(\hat{\theta}), \; \hat{\theta} + 1.96 \cdot SE(\hat{\theta})]
6. Cochran's Heterogeneity Q:
   Q = \sum{i=1}^k w_i (\text{effect_size}_i - \hat{\theta})^2
7. Higgins' I^2:
   I^2 = \max\left(0.0, \frac{Q - (k - 1)}{Q}\right) \times 100\ percent
Return dictionary:
{'pooled_effect': round(theta, 4), 'pooled_se': round(se, 4), 'ci_95': (ci_low, ci_high), 'cochran_q': round(Q, 4), 'i2_pct': round(I2, 2)}.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "If a paper is published in a peer-reviewed journal, its statistics must be correct." Independent audits demonstrate that over 12 percent of published peer-reviewed papers contain mathematical or reporting errors.
            • 

          • "Repeatability, reproducibility, and replicability are interchangeable synonyms." ACM formally distinguishes them: Repeatability is same team, Reproducibility is different team with same artifact, Replicability is different team with new artifacts.
            • 

          • "A non-reproducible paper was necessarily written by fraudulent authors." Most non-reproducibility stems from mundane software engineering decay: unpinned package dependencies, deleted repository links, and missing build steps.
            • 

          • "Meta-analysis is just taking the average of reported study findings." Naive averaging ignores sample size precision; meta-analysis uses inverse-variance weighting so large, low-variance studies dominate the consensus.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Paper reproduction and meta-analysis break down when original raw datasets are proprietary, encrypted, or restricted by patient privacy laws (HIPAA/GDPR), preventing third-party recomputation. Furthermore, when publication bias is extreme (funnel plot asymmetry), meta-analysis pools only the surviving positive studies, inflating the estimated effect size above empirical reality.
          In your real work
          In research engineering and high-reliability systems work, reading a paper means reproducing its baseline in an isolated container before adopting the technique. In a data pipeline, a sync algorithm should be adopted only after its claimed throughput and memory characteristics have been reproduced against real database traces.
          Flash cards
          What are the three passes in Keshav's Three-Pass paper reading method?
          Pass 1: Birds-eye view (10 min). Pass 2: Grasp content without details (1 hour). Pass 3: Virtual re-implementation (several hours).
          What is the ACM definition of Reproducibility?
          An independent team obtains identical results using the original author's artifacts on the same experimental setup.
          What is the ACM definition of Replicability?
          An independent team obtains consistent findings using completely independent code, fresh data, or different experimental setups.
          What is the GRIM test?
          A mathematical test checking whether reported means of integer Likert ratings are consistent with integer arithmetic given the reported sample size.
          How does inverse-variance weighting work in meta-analysis?
          Each study's effect size is weighted by w_i = 1 / SE_i^2, giving higher statistical influence to studies with smaller standard errors.
          What does Higgins' I^2 measure in meta-analysis?
          The percentage of variation across study results that is due to true heterogeneity rather than chance sampling error.
          Stop when
          You can execute Keshav's three-pass reading method, audit a paper's reported t-test statistics for mathematical coherence, and perform an inverse-variance meta-analysis.
