The story

Nina's teacher draws a number line and says every point on it is a number. Nina believes him.

Then he gives her a game. "Write down fractions whose square is less than two. As many as you like. I will tell you when you have the biggest one."

She writes 1. Then 1.4. Then 1.41, 1.414, 1.4142. Each one squares to less than two, and each is bigger than the last.

"Which is the biggest?" he asks.

She says there isn't one — she can always add a digit.

"Fine. Then what is the smallest fraction that is bigger than all of them?"

She tries 1.5, then 1.42, then 1.4143. Each time she finds a smaller one that still works. There is no smallest.

Her teacher lets her struggle, then says the thing that reorganises her picture of numbers. "Among the fractions, that question has no answer. There is a hole where the answer should be. The number line you drew is not made of fractions — it is made of fractions and the things the fractions are reaching towards."

Here is the surprise: the real numbers are not defined by what they are, but by a guarantee — that every set with a ceiling has a least ceiling, with no holes. That one guarantee, called completeness, is what makes limits work, what makes a continuous function on a closed interval attain its maximum, and what separates analysis from arithmetic.

Say it simply

Analysis is calculus with the guarantees made explicit. M22 said a limit is "what the values head towards". That is a picture, not a definition, and pictures are where errors hide.

The precise version is the epsilon game: $a_n\to L$ means that for any tolerance you name, however small, the sequence eventually stays inside it and never leaves.

The foundation underneath is completeness: every non-empty set of reals with an upper bound has a least upper bound. The rationals fail this — Nina's hole — and almost every theorem that feels obvious about continuous functions is really completeness in disguise.

The rest of the module is the consequences: when a sequence converges, when a series converges, what continuity really demands, what goes wrong when you swap a limit with something else, and how to write down the growth rate of a function honestly.

Say it precisely

Completeness and suprema

An upper bound of $S\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is $M$ with $x\le M$ for all $x\in S$. The supremum $\sup S$ is the least upper bound; the infimum is the greatest lower bound.

Completeness axiom. Every non-empty subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above has a supremum in $\mathbb{R}$.

The supremum need not belong to the set: $\sup\{x : x<1\} = 1\notin S$. The rationals fail the axiom — $\{q\in\mathbb{Q}: q^2<2\}$ is bounded above and has no rational supremum, which is Nina's hole.

Archimedean property (a consequence): for any real $x$ there is a natural number $n>x$.

Sequences

$a_n\to L$ means: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N$ such that $n>N$ implies $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$.

The order of quantifiers is the content. For every $\varepsilon$, there exists an $N$ — and $N$ is allowed to depend on $\varepsilon$. Reversing them gives a different and much stronger statement, which is exactly the difference between pointwise and uniform convergence below.

A sequence is Cauchy if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is an $N$ with $|a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon$ for all $m,n>N$: the terms bunch together, with no reference to a limit.

Completeness, restated. In $\mathbb{R}$, a sequence converges if and only if it is Cauchy. In $\mathbb{Q}$ the forward direction holds and the converse fails — Nina's decimals are Cauchy and converge to nothing rational.

Monotone convergence theorem. A bounded monotone sequence converges, to its supremum (if increasing) or infimum (if decreasing).

Bolzano-Weierstrass. Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence.

Series

$\sum a_n$ converges when its partial sums converge.

  • $n$-th term test: if $a_n\not\to0$, the series diverges. The converse is false: the harmonic series $\sum\frac1n$ has terms tending to zero and diverges.
  • Comparison: if $0\le a_n\le b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges, so does $\sum a_n$.
  • $p$-series: $\sum\frac{1}{n^p}$ converges iff $p>1$.
  • Ratio test: with $L=\lim\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|$, converges absolutely if $L<1$, diverges if $L>1$, inconclusive if $L=1$.
  • Root test: same verdicts with $L=\lim\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}$. Strictly stronger than the ratio test.
  • Alternating series test: if $b_n$ decreases to 0 then $\sum(-1)^nb_n$ converges, with error after $n$ terms at most $b_{n+1}$.

Absolute convergence means $\sum|a_n|$ converges; it implies convergence. Conditional convergence is convergence without absolute convergence, and Riemann's rearrangement theorem says a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to sum to any real number, or to diverge. Addition stops being commutative.

Continuity

$f$ is continuous at $a$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $|x-a|<\delta \implies |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon$. Here $\delta$ may depend on both $\varepsilon$ and $a$.

$f$ is uniformly continuous on a set if one $\delta$ works for every $a$ at once:

$$\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x,y:\ |x-y|<\delta\implies|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$$

$f(x)=x^2$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ and not uniformly so: as $x$ grows the function steepens without limit. $f(x)=\frac1x$ on $(0,1]$ is the same story near zero.

Heine-Cantor theorem. A continuous function on a closed bounded interval is uniformly continuous. Compactness is what removes the dependence on $a$.

Also on a closed bounded interval: the Extreme Value Theorem (a continuous function attains its bounds) and the Intermediate Value Theorem. Both fail on open intervals, and both are completeness in disguise.

Uniform convergence

$f_n\to f$ pointwise if $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ for each $x$ separately. Uniformly if

$$\sup_x|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$$

one $N$ serving every $x$. Uniform convergence preserves continuity, and permits term-by-term integration; pointwise convergence permits neither. $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$ converges pointwise to a discontinuous limit, and the supremum distance stays at 1 forever.

Taylor's theorem

If $f$ is $n+1$ times differentiable on an interval containing $a$ and $x$:

$$f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k + R_n(x), \qquad R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}$$

for some $\xi$ strictly between $a$ and $x$ (the Lagrange remainder). The $n=0$ case is the Mean Value Theorem of M22. The remainder is what makes Taylor a theorem rather than a hope: it bounds the error, and a series converges to its function only when $R_n\to0$ — which can fail even for an infinitely differentiable function.

Asymptotic notation

For functions of a positive integer or real variable, as $n\to\infty$:

$$f = O(g) \iff \exists C,N:\ |f(n)|\le C|g(n)| \text{ for } n>N$$ $$f = \Omega(g) \iff g = O(f), \qquad f = \Theta(g) \iff f=O(g) \text{ and } g=O(f)$$ $$f = o(g) \iff \lim\frac{f}{g}=0, \qquad f\sim g \iff \lim\frac{f}{g}=1$$

$O$ is an upper bound, $\Theta$ a tight bound, $o$ a strict upper bound, and $\sim$ asymptotic equality. Note $f=o(g)$ implies $f=O(g)$ but not conversely, and that $f\sim g$ is much stronger than $f=\Theta(g)$: $2n\sim 2n$ but $2n\not\sim n$, although $2n=\Theta(n)$.

A pinch of history

Calculus worked for 150 years on shaky foundations. Bishop Berkeley's The Analyst (1734) called infinitesimals "ghosts of departed quantities", and he was right that the logic was unsound. The repair — the rigorisation of analysis — ran through Bolzano (1817), Cauchy's Cours d'Analyse (1821), and Weierstrass, whose lectures from the 1860s gave the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definitions in the form used today.

Weierstrass also produced the function that ended naive intuition: a function continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere (1872). Dedekind (1872) and Cantor independently constructed the reals from the rationals, finally filling Nina's hole by definition rather than assertion.

Riemann's rearrangement theorem appeared posthumously in 1866. Landau popularised $O$ notation in 1909, having taken it from Bachmann (1894); Knuth introduced $\Omega$ and $\Theta$ in their modern computer-science senses in 1976, explicitly to stop people writing $O$ when they meant a tight bound.

Why it's true

Result 1: a bounded monotone sequence converges.

  1. Let $(a_n)$ be increasing and bounded above. The set $S=\{a_n\}$ is non-empty and bounded, so by completeness it has a supremum $L$.
  2. Let $\varepsilon>0$. Then $L-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound (since $L$ is the least one), so some $a_N>L-\varepsilon$.
  3. The sequence increases, so $a_n\ge a_N>L-\varepsilon$ for all $n>N$.
  4. Also $a_n\le L$ for all $n$, since $L$ is an upper bound.
  5. Hence $L-\varepsilon<a_n\le L$, so $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$ for all $n>N$. That is convergence to $L$.

Step 2 is where completeness does the work — the existence of a least upper bound is exactly what gives the $N$. In $\mathbb{Q}$ the argument collapses at step 1, which is Nina's game.

Result 2: the harmonic series diverges, by Oresme's grouping (c. 1350).

$$1+\underbrace{\tfrac12}_{\ge\frac12}+\underbrace{\tfrac13+\tfrac14}_{\ge\frac12}+\underbrace{\tfrac15+\dots+\tfrac18}_{\ge\frac12}+\dots$$

Each group of $2^k$ terms is at least $\frac12$, so the partial sums exceed $1+\frac{k}{2}$ and grow without bound. The terms tend to zero and the sum does not converge — the standard refutation of "terms go to zero, therefore it converges".

Result 3: $x^n$ on $[0,1]$ converges pointwise but not uniformly.

  1. For fixed $x<1$, $x^n\to0$; at $x=1$, $x^n=1$ for all $n$. So the pointwise limit is $f(x)=0$ for $x<1$ and $f(1)=1$ — discontinuous.
  2. For uniform convergence we would need $\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|x^n-f(x)|\to0$.
  3. The non-obvious step: take $x_n = \left(\frac12\right)^{1/n}$, which lies in $(0,1)$. Then $x_n^n = \frac12$ exactly, while $f(x_n)=0$.
  4. So the supremum is at least $\frac12$ for every $n$, and does not tend to zero.
  5. Hence the convergence is not uniform. And indeed the limit is discontinuous, which uniform convergence would have forbidden.

Step 3 is the whole proof: the trouble spot moves as $n$ grows, which pointwise convergence cannot see because it fixes $x$ first.

Result 4: continuous on a compact interval implies uniformly continuous, by contradiction.

  1. Suppose $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ but not uniformly continuous. Then there is an $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that for every $\delta=\frac1n$ there are points $x_n,y_n$ with $|x_n-y_n|<\frac1n$ and $|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|\ge\varepsilon_0$.
  2. By Bolzano-Weierstrass the bounded sequence $(x_n)$ has a convergent subsequence $x_{n_k}\to c\in[a,b]$.
  3. Since $|x_{n_k}-y_{n_k}|\to0$, the corresponding $y_{n_k}\to c$ too.
  4. Continuity at $c$ gives $f(x_{n_k})\to f(c)$ and $f(y_{n_k})\to f(c)$, so their difference tends to 0.
  5. That contradicts $|f(x_{n_k})-f(y_{n_k})|\ge\varepsilon_0>0$.

Closedness and boundedness enter only at step 2, and that is exactly why the theorem fails on $(0,1]$: the subsequence can escape to a point that is not in the set.

Result 5: why conditional convergence breaks rearrangement. If $\sum a_n$ converges but $\sum|a_n|$ does not, then the positive terms alone sum to $+\infty$ and the negative terms alone to $-\infty$ (otherwise both would be finite and the series absolutely convergent). To reach any target $T$: add positive terms until you exceed $T$, then negative terms until you drop below, and repeat. Since the terms tend to zero the overshoots shrink to nothing, so the rearranged partial sums converge to $T$. The two infinite reservoirs never run dry, so this works for every $T$.

Oracle lab

The oracle decides convergence two genuinely independent ways.

The test route applies the ratio and root tests, which look only at the terms' asymptotic behaviour and never add anything up.

The partial-sum route sums many terms and watches the behaviour directly — whether the sums settle or keep climbing.

They have no step in common: one is a limit of ratios, the other an accumulation. The demo also checks that absolutely convergent series are invariant under rearrangement while a conditionally convergent one is not. Two predictions, both about the gap between pointwise and uniform behaviour.


First, $x^n$ on $[0,1]$. Theory says it converges pointwise to a discontinuous function, and that the supremum distance to the limit stays at least $\frac12$ forever — so the convergence is not uniform, no matter how large $n$ gets. We measure that supremum.


Second, uniform continuity. Theory says $x^2$ on a closed bounded interval is uniformly continuous (Heine-Cantor), but on all of $\mathbb{R}$ it is not: for a fixed gap $\delta$, the change in $f$ grows without bound as $x$ grows. def test_failure_of_uniform_continuity_on_unbounded_sets():
    f = lambda x: x * x
    # the same function on a far-away interval needs a far smaller delta
    near = worst_change(f, 0.0, 5.0, 0.001)
    far = worst_change(f, 10000.0, 10005.0, 0.001)
    assert far > near * 100, "the required delta depends on where you are"
    assert far > 10.0
    # 1/x near zero is the same failure
    g = lambda x: 1.0 / x
    mild = worst_change(g, 0.5, 1.0, 1e-4)
    wild = worst_change(g, 0.001, 0.01, 1e-4)
    assert wild > mild * 100
    # on a closed interval away from zero it is fine
    assert uniformly_continuous_on(g, 0.5, 2.0, 0.01, [0.1, 0.01, 0.001]) is True
    # On a long interval the left end looks harmless while the far end is not,

def test_taylor_and_its_remainder():
    from math import exp, factorial, sin, cos
    # e^x about 0: every derivative is 1
    derivs = [1.0] * 15
    for x in (0.1, 0.5, 1.0):
        for n in (2, 4, 8):
            approx = taylor(derivs, 0.0, x, n)
            err = abs(exp(x) - approx)
            # on [0, x] the max of e^t is e^x
            bound = lagrange_remainder_bound(exp(x), 0.0, x, n)
            assert err <= bound + 1e-15, (x, n, err, bound)

def test_asymptotic_notation():
    from math import log
    n2 = lambda n: float(n * n)
    n3 = lambda n: float(n ** 3)
    nlogn = lambda n: n * log(n) if n > 1 else 1.0

    assert is_big_o(n2, n3, 1.0, 1, 2000) is True
    assert is_big_o(n3, n2, 1.0, 1, 2000) is False

            Misconceptions
              

            • "The terms go to zero, so the series converges." The harmonic series refutes this. Vanishing terms are necessary and never sufficient.
              • 

            • "A Cauchy sequence always converges." In $\mathbb{R}$, yes — that is completeness. In $\mathbb{Q}$ it is false, and Nina's decimals are the counterexample.
              • 

            • "The supremum is the maximum." Only when it belongs to the set. $\sup\{x: x<1\}=1$ is not attained, which is why the definition speaks of least upper bounds.
              • 

            • "Continuous means uniformly continuous." Only on a closed bounded set (Heine-Cantor). $x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ and $\frac1x$ on $(0,1]$ are both continuous and not uniformly so.
              • 

            • "Pointwise convergence is enough." It preserves almost nothing. $x^n$ on $[0,1]$ converges pointwise to a discontinuous function; uniform convergence would have forbidden that.
              • 

            • "Addition is commutative, so I can reorder a sum." For a conditionally convergent series you can rearrange it to reach any value. Only absolute convergence licenses reordering.
              • 

            • "$O$ means the running time." $O$ is an upper bound and nothing more. Saying an algorithm is $O(n^2)$ leaves open that it is $O(n)$; if you mean tight, write $\Theta$.
              • 

            • "An infinitely differentiable function equals its Taylor series." Not necessarily. $e^{-1/x^2}$ (with $f(0)=0$) has every derivative zero at the origin, so its Taylor series is identically zero and matches the function only at one point.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Completeness is an axiom, not a theorem — or rather, it is the defining property in any construction of $\mathbb{R}$, whether by Dedekind cuts or by equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences. You cannot prove it from the ordered-field axioms, because $\mathbb{Q}$ satisfies those and fails completeness.
            

            Convergence tests have gaps. The ratio test is inconclusive for every p-series, which is precisely where the interesting boundary lies, and the root test — though strictly stronger — is inconclusive there too. Deciding convergence in general has no algorithm.
            

            The epsilon-delta machinery assumes exact arithmetic. In floating point there is no "for every $\varepsilon$": below machine epsilon everything collapses, a numerically computed limit stalls, and the demos here must therefore stop at finite tolerances. The mathematical statements are about an idealisation that computation can illustrate but never verify.
            

            And the intuition-breakers are real. The Weierstrass function is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere. The Cantor set is uncountable with measure zero. A function can be Riemann-integrable while discontinuous on a dense set. Every one of these was found by someone checking whether an "obvious" claim was actually true — which is what this whole subject is for.
            In your real work
            The direct payoff is reading and writing complexity claims honestly. $O$, $\Theta$, $o$ and $\sim$ mean different things, and the habit of saying $\Theta$ when you mean tight — and admitting $O$ when you only have an upper bound — is a small discipline that prevents a lot of hand-waving in design documents.
            

            The convergence material is the theory behind every iterative method you will write: when a fixed-point iteration converges, how fast, and what guarantees it. The monotone convergence theorem is why a bounded increasing metric must settle. The Cauchy criterion is the practical stopping rule — you cannot compare to a limit you do not know, so you compare successive iterates instead, which is exactly what every solver in M23 and M25 does.
            

            Uniform versus pointwise is the distinction behind interchanging limits, which in practice means: can I take the average of the limits, or must I take the limit of the averages? Swapping them without justification is a genuine source of wrong results in numerical and statistical work.
            

            And Taylor's remainder is the honest version of "approximately": it does not just say the approximation is good, it says how good, which is the difference between an estimate and a guarantee.
            Flash cards
            State the completeness axiom and name one thing it fails for.
            Every non-empty set of reals bounded above has a least upper bound in the reals. The rationals fail it.
            Give the epsilon-N definition of convergence, and say why the quantifier order matters.
            For every epsilon there exists N such that beyond N the terms are within epsilon. N may depend on epsilon; demanding one N for all cases is a different, stronger statement.
            What is a Cauchy sequence, and what does it have to do with completeness?
            One whose terms eventually bunch within any tolerance, with no reference to a limit. In the reals, Cauchy and convergent are equivalent; that equivalence is completeness.
            Do vanishing terms guarantee a convergent series?
            No. The harmonic series has terms tending to zero and diverges.
            For which p does the sum of 1/n^p converge?
            Exactly for p greater than 1. At p = 1 it is the harmonic series.
            What is the difference between continuity and uniform continuity?
            For continuity delta may depend on the point; for uniform continuity one delta must work everywhere. Heine-Cantor says they coincide on a closed bounded interval.
            Why is x^n on [0,1] not uniformly convergent?
            Because the trouble spot moves: at x = (1/2)^(1/n) the value is exactly 1/2 for every n, so the supremum distance never shrinks.
            What does Riemann's rearrangement theorem say?
            A conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any real number, or to diverge. Only absolute convergence makes the sum order-independent.
            What does the Lagrange remainder add to a Taylor polynomial?
            A bound on the error, in terms of a higher derivative at some intermediate point. It turns an approximation into a guarantee.
            Distinguish O, Theta, little-o and the tilde.
            O is an upper bound, Theta a tight bound, little-o a strict upper bound (ratio to zero), and the tilde asymptotic equality (ratio to one).
            Stop when
            You can explain Nina's hole to a child, you can give the epsilon-N definition without looking and say exactly where the quantifier order matters, you can prove the monotone convergence theorem and point to the step that uses completeness, you can produce the moving witness that shows x^n is not uniformly convergent, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you never again write O when you mean Theta.
