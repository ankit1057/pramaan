The story

Imagine you are playing ring-toss at the summer village carnival. You are trying to throw a wooden ring so that it lands completely around a shiny golden bottle standing on the table. The carnival master tells you that if you throw your ring with a blindfold on, your ring will land around the golden bottle 95 times out of every 100 throws.

Here is the puzzle that confuses almost every grown-up: after you throw your ring and it lands clattering on the floor, is there a 95% chance that the bottle is inside the ring?

No! The bottle is not jumping around or rolling on wheels. The bottle is nailed firmly to the table. The bottle either is completely inside your ring, or completely outside your ring! The 95% does not belong to the static bottle; the 95% belongs to you and your ring-throwing arm before you threw. The confidence interval is the ring, and the true population truth is the motionless golden bottle. If you repeat the experiment tomorrow with twenty fresh samples, nineteen of your twenty computed rings will surround the bottle, and one ring will miss it entirely.

Say it simply

Point estimation provides a single best guess $\hat{\theta}$ for an unknown population parameter $\theta$. The mean squared error decomposes into $\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Var}(\hat{\theta})$. Because point estimates inevitably vary across random samples, interval estimation constructs a random confidence interval $[L, U]$ that covers the true fixed parameter with specified coverage probability $1 - \alpha$.

A 95% confidence interval does NOT mean there is a 95% probability that the true parameter lies within the realized interval. Rather, under hypothetical infinite repetitions of the sampling procedure, 95% of the calculated intervals will contain the true parameter. For proportions, the standard textbook Wald interval fails catastrophically near boundaries; the Wilson score interval or exact Clopper-Pearson interval must be used instead.

Say it precisely

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n \sim F(\theta)$. An estimator $\hat{\theta} = g(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ has: 1. Bias: $\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta$. 2. Variance: $\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[(\hat{\theta} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}])^2]$. 3. Mean Squared Error (MSE): $$\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2] = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}^2(\hat{\theta})$$ 4. Confidence Interval Definition: An interval $[L(X), U(X)]$ has nominal confidence level $1 - \alpha$ if: $$\inf_\theta \mathbb{P}_\theta(L(X) \le \theta \le U(X)) \ge 1 - \alpha$$ 5. CLT Mean Interval: For large $n$, $\bar{X} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$. For small $n$ with normal errors, replace $z$ with Student's $t_{n-1}$. 6. Proportion Intervals: For sample proportion $\hat{p} = k/n$: - Wald: $\hat{p} \pm z \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}$ (known to severely undercover). - Wilson Score: $\frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}$. - Clopper-Pearson: Inverts the Binomial CDF using Beta quantiles: $$L = \text{Beta}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}, k, n-k+1\right), \quad U = \text{Beta}\left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{2}, k+1, n-k\right)$$

Why it's true

Why does the textbook Wald interval fail so severely for proportions, and why does the Wilson score interval solve it?

Step 1 (The Wald assumption): The Wald interval estimates standard error using the sample proportion: $\widehat{\text{SE}} = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$. When $k=0$ or $k=n$, $\hat{p} \in \{0, 1\}$, resulting in $\widehat{\text{SE}} = 0$. The Wald formula outputs the degenerate interval $[0, 0]$ or $[1, 1]$ with zero width, guaranteeing an empirical coverage probability of $0\%$ whenever $p \in (0, 1)$.

Step 2 (The test inversion principle): A mathematically sound confidence interval is formed by inverting a family of hypothesis tests. We seek all candidate parameter values $p_0$ that are not rejected by the score test: $$\left| \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}} \right| \le z$$ Crucially, the denominator uses the true hypothesized variance $p_0(1-p_0)/n$, not the sample estimate $\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n$.

Step 3 (Solving the quadratic): Squaring both sides yields: $$(\hat{p} - p_0)^2 \le z^2 \frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}$$ Expanding this into a quadratic equation in $p_0$: $$\left(1 + \frac{z^2}{n}\right) p_0^2 - \left(2\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{n}\right) p_0 + \hat{p}^2 \le 0$$ The roots of this quadratic equation are exactly the endpoints of the Wilson score interval! Because the coefficient of $p_0^2$ is strictly positive, the interval is guaranteed to have non-zero width even when $\hat{p} = 0$, pulling the center toward $1/2$ and restoring nominal coverage.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "A 95% confidence interval means there is a 95% probability the true mean lies in this specific interval." The parameter is a fixed, non-random constant. The 95% refers to the long-run coverage rate of the interval estimation procedure across hypothetical repeated samples.
            • 

          • "The Wald interval is always valid as long as sample size $n > 30$." Even at $n=100$, if $p$ is rare (e.g. $p=0.01$), the Wald interval suffers severe downward coverage bias and zero-width boundary collapse.
            • 

          • "Non-parametric bootstrap works on arbitrary clustered data without modification." Resampling individual observations destroys cluster correlation and severely understates confidence interval width; cluster units must be resampled intact.
            • 

          • "Standard error and standard deviation are the same thing." Standard deviation measures the variability of individual data points; standard error measures the sampling variability of a statistic (such as the sample mean).
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Asymptotic confidence intervals break down when data is heavy-tailed (e.g. Pareto-distributed server response times) where the Central Limit Theorem converges at an extremely slow rate ($O(1/\sqrt{n})$ or worse). In small samples from skewed non-normal distributions, standard Student's $t$ intervals provide unbalanced one-sided error rates.
          In your real work
          When reporting app conversion rates or rare crash rates — say 2 crashes in 10,000 sessions — engineers using standard analytics dashboards frequently see naive Wald error bars whose bounds dip below zero or look deceptively tight. Wilson score or Clopper-Pearson intervals give reliability bounds that survive scrutiny.
          Flash cards
          What does Mean Squared Error (MSE) equal in terms of bias and variance?
          $\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}^2(\hat{\theta})$.
          Why does the Wald confidence interval for a proportion fail near $p=0$ and $p=1$?
          Because it estimates standard error using $\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})$; when $k=0$, $\hat{p}=0$, collapsing the interval to $[0, 0]$ with zero width and zero coverage.
          How does the Wilson score interval resolve the Wald interval's failure?
          It inverts the score test by using the hypothesized parameter $p_0(1-p_0)$ in the denominator, solving a quadratic equation that yields valid positive width even when $k=0$.
          What is the exact coverage guarantee of the Clopper-Pearson interval?
          It guarantees that the true coverage probability is strictly greater than or equal to $1 - \alpha$ for all parameter values, achieved by inverting Binomial tail probabilities using Beta quantiles.
          Why must clustered data be bootstrapped by resampling clusters rather than individual units?
          Because resampling individual observations assumes independent errors, ignoring intra-cluster correlation and producing artificially narrow, undercovering confidence intervals.
          What is the difference between a standard deviation and a standard error?
          Standard deviation measures the dispersion of values in a population; standard error measures the dispersion of a sample statistic across repeated samples.
          Stop when
          You can explain why a 95% confidence interval does not mean a 95% chance to a product manager, implement the Wilson score interval from scratch, and apply the cluster bootstrap to multi-tenant telemetry.
