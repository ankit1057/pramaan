The story

Imagine you are walking through an ancient museum with your grandfather, and you spot a tiny silver bell resting inside a glass case. Your grandfather tells you that in the old days, castle guards rang this bell only when a fire dragon flew across the kingdom. Suddenly, the little silver bell rings with a clear DING!

Do you scream and run away from a fiery dragon?

A six-year-old might gasp in terror, but a wise detective pauses. First, how often do real dragons exist in the world? Dragons are almost completely imaginary—maybe one dragon sighting in a hundred years! Second, how often does a curious child bump into the glass case and make the bell jingle by accident? Children bump into the case every single afternoon!

Even if the magical bell is 99% accurate at detecting dragons, the bell is thousands of times more likely to have been bumped by an accidental elbow than to have spotted a real dragon. This trap is called the base-rate fallacy. You cannot judge what a signal means without knowing how rare the thing was before the signal ever arrived!

Say it simply

Bayesian reasoning treats probability as a quantified degree of belief that updates rationally in light of new evidence: Posterior is proportional to Likelihood times Prior. The base-rate fallacy occurs when prior probabilities are neglected, leading to gross misinterpretations of rare-event tests.

Unlike frequentist confidence intervals (which reflect the long-run coverage of a hypothetical sampling procedure), a Bayesian credible interval has the natural interpretation: given the prior and observed data, there is a 95% probability that the parameter lies within the interval. For binary observations, the Beta-Binomial conjugate family provides closed-form posterior updates: observing k successes and n-k failures updates prior text{Beta}(alpha, beta) to posterior a Beta distribution combining prior and data.

Say it precisely

Let's understand how to update our beliefs without getting tricked.

  1. The Clue Rule (Bayes' Theorem): A rule for updating your guesses when you get new clues. You start with how likely something was before the clue, and adjust it based on how well the clue matches the story.
  2. The Scoreboard Trick (Beta-Binomial Conjugacy): Instead of using complicated math, you can just keep a scoreboard. If you used to think a player had 2 wins and 5 losses, and today they get 3 wins and 1 loss, you just add them together: 5 wins and 6 losses! Your old belief and new clues just snap together like building blocks.
  3. The "Play It Safe" Rule (Laplace's Rule of Succession): If you flip a brand new coin and get heads, is it a magic coin that will ALWAYS land on heads? No! To prevent guessing 100 percent too early, you pretend the coin started with one invisible "heads" and one invisible "tails" already on the scoreboard before you even flipped it.
  4. The Virtual Race (Bayesian A/B Comparison): To see if Toy B is better than Toy A, you program a computer to race thousands of virtual copies of the toys based on their scoreboards. Then you just count how many times Toy B won the virtual race!
  5. The Tug of War (Bayes Factor): A way to measure which of two stories is pulling harder on the clues.

Why it's true

Why do 99 percent accurate bells give so many false alarms, and why does the scoreboard trick work so perfectly?

Step 1 (The False Alarm Crowd): Imagine a town of 1,000 people. Only 1 person has a rare cold. The doctor's bell is 99 percent accurate. When the 1 sick person walks in, the bell rings correctly. But when the 999 healthy people walk in, the bell makes mistakes about 1 percent of the time. That means the bell rings by accident for about 10 healthy people! Out of 11 total rings, 10 are completely fake! So if the bell rings for you, there is only a tiny chance you are actually sick. The giant crowd of healthy people creates a giant crowd of false alarms.

Step 2 (Snapping the blocks together): When mathematicians designed the "Beta" curve to represent beliefs, they designed it to look exactly like a pile of win/loss tokens. The new clues you collect (the Binomial) are ALSO just a pile of win/loss tokens. Because both your old belief and your new clues are made of the exact same type of tokens, updating your belief is as simple as sweeping all the tokens into one single pile. It is just simple addition!

          Misconceptions
            

          • "A Bayesian credible interval and a frequentist confidence interval mean the same thing." A 95% credible interval means there is a 95% posterior probability the parameter is inside the interval; a frequentist interval means 95% of repeated intervals will cover the fixed parameter.
            • 

          • "Bayesian statistics is unscientific because priors are subjective." Priors explicitly declare baseline assumptions and past evidence; sensitivity analyses verify that reasonable priors converge to the same posterior under data.
            • 

          • "Laplace's rule of succession is an ad-hoc hack." It is the exact mathematical expectation of a Bernoulli parameter under a flat uniform a flat uniform prior prior.
            • 

          • "In A/B testing, you should deploy as soon as the probability B is better is over 50 percent." Decisions must minimize Expected Loss; deploying with high uncertainty risks catastrophic downside even if B has a slight probability advantage.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Bayesian inference breaks down completely when dogmatic prior distributions assign zero probability density to regions containing the true parameter (Cromwell's rule), making Bayesian updating mathematically impossible regardless of how much empirical data is observed. In high-dimensional models with complex or non-convex posterior geometries, MCMC sampling suffers from pathological divergences and fails to converge to the true posterior within acceptable computational budgets.
          In your real work
          In push notification optimisation and ad click-through modelling, teams use Bayesian multi-armed bandits with Thompson sampling to route traffic dynamically toward better-performing variants while continuously bounding the expected loss, which avoids the wasted traffic a rigid frequentist A/B test commits to up front.
          Flash cards
          What is Bayes' Rule for probability densities?
          Posterior equals Likelihood times Prior divided by Evidence..
          What is the base-rate fallacy?
          The tendency to judge the probability of a hypothesis based solely on test evidence while ignoring the prior prevalence (base rate) of the hypothesis in the population.
          What is a conjugate prior?
          A prior distribution family that, when combined with a specific likelihood function, yields a posterior distribution belonging to the exact same algebraic family.
          What does Laplace's rule of succession state?
          Starting from a flat uniform prior, the expected probability of success on trial n+1 after k successes is (wins plus 1) divided by (trials plus 2).
          How is a Bayesian credible interval interpreted?
          Given the observed data and prior assumptions, there is a high (like 95 percent) probability that the true parameter lies within the interval.
          What is Expected Loss in a Bayesian A/B test?
          The expected margin by which variant B is worse than variant A, computed as the expected loss.
