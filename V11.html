The story

Imagine you are walking through an ancient museum with your grandfather, and you spot a tiny silver bell resting inside a glass case. Your grandfather tells you that in the old days, castle guards rang this bell only when a fire dragon flew across the kingdom. Suddenly, the little silver bell rings with a clear DING!

Do you scream and run away from a fiery dragon?

A six-year-old might gasp in terror, but a wise detective pauses. First, how often do real dragons exist in the world? Dragons are almost completely imaginary—maybe one dragon sighting in a hundred years! Second, how often does a curious child bump into the glass case and make the bell jingle by accident? Children bump into the case every single afternoon!

Even if the magical bell is 99% accurate at detecting dragons, the bell is thousands of times more likely to have been bumped by an accidental elbow than to have spotted a real dragon. This trap is called the base-rate fallacy. You cannot judge what a signal means without knowing how rare the thing was before the signal ever arrived!

Say it simply

Bayesian reasoning treats probability as a quantified degree of belief that updates rationally in light of new evidence: $\mathbb{P}(\theta \mid D) \propto \mathbb{P}(D \mid \theta) \mathbb{P}(\theta)$. The base-rate fallacy occurs when prior probabilities are neglected, leading to gross misinterpretations of rare-event tests.

Unlike frequentist confidence intervals (which reflect the long-run coverage of a hypothetical sampling procedure), a Bayesian credible interval has the natural interpretation: given the prior and observed data, there is a 95% probability that the parameter lies within the interval. For binary observations, the Beta-Binomial conjugate family provides closed-form posterior updates: observing $k$ successes and $n-k$ failures updates prior $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ to posterior $\text{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)$.

Say it precisely

Let parameter $\theta \in \Theta$ have prior density $p(\theta)$. Given data $x \sim p(x \mid \theta)$: 1. Bayes' Theorem: $$p(\theta \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid \theta) p(\theta)}{\int_\Theta p(x \mid \theta') p(\theta') d\theta'} = \frac{p(x \mid \theta) p(\theta)}{p(x)}$$ 2. Beta-Binomial Conjugacy: For likelihood $k \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$ and prior $p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$: $$p \mid k \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)$$ 3. Laplace's Rule of Succession: Starting from uniform prior $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$, after $k$ successes in $n$ trials: $$\mathbb{E}[p \mid k] = \frac{k + 1}{n + 2}$$ 4. Bayesian A/B Comparison: For conversion rates $p_A \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_A, \beta_A)$ and $p_B \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_B, \beta_B)$: $$\mathbb{P}(p_B > p_A) = \int_0^1 f_B(y) F_A(y) dy$$ 5. Bayes Factor ($BF_{10}$): Ratio of marginal likelihoods comparing model $M_1$ to $M_0$: $$BF_{10} = \frac{p(x \mid M_1)}{p(x \mid M_0)} = \frac{\mathbb{P}(M_1 \mid x)}{\mathbb{P}(M_0 \mid x)} \cdot \frac{\mathbb{P}(M_0)}{\mathbb{P}(M_1)}$$

Why it's true

Why is the base-rate fallacy so prevalent, and why does Beta-Binomial conjugacy yield such mathematically elegant posterior updates?

Step 1 (The base-rate derivation): Let $D$ be the event of having a rare disease ($P(D) = 0.001$), and let $T$ be a positive test result. Suppose the test has $99\%$ sensitivity ($P(T \mid D) = 0.99$) and $95\%$ specificity ($P(T \mid \neg D) = 0.05$). By Bayes' theorem: $$P(D \mid T) = \frac{P(T \mid D) P(D)}{P(T \mid D) P(D) + P(T \mid \neg D) P(\neg D)}$$ Substituting the values: $$P(D \mid T) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{(0.99 \times 0.001) + (0.05 \times 0.999)} = \frac{0.00099}{0.00099 + 0.04995} \approx \frac{0.00099}{0.05094} \approx 1.94\%$$ Even though the test is 99% accurate on sick patients, a person who tests positive has less than a 2% chance of actually being sick! The vast ocean of healthy people produces 50 false alarms for every 1 true positive.

Step 2 (The conjugate algebraic kernel): The Beta density is $p(p) \propto p^{\alpha - 1} (1 - p)^{\beta - 1}$. The Binomial likelihood is $L(k \mid p) \propto p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}$. Multiplying prior by likelihood: $$p(p \mid k) \propto \left[ p^{\alpha - 1} (1 - p)^{\beta - 1} \right] \times \left[ p^k (1 - p)^{n - k} \right] = p^{(\alpha + k) - 1} (1 - p)^{(\beta + n - k) - 1}$$ The functional form is identical to the Beta prior! The parameters update by simple addition: $\alpha_{\text{post}} = \alpha + k$ and $\beta_{\text{post}} = \beta + n - k$. Prior parameters act as 'virtual pseudo-observations' from past experience.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "A Bayesian credible interval and a frequentist confidence interval mean the same thing." A 95% credible interval means there is a 95% posterior probability the parameter is inside the interval; a frequentist interval means 95% of repeated intervals will cover the fixed parameter.
            • 

          • "Bayesian statistics is unscientific because priors are subjective." Priors explicitly declare baseline assumptions and past evidence; sensitivity analyses verify that reasonable priors converge to the same posterior under data.
            • 

          • "Laplace's rule of succession is an ad-hoc hack." It is the exact mathematical expectation of a Bernoulli parameter under a flat uniform $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ prior.
            • 

          • "In A/B testing, you should deploy as soon as $\mathbb{P}(B > A) > 0.50$." Decisions must minimize Expected Loss; deploying with high uncertainty risks catastrophic downside even if B has a slight probability advantage.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Bayesian inference breaks down completely when dogmatic prior distributions assign zero probability density to regions containing the true parameter (Cromwell's rule), making Bayesian updating mathematically impossible regardless of how much empirical data is observed. In high-dimensional models with complex or non-convex posterior geometries, MCMC sampling suffers from pathological divergences and fails to converge to the true posterior within acceptable computational budgets.
          In your real work
          In push notification optimisation and ad click-through modelling, teams use Bayesian multi-armed bandits with Thompson sampling to route traffic dynamically toward better-performing variants while continuously bounding the expected loss, which avoids the wasted traffic a rigid frequentist A/B test commits to up front.
          Flash cards
          What is Bayes' Rule for probability densities?
          $p(\theta \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid \theta) p(\theta)}{p(D)} = \frac{p(D \mid \theta) p(\theta)}{\int p(D \mid \theta') p(\theta') d\theta'}$.
          What is the base-rate fallacy?
          The tendency to judge the probability of a hypothesis based solely on test evidence while ignoring the prior prevalence (base rate) of the hypothesis in the population.
          What is a conjugate prior?
          A prior distribution family that, when combined with a specific likelihood function, yields a posterior distribution belonging to the exact same algebraic family.
          What does Laplace's rule of succession state?
          Starting from a flat uniform prior, the expected probability of success on trial $n+1$ after $k$ successes is $\frac{k + 1}{n + 2}$.
          How is a Bayesian credible interval interpreted?
          Given the observed data and prior assumptions, there is a $(1-\alpha)$ probability that the true parameter lies within the interval.
          What is Expected Loss in a Bayesian A/B test?
          The expected margin by which variant B is worse than variant A, computed as $\mathbb{E}[\max(p_A - p_B, 0)]$.
          Stop when
          You can solve the false-positive paradox on paper without notes, update a Beta-Binomial posterior in your head, and implement a Bayesian Expected Loss decision engine.
