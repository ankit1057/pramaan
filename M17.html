The story

Dadi gives Vikram a bar of chocolate. It is scored into a grid, six squares across and four squares down — twenty-four little squares in all.

"You may eat it," she says, "but you must separate every square from every other square. And each time you snap, you must snap one piece along a straight scored line, all the way across. No cheating, no biting."

Vikram wants to be clever. He plans. Should he snap all the long lines first? Should he work from a corner? He is sure some orders are faster than others, and he spends ten minutes planning the best route.

His sister Priya walks past, picks up the bar, and snaps it to bits in whatever order her hands land on. She counts her snaps: twenty-three.

Vikram executes his beautiful plan. Twenty-three.

He tries again, deliberately doing the stupidest possible order. Twenty-three.

He is annoyed, and then he sees it, and being annoyed is replaced by something better. Every single snap turns one piece into two. So every snap adds exactly one to the number of pieces. He starts with one piece and must end with twenty-four. There is nothing to plan: it will always be twenty-three snaps, for any bar, in any order, forever.

Here is the surprise: he did not find the answer by trying hard. He found a quantity that never changes its behaviour no matter what you do, and that one observation settled infinitely many strategies at once. That is what a proof is.

Say it simply

Logic is the study of which arguments are valid — which conclusions really do follow from which premises, regardless of what the statements are about.

The raw material is the proposition: a statement that is definitely true or definitely false. Propositions combine with connectives — and, or, not, implies — and a truth table settles exactly what each combination means.

The one connective that causes trouble is implies. "If it rains, the match is cancelled" says nothing at all about what happens when it does not rain, and swapping it round to "if the match is cancelled, it rained" is a different claim which may well be false.

A proof is a finite chain of steps, each justified, leading from things already accepted to the thing claimed. The payoff is Vikram's: a proof settles infinitely many cases at once, which no amount of testing can ever do.

Say it precisely

Propositions and connectives

A proposition is a declarative statement with a definite truth value. The connectives, with $T$ for true and $F$ for false:

$p$ $q$ $\neg p$ $p \wedge q$ $p \vee q$ $p \to q$ $p \leftrightarrow q$
T T F T T T T
T F F F T F F
F T T F T T F
F F T F F T T

Read the implication column carefully. $p\to q$ is false in exactly one situation: a true premise with a false conclusion. When $p$ is false the implication is vacuously true. This is a definition, chosen because it makes the logic work, and it is the single most common source of confusion in the subject.

A tautology is true under every assignment; a contradiction is false under every assignment. Two formulas are logically equivalent, written $\equiv$, when they have identical truth tables.

Key equivalences:

$$p\to q \;\equiv\; \neg p \vee q \;\equiv\; \neg q \to \neg p$$ $$\neg(p\wedge q)\equiv \neg p \vee \neg q, \qquad \neg(p\vee q)\equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q$$

Converse, inverse, contrapositive

Given $p \to q$:

  • Converse: $q \to p$. Not equivalent.
  • Inverse: $\neg p \to \neg q$. Not equivalent.
  • Contrapositive: $\neg q \to \neg p$. Equivalent, always.

The contrapositive being equivalent is what licenses proof by contraposition. The converse being inequivalent is what makes "the converse is also true" a separate theorem needing its own proof, and it is why $\iff$ statements must be proved in both directions.

Quantifiers

$\forall x\, P(x)$ says $P$ holds for every $x$ in the domain; $\exists x\, P(x)$ says it holds for at least one. Negation swaps them and pushes inward:

$$\neg\forall x\, P(x) \equiv \exists x\, \neg P(x), \qquad \neg \exists x\, P(x) \equiv \forall x\, \neg P(x)$$

Order matters and is not symmetric: $\forall x\exists y$ is a far weaker claim than $\exists y\forall x$. "Every person has a mother" is true; "there is one mother of every person" is false.

Rules of inference

$$\textbf{Modus ponens:}\quad \frac{p\to q,\quad p}{\therefore q} \qquad\qquad \textbf{Modus tollens:}\quad \frac{p\to q,\quad \neg q}{\therefore \neg p}$$

Also hypothetical syllogism ($p\to q$, $q\to r$, therefore $p\to r$) and disjunctive syllogism ($p\vee q$, $\neg p$, therefore $q$). The two classic fallacies are affirming the consequent (from $p\to q$ and $q$, concluding $p$) and denying the antecedent (from $p\to q$ and $\neg p$, concluding $\neg q$). Both are invalid, and both are extremely common in informal argument.

Methods of proof

  • Direct: assume $p$, derive $q$.
  • Contrapositive: assume $\neg q$, derive $\neg p$. Equivalent to the direct proof, and often much easier.
  • Contradiction: assume $p \wedge \neg q$, derive an absurdity.
  • Cases: split the domain into exhaustive cases and prove each.
  • Construction: to prove $\exists x\, P(x)$, exhibit one.
  • Counterexample: to disprove $\forall x\, P(x)$, exhibit one $x$ with $\neg P(x)$. One is enough, and nothing less will do.

Induction

Principle of mathematical induction. If $P(n_0)$ holds, and $P(k)\to P(k+1)$ for every $k \ge n_0$, then $P(n)$ holds for all $n \ge n_0$.

Strong induction assumes $P(n_0), \dots, P(k)$ all hold to prove $P(k+1)$. It is logically equivalent to ordinary induction but far more convenient when the step reaches back further than one.

Well-ordering principle. Every non-empty set of non-negative integers has a least element. This is equivalent to induction, and is what makes "take the smallest counterexample" a legitimate move.

Structural induction generalises to recursively defined objects. For a list: prove it for the empty list, then assume it for a list and prove it for one element consed on. For a binary tree: prove it for the empty tree, then assume it for both subtrees and prove it for the node. The base cases of the recursive definition become the base cases of the proof.

Invariants. An invariant is a property preserved by every permitted move. To show a goal state is unreachable, find an invariant true at the start and false at the goal. Vikram's "each snap adds exactly one piece" is an invariant of the change; the chessboard parity argument below is an invariant of the state.

A pinch of history

Formal study of inference begins with Aristotle's syllogistic, and independently in India with the Nyaya school, whose five-membered argument form appears in the Nyaya Sutras attributed to Gautama (roughly the early centuries CE); the Navya-Nyaya tradition, especially Gangesa in the fourteenth century, developed a sophisticated technical vocabulary for quantification and relations.

The modern algebraic turn is George Boole's The Laws of Thought (1854). Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) introduced quantifiers and the first real formal system. Giuseppe Peano axiomatised arithmetic in 1889, with induction as an axiom. And in 1931 Kurt Gödel proved that any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove — which put a permanent ceiling on the whole programme.

Why it's true

Result 1: the contrapositive is equivalent to the implication. Build the truth table.

  1. $p\to q$ is false only when $p$ is true and $q$ false.
  2. $\neg q \to \neg p$ is false only when $\neg q$ is true and $\neg p$ false — that is, when $q$ is false and $p$ is true.
  3. Those are the same row. Since the two formulas are false on exactly the same assignment, they are true on exactly the same assignments.
  4. Hence they are logically equivalent, and proving one proves the other.

Contrast the converse $q\to p$, which is false when $q$ is true and $p$ false — a different row. One example settles it: "if $x = 2$ then $x^2 = 4$" is true, its converse is false at $x=-2$.

Result 2: induction is valid, via well-ordering.

  1. Suppose $P(n_0)$ holds and $P(k)\to P(k+1)$ for all $k\ge n_0$, yet $P(n)$ fails for some $n \ge n_0$.
  2. Let $S$ be the set of integers $n\ge n_0$ for which $P(n)$ is false. By assumption $S$ is non-empty.
  3. By well-ordering, $S$ has a least element $m$.
  4. $m \neq n_0$, because $P(n_0)$ holds. So $m > n_0$ and $m - 1 \ge n_0$.
  5. The non-obvious step: since $m$ is the least element of $S$, the number $m-1$ is not in $S$, so $P(m-1)$ is true.
  6. But the inductive step applied to $k = m-1$ gives $P(m)$, contradicting $m\in S$.
  7. So $S$ is empty: $P(n)$ holds for all $n\ge n_0$.

Induction is therefore not an article of faith; it follows from the fact that you cannot descend forever through the non-negative integers.

Result 3: a mutilated chessboard cannot be tiled by dominoes. Remove two diagonally opposite corners from an $8\times 8$ board, leaving 62 squares. Can 31 dominoes, each covering two adjacent squares, tile it?

  1. Colour the board alternately black and white, as chessboards are.
  2. Any domino covers two adjacent squares, and adjacent squares always have opposite colours. So every domino covers exactly one black and one white square. This is the invariant.
  3. Therefore 31 dominoes cover exactly 31 black and 31 white squares.
  4. But two diagonally opposite corners share a colour. Removing them leaves 32 of one colour and 30 of the other.
  5. $32 \neq 31$. No tiling exists.

Notice what the argument does not do: it never tries a single arrangement. Searching would take essentially forever and could only ever report failure, never impossibility. The invariant settles every arrangement at once — Vikram's lesson again.

Result 4: structural induction on binary trees. Claim: a binary tree with $n$ internal nodes (each having exactly two children) has $n+1$ leaves.

  1. Base case. A single leaf has $n = 0$ internal nodes and $1 = 0+1$ leaves.
  2. Inductive step. Take a tree whose root is internal with subtrees $L$ and $R$, having $n_L, n_R$ internal nodes and, by the inductive hypothesis, $n_L+1$ and $n_R+1$ leaves.
  3. The whole tree has $n = n_L+n_R+1$ internal nodes, counting the root.
  4. Its leaves are exactly the leaves of $L$ and of $R$: $(n_L+1)+(n_R+1) = n_L+n_R+2 = n+1$.
  5. The claim holds for the whole tree, so by structural induction it holds for all such trees.

Oracle lab

The oracle checks logical equivalence two genuinely independent ways.

The first is semantic: enumerate every assignment of truth values and compare the two formulas row by row. This is the definition of equivalence, done by brute force.

The second is syntactic: rewrite the formula using a different construction entirely — building $p\to q$ as $\neg p \vee q$, and building the contrapositive by swapping and negating — and then evaluate. The two routes share no step: one walks a table, the other rebuilds the expression.

The demo also checks the quantifier negation laws over finite domains, and exercises the two valid inference rules against the two classic fallacies. The mutant asserts that the converse is equivalent to the implication, which is the error that underlies "affirming the consequent" and a great deal of bad reasoning in the wild.

Experiment lab
Two predictions, both about proofs settling infinitely many cases.


First, Vikram's invariant. Theory says that breaking a chocolate bar into its individual squares takes exactly (number of squares − 1) snaps, whatever order you choose. A strategy-dependent answer would refute it. So we let a random strategy loose, thousands of times, on many bar sizes, and count.


Second, the mutilated chessboard. Theory says no tiling exists, by a colouring invariant. We cannot enumerate all tilings of a 62-square board, but we can check the invariant's premise exhaustively — that every domino placement covers one square of each colour — and we can verify the colour counts that make the conclusion follow.

Where reality disagrees: the chocolate experiment is a genuine measurement and it came back as a single value every time, which is exactly what the invariant predicts — but note that a thousand agreeing trials is still not a proof. It is consistent with the theorem; it does not establish it. The one-line argument ("each snap adds one piece") does establish it, for every bar and every strategy, including the ones we never ran. The chessboard half makes the point from the other side: we verified the premise exhaustively and then let the proof do the work, because enumerating the tilings is not feasible. Testing can refute a universal claim with one counterexample and can never confirm one. That asymmetry is why this module exists.

            Misconceptions
              

            • "If the premise is false, the implication is false." It is true, vacuously. "All my unicorns are purple" is true because I have no unicorns. This convention makes $\forall$ over an empty set behave correctly, and it is why a loop that never runs satisfies its postcondition.
              • 

            • "Proving the converse proves the theorem." It proves a different theorem. Only the contrapositive is equivalent. Mixing them up is affirming the consequent, which is invalid.
              • 

            • "A counterexample is just one case; the pattern still mostly holds." A single counterexample destroys a universal claim completely. $n^2+n+41$ is prime for $n = 0$ through $39$ and composite at $40$; thirty-nine successes are worth nothing against one failure.
              • 

            • "The inductive step alone proves the statement." Without a base case you can "prove" nonsense: $n = n+1$ survives any step you like and is false everywhere. Both halves are needed, and the base must be at the right starting value.
              • 

            • "Testing many cases is almost a proof." It is categorically not. Vikram's thousand successful trials do not cover the strategies he did not try; the invariant covers all of them in one line. Testing can only refute.
              • 

            • "Strong induction is a stronger axiom than ordinary induction." They are logically equivalent; strong induction is merely more convenient when the step needs more than the immediately preceding case.
              • 

            • "$\forall x \exists y$ and $\exists y \forall x$ say the same thing in a different order." They do not. The second is much stronger: it demands one $y$ that works for every $x$.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Classical propositional logic assumes every proposition is definitely true or definitely false — the law of the excluded middle. Intuitionistic logic rejects it, and with it proof by contradiction for existence claims: there, to show something exists you must construct it. That is not a quibble; it is the logic underlying type theory and proof assistants, where a proof of existence must carry an algorithm.
            

            Material implication is a deliberately crude model of "if". It makes "if the moon is cheese then 2+2=4" true, which no English speaker accepts. Relevance logics and counterfactual conditionals try to do better, at the cost of much more machinery.
            

            Induction needs a well-founded ordering. Over the non-negative integers it works because you cannot descend forever. Over the rationals, or the reals, there is no "next" element and ordinary induction is simply unavailable; you need transfinite induction, or continuity arguments, or an entirely different approach.
            

            And Gödel set the ceiling. Any consistent formal system strong enough to express arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, and cannot prove its own consistency. Proof is immensely powerful, and it is not unlimited.
            In your real work
            Material implication and De Morgan are the daily arithmetic of conditionals: simplifying a nested if, inverting a guard clause, or writing the negation of a compound filter. The vacuous-truth rule is why all([]) is True, and why a validation loop over an empty list passes — a behaviour that surprises people until they have seen this truth table.
            

            Invariants are the practical heart of the module. A loop invariant is what makes a loop correct rather than merely tested; a class invariant is what a constructor establishes and every method must preserve; a database constraint is an invariant the storage engine enforces for you. Reasoning about concurrency is almost entirely invariant reasoning, because the interleavings cannot be enumerated — the same situation as the chessboard. And structural induction is how you reason about recursive data: a proof over a tree has exactly the shape of the function that walks it.
            Flash cards
            When is the implication p -> q false?
            Only when p is true and q is false. In every other case it is true, including vacuously when p is false.
            Which of converse, inverse and contrapositive is equivalent to the original implication?
            Only the contrapositive, not q -> not p. The converse and inverse are equivalent to each other but not to the original.
            How do you negate "for all x, P(x)"?
            "There exists an x with not P(x)". The quantifier flips and the negation moves inside.
            State modus ponens and modus tollens, and name the two fallacies that mimic them.
            From p->q and p conclude q; from p->q and not q conclude not p. The fallacies are affirming the consequent (p->q, q, so p) and denying the antecedent (p->q, not p, so not q).
            Why does induction need both a base case and an inductive step?
            The step only propagates truth; without a true starting point it propagates nothing. "n = n+1" satisfies a step and is false everywhere.
            What is an invariant and why is it so powerful?
            A property preserved by every permitted move. It settles every reachable state at once, including the infinitely many you could never enumerate.
            How does the colouring argument show the mutilated chessboard cannot be tiled?
            Every domino covers one square of each colour, so a tiling needs equal counts. Removing two same-coloured corners leaves 32 and 30, so no tiling exists.
            How many counterexamples are needed to refute a universal statement?
            Exactly one.
            What does structural induction look like for a binary tree?
            Prove the property for the empty tree or a leaf, then assume it for both subtrees and prove it for a node built from them.
            Why is "I tested a thousand cases" not a proof?
            Because a universal claim covers cases you did not test. Testing can refute a claim with one counterexample but can never confirm one.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why the chocolate bar always takes the same number of snaps, you can state the truth table for implication from memory and justify the vacuous rows, you can tell converse from contrapositive without hesitating and say which one your proof is allowed to use, you can prove the validity of induction from well-ordering, you can find the invariant in a puzzle rather than searching the state space, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can say precisely why testing is not proof.
