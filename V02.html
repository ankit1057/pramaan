The story

Imagine you are at an ice cream shop with your little sister, and she says, "My strawberry ice cream is ten degrees deliciouser than your chocolate ice cream!" You smile because you know that deliciousness does not come with tick marks on a ruler. Can you measure happiness with a wooden meter stick? What if a judge at a race simply writes down who came First, Second, and Third, but forgets to bring a stopwatch?

A runner who came First was certainly faster than the runner who came Second, but you have no idea whether they won by half an inch or by three whole city blocks. A number on a piece of paper does not always mean what you think it means. When we turn the real world into numbers, we have to know what kind of number we created. Are we just putting names on team jerseys, or putting runners in order, or measuring the temperature with equal tick marks, or counting coins in a piggy bank where zero coins really means an empty pocket? If you treat a jersey number like a pile of coins, your math becomes complete nonsense.

Say it simply

Measurement is the operational mapping of real-world phenomena into numbers. Numbers exist on four distinct measurement scales defined by Stanley Smith Stevens: nominal (categories/labels), ordinal (ordered ranks without uniform distance), interval (uniform distances without a true physical zero, like Celsius), and ratio (uniform distances with a true physical zero, like Kelvin or mass).

Applying arithmetic operations suited for ratio scales to ordinal or nominal data produces mathematical artifacts. A metric is valid only if it measures the intended construct (construct validity), and reliable only if repeated independent measurements yield consistent results (test-retest and inter-rater reliability). Goodhart's law warns that whenever a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

Say it precisely

Stevens' typology formalises measurement as a homomorphism from an empirical relational structure $(A, R)$ to a numerical relational structure $(B, S)$.

  1. Nominal Scale: Permissible transformations are any 1-to-1 mappings $f(x)$. Statistics: mode, contingency tables, chi-square. Distance and ordering are meaningless.
  2. Ordinal Scale: Permissible transformations are strictly monotonic increasing functions $f(x)$ where $x > y \iff f(x) > f(y)$. Statistics: median, percentiles, rank correlation (Spearman $ ho$, Kendall $ au$). Means and standard deviations are meaningless.
  3. Interval Scale: Permissible transformations are positive affine transforms $f(x) = lpha x + eta$ with $lpha > 0$. Differences have meaning ($x_1 - x_2 = x_3 - x_4$), but ratios $x_1 / x_2$ do not, because the zero point is arbitrary (e.g., $20^\circ ext{C}$ is not twice as hot as $10^\circ ext{C}$).
  4. Ratio Scale: Permissible transformations are scalar multiplications $f(x) = lpha x$ with $lpha > 0$. A true physical zero exists ($0$ indicates absence of quantity). Ratios $x_1 / x_2$ are meaningful.
  5. Errors: Observed value $X = T + E_{ ext{sys}} + E_{ ext{rand}}$, where $T$ is true score, $E_{ ext{sys}}$ is systematic bias ($\mathbb{E}[E_{ ext{sys}}] e 0$), and $E_{ ext{rand}}$ is random error ($\mathbb{E}[E_{ ext{rand}}] = 0$). Accuracy measures proximity to $T$ (minimising $|E_{ ext{sys}}| + \sigma_{E}$); precision measures repeatability (minimising $\sigma_{E}$).

Why it's true

Why does computing the mean of Likert ratings create dangerous artifacts? Consider a standard 5-point Likert questionnaire: 1=Strongly Disagree, 2=Disagree, 3=Neutral, 4=Agree, 5=Strongly Agree.

Step 1 (The psychological distance): The psychological difference between "Strongly Disagree" and "Disagree" is rarely equal to the difference between "Neutral" and "Agree". Mathematically, the mapping from internal conviction $\psi$ to response $r \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ is strictly monotonic, but non-linear: $r = g(\psi)$ where $g'(\psi) > 0$, but differences $\Delta g$ are arbitrary.

Step 2 (Invariance under permissible transformation): For an ordinal scale, any monotonic transform $h(r)$ preserves the ordering. If $h(1)=1, h(2)=2, h(3)=3, h(4)=10, h(5)=11$, the rank order of all candidates is unchanged. However, the arithmetic mean $ar{r}$ changes radically under $h$. If Group A has ratings $(3, 3, 3)$ with mean $3.0$ and Group B has $(1, 2, 5)$ with mean $2.67$, Group A appears superior. Under transform $h$, Group A has mean $3.0$ and Group B has $(1 + 2 + 11)/3 = 4.67$, reversing the ranking!

Step 3 (Dimensional Analysis): Physical laws must be dimensionally homogeneous. By the Buckingham $\pi$ theorem, any physically meaningful equation $f(q_1, \dots, q_n) = 0$ can be recast in terms of dimensionless products. If units are mixed without conversion factors, dimensional analysis fails and calculations become invalid.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "Likert ratings can be averaged because they are numbers 1 through 5." Likert scale data is strictly ordinal; intervals between subjective sentiments are non-uniform and arbitrary.
            • 

          • "High precision implies high accuracy." A scale can report values to six decimal places (high precision) while suffering from a 20% calibration bias (low accuracy).
            • 

          • "Goodhart's law only applies to economics and finance." It applies equally to software engineering metrics like lines of code, test coverage percentage, and commit counts.
            • 

          • "If a metric is mathematically elegant, it possesses construct validity." A formula can be mathematically flawless yet measure a proxy that has zero correlation with the underlying real-world construct.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Stevens' typology breaks down when dealing with pragmatic pseudo-interval measurements in psychometrics where Rasch modelling or Item Response Theory (IRT) transforms ordinal response patterns into linear logit scales on an interval continuum. In physical computing, sensor quantisation noise and thermal drift prevent physical sensors from achieving pure theoretical ratio measurements at micro-scales.
          In your real work
          In mobile analytics — user satisfaction surveys, battery drain telemetry — naive pipelines average 1-to-5 satisfaction stars across releases. That produces phantom regressions or improvements caused entirely by a monotonic shift in who chose to respond, rather than by any real change in app stability.
          Flash cards
          What are the four measurement scales defined by S.S. Stevens?
          Nominal (labels), Ordinal (ranks), Interval (equal intervals, arbitrary zero), and Ratio (equal intervals, true zero).
          Why is calculating the arithmetic mean of Likert ratings invalid?
          Because Likert items are ordinal; arithmetic means assume equal intervals between categories, which is psychologically false and mathematically non-invariant under monotonic transformation.
          What is the difference between systematic error and random error?
          Systematic error is repeatable bias that shifts the mean away from truth; random error fluctuates symmetrically around the mean with expected value zero.
          State Goodhart's Law.
          When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure, because agents optimize the proxy metric rather than the underlying construct.
          What is construct validity?
          The degree to which a test or operational metric genuinely measures the theoretical construct it claims to measure.
          What does the Buckingham Pi theorem guarantee in dimensional analysis?
          Any physically meaningful equation involving $n$ physical variables can be rewritten in terms of $n - k$ independent dimensionless parameters, where $k$ is the number of fundamental dimensions.
          Stop when
          You can explain to a junior engineer why averaging customer survey stars is statistically unsound, pass the boss exercise on dimensional analysis, and identify Goodhart gaming in team KPIs.
