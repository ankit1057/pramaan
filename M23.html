The story

Sunil's class is given a square sheet of card, thirty centimetres on a side, and told to make the biggest open box they can. Cut an equal square from each corner, fold the flaps up, tape the sides. Whoever's box holds the most sand wins.

Rishi cuts tiny corners, one centimetre. His box is wide and flat, like a tray. It holds some sand, not much — the walls are too short.

Priya cuts huge corners, fourteen centimetres. Her box has tall walls and a base barely two centimetres square. It holds almost nothing.

Sunil realises something that surprises him. Both extremes are bad. Cut nothing and there are no walls; cut almost half and there is no floor. The best box is somewhere in between, and there is no reason it should be at a nice round number.

He tries five centimetres. Then six. Then four. The volumes go up, then down. Somewhere between four and six the box is at its best, and right at that point the volume briefly stops changing — a little more cut, a little less cut, barely any difference.

Here is the surprise: the best answer is exactly where the rate of change is zero. Not because zero is special, but because if the volume were still rising you could cut more, and if it were falling you should have cut less. Standing still is the signature of the top of the hill, and that single observation turns "try everything" into "solve one equation".

Say it simply

The derivative is a rate, and once you can compute rates you can answer a great many practical questions.

Where is a function rising or falling? Wherever $f' > 0$ or $f' < 0$.

Where is it at its highest or lowest? At a critical point where $f'=0$ or $f'$ fails to exist — or at an endpoint. The two derivative tests tell you which critical points are peaks and which are valleys.

What does the function look like near a point? Almost exactly like its tangent line. That is linear approximation, and it is the idea behind almost every numerical method.

How do you solve $f(x)=0$? Follow the tangent to where it crosses the axis, and repeat. That is Newton's method, and it roughly doubles the number of correct digits each step.

The one-line takeaway: optimisation becomes equation-solving, and equation-solving becomes repeated linear approximation.

Say it precisely

Rates and tangents

If $y=f(x)$ then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is the rate of change of $y$ with respect to $x$. Related rates chain through: if two quantities are linked and both vary with time, $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{dA}{dr}\cdot\frac{dr}{dt}$.

At $(a, f(a))$ the tangent has slope $f'(a)$, so

$$y - f(a) = f'(a)(x-a)$$

and the normal, perpendicular to it, has slope $-\frac{1}{f'(a)}$ when $f'(a)\neq0$:

$$y - f(a) = -\frac{1}{f'(a)}(x-a)$$

Linear approximation and differentials

$$f(a+h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)h$$

The differential is $dy = f'(x)\,dx$. The error of this approximation is $O(h^2)$ — by Taylor's theorem it is $\frac{f''(\xi)}{2}h^2$ for some $\xi$ between $a$ and $a+h$ — so halving $h$ quarters the error.

Increasing, decreasing, and the derivative tests

On an interval, $f$ is strictly increasing if $f'>0$ throughout, and strictly decreasing if $f'<0$. (The converse is slightly weaker: an increasing function has $f'\ge0$, and $x^3$ shows that $f'$ may vanish at an isolated point without breaking strict increase.)

A critical point is where $f'(c)=0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist.

First derivative test. At a critical point $c$: if $f'$ changes $+\to-$ there, $c$ is a local maximum; if $-\to+$, a local minimum; if it does not change sign, neither.

Second derivative test. If $f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)<0$ then $c$ is a local maximum; if $f''(c)>0$, a local minimum. If $f''(c)=0$ the test is inconclusive — $x^3$, $x^4$ and $-x^4$ all have $f'=f''=0$ at the origin and behave completely differently.

Concavity and inflexion

$f$ is convex (concave up) on an interval when $f''\ge0$, and concave (concave down) when $f''\le0$. Equivalently, $f$ is convex when the chord between any two points lies on or above the graph:

$$f\left(tx_1+(1-t)x_2\right) \le t f(x_1)+(1-t)f(x_2), \qquad t\in[0,1]$$

A point of inflexion is where concavity changes. Requiring $f''=0$ is necessary but not sufficient: $x^4$ has $f''(0)=0$ and no inflexion there, because the sign of $f''$ does not change.

Absolute extrema on a closed interval

On a closed bounded interval $[a,b]$ a continuous function attains an absolute maximum and minimum (the Extreme Value Theorem). To find them:

  1. Find all critical points in $(a,b)$.
  2. Evaluate $f$ at those points and at both endpoints.
  3. The largest value is the absolute maximum, the smallest the absolute minimum.

Step 2's endpoints are the step people forget, and it is why "set the derivative to zero" alone is not an algorithm.

Newton's method

To solve $f(x)=0$, approximate $f$ by its tangent at the current guess and take the tangent's root:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$$

Convergence. If $f$ is twice continuously differentiable, $f(r)=0$, $f'(r)\neq0$ and $x_0$ is close enough to $r$, then

$$|x_{n+1}-r| \approx \frac{|f''(r)|}{2|f'(r)|}\,|x_n-r|^2$$

This is quadratic convergence: the error squares each step, so the number of correct digits roughly doubles. Bisection, by contrast, is linear — it gains a fixed factor of 2 per step, about one decimal digit every three and a bit steps.

Newton fails or misbehaves when $f'(x_n)$ is near zero, when the root is multiple (then convergence degrades to linear), or when the starting guess is poor — it can cycle, or run off to infinity.

A pinch of history

Fermat, around 1636, found maxima and minima by a method of "adequality" that is recognisably setting a difference quotient to zero, predating Newton and Leibniz. Newton described his root-finding method around 1669 (published 1711) for polynomials; Joseph Raphson gave the iterative form in 1690, which is why it is often the Newton-Raphson method, and Thomas Simpson in 1740 stated it for general functions using calculus notation.

Bhaskara II, in the twelfth century, had already stated that a quantity's rate of change vanishes at its maximum, in the context of planetary motion, and gave an instance of what we now call Rolle's theorem.

The extension of these ideas — that optimisation is about derivatives vanishing, and that convexity is what makes the answer unique — is the whole of M36.

Why it's true

Result 1: at an interior maximum the derivative vanishes (Fermat's theorem).

  1. Let $f$ have a local maximum at an interior point $c$ and suppose $f'(c)$ exists.
  2. For small $h>0$, $f(c+h)\le f(c)$, so $\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}\le 0$. Letting $h\to0^+$ gives $f'(c)\le0$.
  3. For small $h<0$, $f(c+h)\le f(c)$, but now dividing by the negative $h$ flips the inequality: $\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}\ge0$. Letting $h\to0^-$ gives $f'(c)\ge0$.
  4. Both hold, so $f'(c)=0$.

The non-obvious step is 3, where dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality — the same rule as M20. Note what the theorem does not say: it does not say $f'(c)=0$ implies a maximum ($x^3$ at 0), and it says nothing at endpoints, where a maximum can occur with a non-zero derivative. Both omissions are exactly the mistakes people make.

Result 2: $f'>0$ on an interval implies $f$ is increasing. Take $x_1<x_2$ in the interval. By the Mean Value Theorem of M22 there is a $c$ between them with

$$f(x_2)-f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2-x_1)$$

The right side is a positive number times a positive number, so $f(x_2)>f(x_1)$. This is the MVT earning its keep: a statement about the derivative at one unknown point becomes a statement about the function everywhere.

Result 3: the second derivative test. If $f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)<0$, then $f'$ is strictly decreasing near $c$. Since $f'(c)=0$, this means $f'>0$ just to the left and $f'<0$ just to the right. By Result 2, $f$ rises up to $c$ and falls after it, so $c$ is a local maximum. The test is silent when $f''(c)=0$ precisely because $f'$ might then be doing anything.

Result 4: Newton's method converges quadratically.

  1. Let $r$ be the root and $e_n = x_n - r$ the error.
  2. Taylor-expand $f$ about $x_n$ and evaluate at $r$: $0 = f(r) = f(x_n) - e_nf'(x_n) + \frac{e_n^2}{2}f''(\xi)$ for some $\xi$ between $r$ and $x_n$.
  3. Divide by $f'(x_n)$ and rearrange: $x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} - r = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2f'(x_n)}e_n^2$.
  4. The left side is exactly $x_{n+1}-r = e_{n+1}$.
  5. So $e_{n+1} = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2f'(x_n)}e_n^2$. Near a simple root $f'(x_n)\to f'(r)\neq0$, so the constant settles and the error squares.

Step 2 is where the whole result lives: Newton's step is exactly the linear part of the Taylor expansion, so the error it leaves behind is exactly the quadratic remainder.

Result 5: convexity from the second derivative. If $f''\ge0$ then $f'$ is non-decreasing. For $x_1<x_2$ and the chord between them, the MVT applied on each side of any interior point shows the function cannot rise above the chord — a slope that only ever increases cannot overshoot and come back. This is the one-dimensional case of the convexity that makes M36's optimisation tractable.

Oracle lab

The oracle finds a maximum two genuinely independent ways.

The calculus route differentiates, solves $f'(x)=0$ on the interval, evaluates $f$ at those critical points and at both endpoints, and takes the largest. It uses the theory.

The search route knows no calculus: it evaluates $f$ at a very fine grid of points across the interval and reports the largest value it saw.

If the theory were wrong — if maxima could hide somewhere other than critical points and endpoints — the grid would find something the calculus missed. The mutant below checks only the critical points and forgets the endpoints, which is the classic error and is caught the moment the maximum sits at a boundary.

Experiment lab
Theory makes two quantitative predictions worth measuring.


First, linear approximation. $f(a+h)\approx f(a)+f'(a)h$ should have error proportional to $h^2$, so halving $h$ quarters the error. That is testable.


Second, Newton's quadratic convergence. The error should square each step, which means the number of correct decimal digits roughly doubles per iteration. Bisection, by contrast, should gain a constant amount per step. We run both on the same problem and count digits.

Where reality disagrees: Newton stops improving once it reaches machine precision — the digit count flattens at about 16 and the "doubling" prediction simply runs out of room, which is why the assertion above allows for that. More interestingly, quadratic convergence is a local promise. Start Newton far away, or near a point where $f'$ is small, and it can overshoot wildly or cycle forever; on $x^{1/3}$ it diverges from any starting point. And at a multiple root, where $f'(r)=0$ too, the derivation's assumption $f'(r)\neq0$ fails and convergence drops to linear. Bisection is far slower and never fails if it starts with a sign change — the usual trade: speed against robustness. Production root-finders such as Brent's method combine both.

def test_convergence_order_is_two_for_a_simple_root():
    from math import sqrt
    f = lambda x: x * x - 2
    df = lambda x: 2 * x
    its = newton(f, df, 1.5, iterations=6, tol=0.0)
    errors = [abs(x - sqrt(2)) for x in its]
    order = convergence_order([e for e in errors if e > 1e-15])
    assert 1.7 < order < 2.3, f"expected order near 2, got {order}"
    # bisection is first order
    bs = bisection(f, 1.0, 2.0, 40)
    berr = [abs(x - sqrt(2)) for x in bs[-6:]]
    border = convergence_order([e for e in berr if e > 1e-15])
    assert border < 1.5, f"bisection must be roughly linear, got {border}"

            Misconceptions
              

            • "Setting $f'=0$ finds the maximum." It finds critical points, which may be maxima, minima or neither, and it misses endpoints entirely. On a closed interval the maximum may well be at a boundary with a non-zero derivative — the demo's mutant failed on exactly this.
              • 

            • "$f''(c)=0$ means an inflexion point." It is necessary, not sufficient. $x^4$ has $f''(0)=0$ and is convex on both sides, so no inflexion. The sign must change.
              • 

            • "The second derivative test always works." When $f''(c)=0$ it says nothing, and $x^3$, $x^4$ and $-x^4$ are three different behaviours with identical first and second derivatives at the origin. Fall back to the first derivative test.
              • 

            • "Increasing means $f'>0$ everywhere." $x^3$ is strictly increasing with $f'(0)=0$. The clean statement is: $f'>0$ implies strictly increasing, and strictly increasing implies $f'\ge0$.
              • 

            • "Newton's method always converges." It converges fast when started close enough to a simple root. It can cycle, diverge, or crash on a zero derivative, and on $x^{1/3}$ it diverges from every starting point.
              • 

            • "Newton is simply better than bisection." It is faster and less reliable. Bisection cannot fail once it has a sign change. Real libraries use hybrids such as Brent's method for exactly this reason.
              • 

            • "A local maximum is the maximum." Only for a convex problem (or concave, for maxima). In general there can be many local optima, and that distinction is the entire difficulty of M36.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            The Extreme Value Theorem needs a closed and bounded interval and a continuous function. Drop any hypothesis and the maximum may not exist: $\frac1x$ on $(0,1]$ is unbounded, and $x$ on $(0,1)$ never attains its supremum.
            

            Critical-point analysis assumes differentiability. A function with a corner can have its extremum exactly there, where $f'$ does not exist — so "where $f'=0$ or $f'$ is undefined" is the correct phrase, and dropping the second clause loses $|x|$'s minimum.
            

            Numerically, finding critical points by scanning for sign changes misses roots of even multiplicity, where $f'$ touches zero without crossing, and misses any pair of roots closer together than the grid spacing. That is a real limitation of the exercise code, not a detail.
            

            Newton's quadratic convergence is local and assumes a simple root. At a multiple root it degrades to linear; near a point where $f'$ is small it takes enormous steps; and in finite precision the iteration stalls once the step is comparable to the rounding error in $f$, which is why the implementation stops on a small step rather than a small residual.
            

            Finally, all of this is one-dimensional. In several variables "set the derivative to zero" becomes "set the gradient to zero", the second derivative becomes the Hessian matrix, and a critical point can be a saddle — neither a maximum nor a minimum. That is M32, and it is where optimisation gets genuinely hard.
            In your real work
            Newton's method is everywhere, usually invisibly. It is how libraries compute reciprocals and square roots in hardware-adjacent code; the famous fast inverse square root is a crude initial guess followed by one Newton step. It underlies the solvers in physics engines and in layout constraint systems.
            

            Linear approximation is the mental model for almost all performance reasoning: "at this operating point, each extra request costs about 2 ms" is a derivative, and it is only valid locally — which is exactly why extrapolating a load test far beyond its measured range goes wrong. The second-order term is the curvature that bites you near saturation, and M40 explains why that curve goes vertical.
            

            Optimisation under constraints is the shape of most engineering trade-offs: largest cache that fits in memory, best quality within a frame budget, maximum throughput under a latency ceiling. And the convexity question — is this a problem where a local optimum is the global one? — decides whether gradient descent can be trusted, which is the opening question of M36.
            Flash cards
            Why does the derivative vanish at an interior maximum?
            The one-sided difference quotients have opposite signs, so the limit must be both at most and at least zero.
            What is the complete recipe for the absolute maximum on a closed interval?
            Evaluate the function at every critical point inside the interval and at both endpoints, then take the largest.
            When is the second derivative test inconclusive, and what do you do then?
            When f''(c) = 0. Fall back to the first derivative test and look at the sign change of f'.
            What distinguishes a point of inflexion from a point where f'' = 0?
            An inflexion requires the second derivative to change sign. x^4 at 0 has f'' = 0 and no inflexion.
            State Newton's iteration and its geometric meaning.
            x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n): follow the tangent at the current guess down to the axis.
            What does quadratic convergence mean in practice?
            The error squares each step, so the number of correct digits roughly doubles per iteration.
            Give two ways Newton's method can fail.
            A vanishing derivative at an iterate, and a multiple root, where convergence drops from quadratic to linear. It can also cycle or diverge from a poor start.
            How accurate is the tangent-line approximation?
            Its error is of order h squared, so halving the step quarters the error.
            What does convexity buy you in optimisation?
            Every local minimum is global, so a method that goes downhill cannot get stuck in the wrong place.
            Why does "increasing implies f' > 0" need care?
            x^3 is strictly increasing yet has a zero derivative at the origin. The correct statement is f' >= 0.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why both the tiny cut and the huge cut make a bad box, you can prove that the derivative vanishes at an interior extremum and say exactly which step uses the sign-flip rule, you never forget the endpoints when hunting an absolute maximum, you can derive Newton's iteration from the tangent line and explain why its errors square, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can say when you would reach for bisection instead.
