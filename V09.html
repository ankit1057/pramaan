The story

Imagine you want to build a magical paper airplane that flies farther than any other paper airplane on the school playground. You wonder: should you fold the wings narrow or wide? Should you use thick yellow construction paper or thin white notebook paper? Should you put a shiny metal paperclip on the nose or leave it off?

If you change all three things at once—folding a wide-wing plane out of heavy yellow paper with a paperclip, and comparing it to a narrow-wing plane out of thin white paper without a paperclip—and the yellow plane flies ten feet farther, which change helped? Was it the wide wings? The heavy paper? The paperclip? Or did the wind blow a lucky gust just as you threw?

You cannot tell, because you changed three things together! A true scientist tests one change at a time, or uses a special grid called a factorial design where every combination is tested fairly. You throw each plane three times from the exact same spot in a gym with no wind. You make sure your arm does not get tired on the tenth throw by shuffling the order. That is how you discover the truth.

Say it simply

Experimental design transforms informal exploration into rigorous causal inference. Key principles include randomisation (eliminating systematic bias), local control and blocking (grouping homogeneous units to reduce residual noise), and factorial designs (efficiently evaluating multiple factors and their interactions).

Pre-registration of hypotheses and stopping rules protects against p-hacking and optional stopping, where continuously monitoring data until p-value less than 0.05 inflates false positive rates. In large-scale online experimentation, variance reduction techniques like CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data) leverage pre-experiment user covariates to shrink confidence intervals and dramatically reduce sample size requirements.

Say it precisely

Let's understand how to run an experiment without tricking yourself.

  1. The Magic Combo (Factorial Design): Imagine you are growing tomato plants. You want to test if extra Water helps, and if Fertilizer helps. You could run two separate tests. But if you test them BOTH at the same time, you might discover something amazing: Water alone helps a little, Fertilizer alone helps a little, but Water AND Fertilizer together make the tomatoes explode in size! That extra boost is called an "Interaction."
  2. The "Are We There Yet?" Trick (Peeking): Imagine you flip a normal coin. If you check after 100 flips, it's about half heads, half tails. But what if you check after EVERY single flip, and stop the moment you are ahead by 5 heads? You will eventually hit that lucky streak by pure chance! If you stop there and declare the coin is rigged, you just tricked yourself. You have to decide when you will stop looking before you start.
  3. The History Hint (CUPED): Imagine a race to test new running shoes. One runner finishes in 5 minutes, another in 10 minutes. Did the shoes help? It's hard to tell because some people are naturally faster! But what if you looked at their old race times? If the 10-minute runner used to take 12 minutes, the shoes worked! Using past history removes the noise of natural differences, making your test much clearer without needing more runners.
  4. Checking Your Work (Validity): - Internal: Did the shoes actually make them faster, or was there a tailwind? - External: Will these shoes work for marathon runners, or just sprinters? - Construct: Are we measuring speed properly, or did someone take a shortcut? - Statistical: Did we use enough runners to be sure, or did we just get lucky?

Why it's true

Why does peeking ruin your test, and why does checking history help?

Step 1 (The Drunk Walk): If you flip a coin and take a step forward for Heads and backward for Tails, you are doing a "random walk." Even if the coin is totally fair, if you wander long enough, you will eventually wander 5 steps forward just by accident.

Step 2 (The Trap of Stopping Early): A real science test only works if you agree to check the score at the very end of the game. If you check the score every minute and ring the bell to end the game the moment your team takes the lead, you aren't finding the best team—you are just stopping on a lucky bounce! This can turn a perfectly fair 5 percent chance of making a mistake into a 35 percent chance of looking foolish.

Step 3 (Erasing the Noise): If you don't know a runner's history, their race time is a big mystery. It is made of two things: their natural speed + the shoe's help. If you subtract their natural speed (which you know from their past races), the only thing left is the shoe's help! This erases so much noise that it is like having three times as many runners in your race, completely for free.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "Looking at an A/B test dashboard every morning is harmless as long as we only stop when p-value less than 0.05." Continuous monitoring without sequential alpha correction inflates false positive rates from 5% to over 30% through optional stopping.
            • 

          • "Changing multiple factors simultaneously is always bad science." Factorial designs test multiple factors simultaneously with complete statistical validity, and are the only way to detect synergistic interactions between factors.
            • 

          • "Pre-registration is only needed in academic medicine." Pre-registration in software prevents teams from secretly shifting target metrics after seeing which telemetry numbers improved.
            • 

          • "CUPED changes the metric being measured." CUPED removes pre-existing variance without altering the expectation of the treatment effect, producing an unbiased estimator with smaller error bars.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          CUPED variance reduction breaks down when pre-experiment user history is missing (e.g. newly acquired users, cold-start devices), or when pre-experiment and post-experiment metrics have low correlation is less than 0.2, yielding negligible benefit. Factorial designs break down when higher-order interactions (A     imes B  imes C) cannot be reliably estimated without massive sample sizes.
          In your real work
          In feature flags and A/B testing, engineering teams frequently suffer from peeking bias: checking dashboard metrics daily and pulling the launch lever on day 4 when p-value is 0.04. Sequential Pocock boundaries or pre-experiment CUPED covariates enable faster, statistically sound decisions without inflating the false discovery rate.
          Flash cards
          What is the primary danger of optional stopping (peeking) in A/B testing?
          Continuous data monitoring inflates Type I error because random walk noise eventually crosses the nominal critical boundary even under a true null.
          What does CUPED stand for and what does it achieve?
          Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data; it uses pre-experiment covariates to reduce metric variance by factor 1 minus the squared correlation, shrinking required sample size.
          What is an interaction effect in a factorial design?
          When the effect of one independent variable on the outcome depends upon the specific level of another independent variable.
          What is the difference between internal validity and external validity?
          Internal validity asks whether the experimental treatment genuinely caused the outcome within the study; external validity asks whether the conclusion holds in other populations and environments.
          Why is permuted block randomisation preferred over pure coin-flip assignment?
          It ensures exact balance in sample sizes between treatment arms across time and within prognostic strata, preventing temporal confounding.
          How does a Pocock boundary control the error rate across multiple interim looks?
          It raises the critical significance threshold (e.g. from 1.96 to 2.36) so that the cumulative probability of a false rejection across all planned looks remains exactly alpha.
          Stop when
          You can explain why dashboard peeking inflates false positive rates to an executive, implement CUPED variance reduction on user logs, and calculate interaction terms in a factorial design.
