The story

Imagine you are a detective investigating why ice cream sales and sunburn cases rise and fall together across the summer months. On sunny days, thousand of people buy cold vanilla ice cream cones, and thousands of people get red, stinging sunburns. If you only look at your clipboard, you might conclude that eating vanilla ice cream causes painful sunburns, or that getting a sunburn makes you crave ice cream!

A six-year-old child laughs at this idea because the child knows there is a giant, fiery sun blazing in the sky. The hot summer sun causes people to buy refreshing ice cream, and the exact same hot summer sun burns unprotected skin. The sun is the hidden puppet master—the confounder.

Even stranger things happen when you compare two hospitals. Hospital A might have a higher overall death rate than small rural Hospital B. But when you look at mild patients, Hospital A cures more of them. When you look at severe patients, Hospital A cures more of them too! How can Hospital A be better for mild cases and better for severe cases, yet have a worse overall death rate? Because all the critically ill, dying patients are sent in ambulances to Hospital A. This optical illusion is called Simpson's Paradox.

Say it simply

Correlation does not imply causation. Two variables can correlate strongly due to a common cause (confounder), reverse causation, or conditioning on a common effect (collider bias). Causal Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) formalize causal assumptions and identify necessary adjustment sets using the back-door criterion.

Simpson's paradox occurs when an apparent association between two variables reverses upon conditioning on a third confounding variable. In software engineering, observational telemetry is riddled with selection confounding (e.g. power users adopt a new feature and experience more crashes simply because they use the app 10x longer). Randomised controlled trials (A/B tests) break all confounding back-door paths by assigning treatment independently of all pre-existing user attributes.

Say it precisely

Let causal DAG $G = (V, E)$ represent structural causal relationships. 1. The Back-Door Criterion (Judea Pearl): A set of variables $Z$ satisfies the back-door criterion relative to treatment $X$ and outcome $Y$ if: - No node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$. - $Z$ blocks every back-door path between $X$ and $Y$ (paths ending with an arrow pointing into $X$). 2. The Adjustment Formula: If $Z$ satisfies the back-door criterion: $$\mathbb{P}(Y = y \mid \text{do}(X = x)) = \sum_z \mathbb{P}(Y = y \mid X = x, Z = z) \mathbb{P}(Z = z)$$ 3. Collider Bias (Berkson's Paradox): On a path $X \to C \leftarrow Y$, $C$ is a collider. Conditioning on $C$ opens the path, inducing a spurious correlation between $X$ and $Y$. 4. Simpson's Paradox: A situation where: $$\mathbb{P}(Y \mid X) > \mathbb{P}(Y \mid \neg X) \quad \text{yet} \quad \forall z, \; \mathbb{P}(Y \mid X, Z = z) < \mathbb{P}(Y \mid \neg X, Z = z)$$ 5. Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM): In an A/B test with intended ratio $r$, an observed count ratio differing significantly ($\chi^2$ test $p < 0.001$) indicates broken randomisation or attrition bias.

Why it's true

Why does Simpson's paradox occur mathematically, and why does randomisation prevent it?

Step 1 (The marginal probability expansion): By the law of total probability, the overall success rate for treatment $X=1$ versus control $X=0$ is: $$\mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid X = x) = \sum_z \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid X = x, Z = z) \mathbb{P}(Z = z \mid X = x)$$ Notice that the overall rate is a weighted average of the stratum-specific rates $\mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid X = x, Z = z)$, where the weights are the stratum allocation proportions $\mathbb{P}(Z = z \mid X = x)$.

Step 2 (The mechanism of reversal): Suppose stratum $Z=1$ represents severe disease where survival is universally low, while $Z=0$ represents mild disease where survival is high. If doctors preferentially assign the new treatment $X=1$ to severe patients, the weight $\mathbb{P}(Z=1 \mid X=1)$ is large (e.g. $80\%$), pulling the weighted average down. If control $X=0$ is given mostly to mild patients, its weight $\mathbb{P}(Z=0 \mid X=0)$ is large, pulling its weighted average up. Consequently, the treatment can have a higher survival rate in every single stratum, yet have a lower overall survival rate.

Step 3 (Why randomisation breaks the paradox): In a randomised controlled trial, treatment $X$ is assigned by a coin toss or PRNG, independent of patient severity $Z$: $\mathbb{P}(Z = z \mid X = 1) = \mathbb{P}(Z = z \mid X = 0) = \mathbb{P}(Z = z)$. The weights become identical across both groups. When weights are equal, if treatment dominates in every stratum, it is mathematically impossible for the weighted average to reverse!

          Misconceptions
            

          • "If two metrics have Pearson $r = 0.95$, one must be causing the other." Spurious correlation arises whenever both metrics are driven by latent confounding variables (e.g. population growth, inflation, daytime temperature).
            • 

          • "Conditioning on more covariates always reduces bias." Conditioning on a collider (a variable caused by both treatment and outcome) opens a spurious back-door path, inducing artificial selection bias.
            • 

          • "Sample Ratio Mismatch is a minor cosmetic issue that can be ignored." SRM indicates that the randomisation engine or data pipeline is corrupted, rendering all downstream p-values and effect sizes invalid.
            • 

          • "Natural experiments and Difference-in-Differences provide the same certainty as randomised trials." DiD relies on the unverifiable 'parallel trends' counterfactual assumption; if treatment and control groups would have diverged in the absence of treatment, DiD estimates are biased.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Causal inference from observational telemetry breaks down completely under unobserved confounding where latent variables violate the back-door criterion. Difference-in-differences estimators fail when the pre-intervention parallel trends assumption is violated or when spillover effects across connected users violate the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA). In complex systems with feedback loops, simple DAG directed acyclic models fail to capture dynamic equilibrium.
          In your real work
          In mobile A/B testing, teams frequently observe a sample ratio mismatch where treatment has 48,000 users and control has 50,000. Analysing conversion rates on that data produces invalid conclusions, because the 2,000 'missing' users in treatment crashed on launch before their conversion event was ever logged. Detecting SRM is the mandatory first health check of any real A/B test.
          Flash cards
          What is the Back-Door Criterion?
          A graphical criterion in causal DAGs: a set of variables $Z$ blocks all confounding paths between treatment $X$ and outcome $Y$ without containing descendants of $X$.
          What is a collider in a causal DAG?
          A node where two arrows converge ($A \to C \leftarrow B$). Conditioning on a collider opens the path, creating spurious association between $A$ and $B$.
          State Simpson's Paradox.
          A statistical phenomenon where a trend or correlation observed in several distinct groups reverses when the groups are aggregated.
          What is Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM)?
          A statistically significant difference between the intended and observed sample allocation ratio in an A/B test, indicating broken randomisation or attrition bias.
          What is the core identifying assumption of Difference-in-Differences (DiD)?
          The parallel trends assumption: in the absence of treatment, the average change in the treatment group would have been identical to the change in the control group.
          What is regression to the mean?
          The statistical tendency for extreme random scores on an initial test to move closer to the true population average upon subsequent re-testing.
          Stop when
          You can explain Simpson's paradox with a concrete 2x2 contingency table, detect Sample Ratio Mismatch in an A/B test log, and identify colliders on a causal DAG.
