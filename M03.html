The story

In the seventeenth century, European financial houses and trading fleets faced a mathematical revolution that transformed global commerce: the widespread adoption of decimal fractions and compound interest tables. For millennia, ancient civilizations had calculated parts of wholes using cumbersome systems. The ancient Egyptians used only unit fractions—fractions with a numerator of one—requiring complex tables simply to express two-fifths as one-third plus one-fifteenth ($2/5 = 1/3 + 1/15$). The Babylonians used base-60 sexagesimal fractions for astronomical calculations, while medieval European merchants relied on abacuses and physical coin denominations (such as pounds, shillings, and pence) that defied clean decimal calculation.

The breakthrough came in 1585, when Flemish mathematician and engineer Simon Stevin published a small thirty-six-page booklet titled De Thiende ("The Art of Tenths"). Stevin demonstrated that by extending the decimal place-value system to the right of the unit position using a decimal point, all calculations involving fractions—addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division—could be performed using the exact same mechanical rules as whole numbers. Soon after, Scottish mathematician John Napier invented decimal point notation as we know it today. Meanwhile, in Italian renaissance banking houses, the transition from simple interest to compound interest—famously dubbed by Albert Einstein as "the eighth wonder of the world"—unlocked the modern global financial system. The ability to express proportions, convert repeating decimals, and compute compounding percentage returns allowed merchant ships to insure cargo, governments to issue sovereign bonds, and cartographers to map global trade routes with precise representative fractions.

Say it simply

A fraction represents parts of a whole, written as a numerator divided by a denominator. Two fractions are equivalent if multiplying or dividing both parts by the same number preserves their value. To divide by a fraction, we 'invert and multiply' because dividing by a part is identical to multiplying by how many of those parts fit into the whole. Decimals are simply fractions whose denominators are powers of ten (tenths, hundredths, thousandths), extending place value to the right of the decimal point. Ratios compare two or more quantities relative to each other, like dividing an inheritance or scaling distances on a map. Percentages express ratios as parts out of one hundred, providing a universal benchmark for comparing discounts, taxes, and compound investment growth across time.

Say it precisely

The formal definitions, algebraic identities, and computational rules for rational numbers, decimals, and commercial ratios are defined as follows:

  1. Rational Numbers and Equivalence: The field of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ consists of equivalence classes of pairs $(p, q)$ where $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $q \ne 0$: $$\mathbb{Q} = \left\{ \frac{p}{q} \;\middle|\; p, q \in \mathbb{Z}, q \ne 0 \right\}$$ Two fractions $\frac{a}{b}$ and $\frac{c}{d}$ are equivalent ($\frac{a}{b} \sim \frac{c}{d}$) if and only if $a \cdot d = b \cdot c$. - Addition: $\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a d + b c}{b d}$. - Multiplication: $\frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a c}{b d}$. - Division (Invert and Multiply): $$\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a/b}{c/d} = \frac{(a/b) \cdot (d/c)}{(c/d) \cdot (d/c)} = \frac{(a \cdot d)/(b \cdot c)}{1} = \frac{a \cdot d}{b \cdot c}$$

  2. Converting Repeating Decimals to Fractions: Every rational number $x \in \mathbb{Q}$ has a decimal expansion that either terminates or eventually repeats periodically: $$x = 0.a_1 a_2 \dots a_k \overline{b_1 b_2 \dots b_m}$$ Let non-repeating part have length $k$ and repeating period have length $m$: $$10^k \cdot x = A.\overline{B} \quad \text{and} \quad 10^{k+m} \cdot x = AB.\overline{B}$$ Subtracting eliminates the infinite repeating tail: $$(10^{k+m} - 10^k) \cdot x = AB - A \implies x = \frac{AB - A}{10^k(10^m - 1)}$$

  3. Ratio, Proportion, and Multi-Term Division: A ratio $a : b : c$ divides a total quantity $Q$ into parts proportional to weights: $$\text{Part}_i = Q \cdot \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j} w_j}$$ - Direct Proportion: $y = k x \implies \frac{y_1}{x_1} = \frac{y_2}{x_2} = k$. - Inverse Proportion: $y \cdot x = k \implies y_1 x_1 = y_2 x_2 = k$. - Representative Fraction (RF): In cartography, a scale of $1 : S$ means $1\text{ cm}$ on the map equals $S\text{ cm} = \frac{S}{100000}\text{ km}$ in reality.

  4. Percentages and Commercial Mathematics: - Percentage Change vs Percentage Points: An increase from $20\%$ to $25\%$ is an increase of $5$ percentage points, but a $25\%$ relative increase ($\frac{25 - 20}{20} = 0.25$). - Profit and Discount: $$\text{Cost Price (CP)}, \quad \text{Marked Price (MP)} = \text{CP} \cdot (1 + \text{markup}), \quad \text{Selling Price (SP)} = \text{MP} \cdot (1 - \text{discount})$$ $$\text{Profit} = \text{SP} - \text{CP}, \quad \text{Profit \%} = \frac{\text{SP} - \text{CP}}{\text{CP}} \times 100$$ - Compound Interest (CI): $$A = P \left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{n t}, \quad \text{CI} = A - P$$ where $P$ is principal, $r$ is annual rate, $n$ is compounding frequency per year, and $t$ is years. As $n \to \infty$, $A = P e^{r t}$ (continuous compounding).

Why it's true

Mastering fractions, decimals, ratios, and percentages is crucial for software engineering, quantitative finance, and systems architecture. In computer science, naive decimal-to-binary floating-point conversion is one of the most frequent causes of catastrophic software bugs. Because base-10 fractions like $0.1$ and $0.01$ cannot be represented finitely in binary ($1/10 = 0.000110011\dots_2$), financial systems like banking ledgers and automated exchanges must represent currency balances using exact rational numbers or integer hundredths (cents/paise) to prevent fractional cent leakage.

In UI engineering and mobile development (Flutter, Android), understanding ratios and map scales is essential for responsive layout design. Aspect ratios ($16:9, 4:3$), device pixel ratios (DPR), vector scaling, and coordinate transformations between screen points and real-world geographical coordinates require precise ratio and proportion calculations.

Furthermore, commercial mathematics—profit, loss, and compound interest—governs SaaS business metrics: customer acquisition cost (CAC), lifetime value (LTV), annual recurring revenue (ARR), and server depreciation schedules. Engineers who understand the mathematical difference between percentage changes and percentage points avoid misinterpreting A/B test results, performance regressions, and cloud billing tier discounts.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "An increase of 10% followed by a decrease of 10% leaves you back at the original amount." Percentage changes are not symmetric because the base changes. If you start with 100, a 10% increase yields 110. A subsequent 10% decrease takes 10% of 110 (11), leaving you with 99, a net 1% loss.
            • 

          • "Percentage points and percent mean the exact same thing." They are fundamentally different. If an interest rate or unemployment rate rises from 10% to 12%, it increased by 2 percentage points, but 20 percent in relative terms ((12 - 10) / 10 = 0.20). Confusing the two causes catastrophic statistical errors.
            • 

          • "To divide by a fraction, you should divide numerators and denominators directly." While dividing numerators and denominators is technically true when they divide evenly, it is practically unusable (e.g. (2/3) / (5/7) = (2/5) / (3/7)). The universal algebraic method is to invert the divisor and multiply: (2/3) x (7/5) = 14/15.
            • 

          • "A recurring decimal like 0.999... is slightly less than 1." 0.999... is mathematically and identically equal to 1. By the geometric series formula or by algebra: if x = 0.999..., then 10x = 9.999..., so 9x = 9, yielding x = 1 exactly.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Rational arithmetic and decimal representations break down under specific computational boundaries:
1. IEEE 754 Floating-Point Precision Leakage: In binary floating point, $0.1 + 0.2 = 0.30000000000000004$, causing equality tests 0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 to evaluate to False in modern programming languages.
2. Infinite Periodic Repetend Expansion in Fixed Buffers: Fractions with prime factors other than 2 and 5 in the denominator (such as $1/7 = 0.\overline{142857}$) require infinite decimal representation, truncating with precision loss in finite decimal floats.
3. Compound Growth Exponent Overflow in Bounded Integers: Compounding calculations over long time spans with frequent periods ($(1 + r/n)^{nt}$) can exceed floating-point maximums ($10^{308}$) or cause devastating overflow in fixed-width integers.
          In your real work
          Ratios, proportions, and rational arithmetic are implemented across production software:
- E-Commerce Pricing and Tax Engines: Platforms like Shopify and Flipkart compute discounts, GST tax percentages, and coupon markdowns using integer cents and decimal fixed-point types (e.g., Python decimal.Decimal or Java BigDecimal).
- Map Tile Pyramids and GIS Scaling: Mapping services (Google Maps, OpenStreetMap) use Representative Fractions and tile coordinate ratios ($2^{\text{zoom}}$) to map screen pixels to real-world geographical meters.
- Automated Market Makers (AMM) in DeFi: Decentralized exchanges (Uniswap) calculate token swap exchange ratios using constant-product invariant formulas ($x \cdot y = k$) based on exact rational fraction division.
          Flash cards
          Why does the 'invert and multiply' rule work for fraction division?
          Multiplying numerator and denominator by the reciprocal of the divisor turns the denominator into 1, leaving (a/b) x (d/c).
          How is a repeating decimal like 0.1666... converted into a fraction?
          Let x = 0.1666...; multiply by 100 (16.666...) and 10 (1.666...); subtract to get 90x = 15, yielding x = 15/90 = 1/6.
          What is the difference between an increase of 5 percentage points and 5 percent?
          An increase from 20% to 25% is 5 percentage points, but a 25% relative increase ((25 - 20) / 20 = 0.25).
          What is the formula for compound interest compounded n times per year?
          A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), which approaches continuous compounding Pe^(rt) as frequency n approaches infinity.
          What does a cartographic Representative Fraction scale of 1:50,000 mean?
          One centimeter on the map represents 50,000 centimeters (500 meters or 0.5 kilometers) in reality.
          Why does a 10% price increase followed by a 10% decrease result in a net loss?
          Because the 10% decrease is calculated on a larger base: 100 x 1.10 = 110, then 110 x 0.90 = 99 (a net 1% loss).
          Stop when
          You can perform exact rational fraction arithmetic, convert repeating decimals to reduced fractions, partition quantities using multi-term ratios, convert cartographic map scales, and calculate compound interest and commercial profits.
