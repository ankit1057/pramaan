The story

Kabir's grandmother has thirty-six square tiles and wants a rectangular patch of floor using every one. She asks Kabir how long the sides should be.

Kabir finds nine by four. Then he finds six by six. Then twelve by three. "Which one do you want?" he asks, slightly annoyed. The question has too many answers.

So Dadi adds a second condition. "The patch must be five tiles longer than it is wide."

Now Kabir has a puzzle. Width times (width plus five) must be thirty-six. He tries four: four times nine is thirty-six. It works.

Then, being thorough, he keeps going backwards past zero, the way his teacher showed him. Minus nine: minus nine times minus four is also thirty-six. That works too.

Kabir is confused. There is only one floor. How can there be two answers?

Dadi laughs. "Can you lay minus nine tiles across my floor?"

Here is the surprise: the arithmetic is more generous than the floor. The equation faithfully answers the question it was asked, which was about numbers, and it hands back both. Deciding which answers describe a real floor is a separate job, and it is yours, not the equation's. Every quadratic you will ever solve does this — and sometimes it hands back answers that are not on the number line at all.

Say it simply

A quadratic equation is one where the unknown appears squared and no higher: $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \neq 0$. Because squaring loses the sign, these equations characteristically have two solutions.

There are three ways to solve one. Factorise it, when the numbers are friendly. Complete the square, which always works and shows you why. Or use the quadratic formula, which is just completing the square done once in general and remembered.

A single number, the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$, tells you in advance what kind of answer you will get: two separate real roots, one repeated root, or two complex roots that live off the number line.

The one-line takeaway: a quadratic always has exactly two roots once you allow complex numbers and count repeats, and the discriminant tells you which kind you are about to meet.

Say it precisely

Standard form and the three methods

The standard form is

$$ax^2 + bx + c = 0, \qquad a \neq 0$$

where $a$, $b$ and $c$ are the coefficients. If $a = 0$ the equation is linear, not quadratic.

Factorisation rewrites the left side as $a(x - \alpha)(x - \beta)$ and uses the zero-product rule: a product is zero exactly when one of its factors is zero.

Completing the square turns the left side into a perfect square plus a constant:

$$ax^2+bx+c = a\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + \left(c - \frac{b^2}{4a}\right)$$

The point $\left(-\frac{b}{2a},\; c - \frac{b^2}{4a}\right)$ is the vertex of the parabola, its lowest point when $a > 0$ and its highest when $a < 0$.

The quadratic formula:

$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$

The discriminant

$$D = b^2 - 4ac$$

$D = 0$ one real root, repeated touches the axis once
$D < 0$ two complex conjugate roots never meets the axis

When $D < 0$ the roots are $\frac{-b}{2a} \pm i\frac{\sqrt{|D|}}{2a}$, where $i^2 = -1$. For real coefficients complex roots always arrive in conjugate pairs: if $p + qi$ is a root then so is $p - qi$.

Vieta's relations

If $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the roots of $ax^2+bx+c=0$ then

$$\alpha + \beta = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad \alpha\beta = \frac{c}{a}$$

These let you build a quadratic from its roots — $x^2 - (\text{sum})x + (\text{product}) = 0$ — and they give a cheap, independent check on any computed pair of roots.

The formula is not safe to use as written

Here is the part textbooks omit. When $b^2 \gg 4ac$, the square root $\sqrt{b^2-4ac}$ is very close to $|b|$. One of the two numerators, $-b \pm \sqrt{D}$, is then a subtraction of two nearly equal quantities — and in floating-point arithmetic that is catastrophic cancellation: the leading digits agree and cancel, leaving a result built from the few noisy digits at the bottom. The root comes back with almost no correct significant figures.

The fix uses Vieta. Compute the well-conditioned root first, choosing the sign so that the two terms add rather than subtract:

$$q = -\frac{1}{2}\left(b + \operatorname{sgn}(b)\sqrt{b^2-4ac}\right), \qquad x_1 = \frac{q}{a}, \qquad x_2 = \frac{c}{q}$$

Here $\operatorname{sgn}(b)$ is $+1$ when $b \ge 0$ and $-1$ when $b < 0$. The first root never cancels because the signs are arranged to agree; the second comes from the product relation $\alpha\beta = c/a$, which needs no subtraction at all.

A pinch of history

Procedures for quadratic problems are very old. Babylonian tablets from around 1800 BCE solve problems equivalent to quadratics by a method that amounts to completing the square, stated as recipes on specific numbers.

In India, Brahmagupta in the Brahmasphutasiddhanta (628 CE) gave an explicit rule for solving the quadratic, and — crucially — worked with negative quantities and with zero as numbers in their own right, which is what allows both roots to be named rather than only the positive one. Sridhara, some time in the ninth or tenth century, is credited in later commentaries with the method of multiplying through to complete the square; the rule is often called Sridharacharya's formula in Indian textbooks. Bhaskara II's Bijaganita (1150) treats both roots explicitly and observes that a negative quantity has no real square root.

Al-Khwarizmi's al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa'l-muqabala (c. 820 CE) classified quadratics into cases — since negative coefficients were avoided — and gave geometric completions of the square for each; the word algebra comes from al-jabr in that title.

Why it's true

Result 1: deriving the quadratic formula by completing the square.

Start from $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \neq 0$.

  1. Divide by $a$: $x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{c}{a} = 0$.
  2. Move the constant: $x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}$.
  3. The non-obvious step. Add $\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2$ to both sides. This specific number is chosen because $x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{b^2}{4a^2}$ is exactly $\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2$ — we are forcing the left side to become a perfect square.
  4. Now $\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{c}{a} = \frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}$.
  5. Take square roots, remembering both signs: $x + \frac{b}{2a} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$.
  6. Rearrange: $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$.

Step 3 is where the whole derivation lives. Everything else is algebra. Notice the $\pm$ in step 5 is not decoration: it is where the second root comes from, and it is the formal version of Kabir's negative nine.

Result 2: Vieta's relations.

  1. If $\alpha, \beta$ are the roots, then $ax^2+bx+c = a(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)$, because both sides are quadratics with the same roots and the same leading coefficient.
  2. Expand the right side: $a\left(x^2 - (\alpha+\beta)x + \alpha\beta\right) = ax^2 - a(\alpha+\beta)x + a\alpha\beta$.
  3. Two polynomials are equal exactly when their coefficients match.
  4. Matching the $x$ coefficient: $b = -a(\alpha+\beta)$, so $\alpha+\beta = -\frac{b}{a}$.
  5. Matching the constant: $c = a\alpha\beta$, so $\alpha\beta = \frac{c}{a}$.

Result 3: why the stable formula works.

  1. The danger is subtracting two nearly equal floating-point numbers, which destroys leading digits.
  2. In $-b \pm \sqrt{D}$, exactly one choice of sign is a subtraction when $D$ is close to $b^2$.
  3. Choosing the sign to match $b$'s sign makes the two terms add in magnitude, so no cancellation occurs. That is $q = -\frac{1}{2}(b + \operatorname{sgn}(b)\sqrt{D})$.
  4. By Vieta, the product of the roots is $\frac{c}{a}$. So once one root $x_1 = \frac{q}{a}$ is known accurately, the other is $x_2 = \frac{c}{a} \div x_1 = \frac{c}{q}$.
  5. A division never cancels. So both roots come out with full precision.

Oracle lab

The oracle finds the roots two completely independent ways.

The first is the quadratic formula. The second is bisection: a numerical root-finder that knows no algebra at all. It brackets a sign change of the function and repeatedly halves the interval. Bisection has never heard of discriminants, of $-b$, or of $2a$; it only ever evaluates $ax^2+bx+c$ and looks at the sign. If the formula had a wrong coefficient or a flipped sign, the two would part company immediately.

We also check Vieta's relations against the roots the formula produced, which is a third, cheap cross-check on a different piece of structure. The mutant uses $b^2 + 4ac$ for the discriminant, a sign slip that leaves the formula looking perfectly plausible.

Experiment lab
Now the prediction that matters in real code. Theory says that when $b^2 \gg 4ac$, the textbook formula computes one root by subtracting two nearly equal numbers, and loses roughly as many significant digits as the two numbers share. The stable Vieta form should lose none.


We measure this directly. For $x^2 + bx + 1 = 0$ with $b$ growing through powers of ten, the small root is near $-\frac{1}{b}$ and the large root near $-b$. We compute both roots each way and then measure the residual $|ax^2+bx+c|$ — how badly the returned root fails to actually satisfy the equation. The residual is an honest referee: it never consults either formula.

Where reality disagrees: notice what the residual test does not tell you. A tiny residual does not by itself prove a root is accurate — for an ill-conditioned polynomial a point can satisfy the equation to machine precision while sitting some distance from the true root. Residual measures backward error (which nearby problem did we solve exactly), not forward error (how far is our answer from the right one). For the quadratic the two coincide closely enough for this demonstration, but the distinction is the whole subject of conditioning, and it returns properly in M35. Note also that this is not a flaw in the mathematics: the formula is algebraically exact. It is a flaw in evaluating it with finite-precision arithmetic.

            def test_agrees_with_the_textbook_formula_when_it_is_safe():

    for a, b, c in ((1, -7, 12), (2, 5, -3), (-1, 4, 5), (3, -2, -8)):

        lo = min((-b - d) / (2 * a), (-b + d) / (2 * a))
        hi = max((-b - d) / (2 * a), (-b + d) / (2 * a))

        assert abs(got[1] - hi) < 1e-9


def test_keeps_precision_where_the_textbook_formula_fails():
    from math import sqrt
    # x^2 + 1e8 x + 1 = 0. The true small root is -1e-8 to many digits,
    # because the roots multiply to c/a = 1 and the large one is -1e8.
    a, b, c = 1.0, 1e8, 1.0
    true_small = -1e-8
    small_stable = min(solve_stable(a, b, c), key=abs)
    d = sqrt(b * b - 4 * a * c)
    naive_small = (-b + d) / (2 * a)

    # The stable root is correct to within a rounding error.
    assert abs(small_stable - true_small) <= 1e-10 * abs(true_small)
    assert residual(a, b, c, small_stable) < 1e-12

    # The textbook form cancels and comes back about 25% wrong.
    assert abs(naive_small - true_small) > 0.1 * abs(true_small)
    assert residual(a, b, c, naive_small) > 1e-3

    # Vieta still holds for the stable pair.
    r1, r2 = solve_stable(a, b, c)
    assert abs(r1 * r2 - c / a) < 1e-9

    # The LARGE root's residual is about 1, and that is not a bug: at a
    # magnitude of 1e16 the gap between neighbouring doubles is about 2.
    assert max_residual(a, b, c) < 10

            Misconceptions
              

            • "A quadratic has two solutions, so a word problem has two answers." The equation has two roots; the problem may have one, or none. Lengths, counts and times cannot be negative, so you must discard roots that the physical situation forbids. The equation will not do this for you.
              • 

            • "$D < 0$ means there is no solution." It means there is no real solution. There are still exactly two roots, and they are complex conjugates. "No solution" and "no real solution" are different statements.
              • 

            • "The quadratic formula is exact, so a program using it is accurate." Exact in algebra, unreliable in floating point. When $b^2 \gg 4ac$ one of the two roots is computed by subtracting nearly equal numbers and can come back with no correct digits at all.
              • 

            • "Completing the square is just an old-fashioned route to the formula." It is where the formula comes from, and it is the form that shows you the vertex directly. It also generalises: the same move diagonalises quadratic forms and underlies the Gaussian integral.
              • 

            • "If a product equals zero, I can set each factor to whatever I like." The zero-product rule says at least one factor is zero, and it holds only when the right-hand side is zero. Factorising $x(x-3) = 4$ and setting $x = 4$ is a standard disaster.
              • 

            • "Complex roots are a made-up patch for equations that fail." They are forced. Once you accept that every quadratic should have two roots, the complex numbers are not optional, and they turn out to describe rotation, oscillation and alternating current.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Everything here assumes $a \neq 0$. If $a$ is not exactly zero but merely tiny, the equation is nearly linear, one root runs off towards infinity, and the formula divides by $2a$ with predictably poor results. That is the same conditioning problem as the near-parallel lines of M12, in a new costume.
            

            The factorisation exercise only finds roots that are integers dividing $c$. Rational roots with a denominator, and all irrational roots, are invisible to it. Factorisation as a solving method is a convenience for textbook numbers, not a general algorithm.
            

            The discriminant test d == 0 is exact only in integer arithmetic. With floating-point coefficients a genuinely repeated root almost never produces a discriminant of exactly zero, so code that branches on equality will take the wrong branch and then take the square root of a small negative number. Real solvers compare against a tolerance scaled to the coefficients.
            

            Beyond degree two, the story changes. Cubics and quartics have formulas, both far uglier. From degree five upward, the Abel-Ruffini theorem says no general formula in radicals exists — and Galois theory explains exactly why, in terms of which permutations of the roots the equation cannot tell apart.
            In your real work
            The stable solver is not a curiosity; it is what numpy.roots and every serious library do, and the recipe appears in Numerical Recipes precisely because the textbook form burns people. Quadratics turn up in graphics and animation constantly: ray-sphere intersection is a quadratic in the ray parameter, and an ill-conditioned one when the ray grazes the sphere, which is exactly the $b^2 \gg 4ac$ regime where naive code produces visual artefacts. Easing curves, ballistic arcs and "time until these two moving views collide" are all quadratics. Knowing why the textbook formula is unsafe, and being able to derive the fix from Vieta on the spot, is a strong signal in an L5 interview.
            Flash cards
            What is the discriminant and what does each of its three cases mean?
            b^2 - 4ac. Positive means two distinct real roots, zero means one repeated real root, negative means two complex conjugate roots.
            Which step in completing the square is the one that matters, and why that number?
            Adding (b/2a)^2 to both sides, because that is exactly the number that turns the left side into a perfect square.
            State Vieta's relations for a quadratic.
            The roots sum to -b/a and multiply to c/a.
            Why can the textbook quadratic formula lose all precision?
            When b^2 is much larger than 4ac, sqrt(D) is nearly equal to |b|, so one of the numerators subtracts two nearly equal numbers and the leading digits cancel.
            Give the numerically stable way to compute both roots.
            Let q = -(b + sign(b)*sqrt(D))/2. Then the roots are q/a and c/q. The first never cancels, and the second uses division rather than subtraction.
            For real coefficients, why do complex roots always come in pairs?
            Because the only complex part comes from the square root of a negative discriminant, which enters as plus or minus the same imaginary quantity, giving conjugates.
            A word problem gives roots 4 and -9 for a length. What do you do?
            Discard -9. The equation answers a question about numbers; checking which roots are physically admissible is a separate step you must perform.
            What does a zero discriminant look like on the graph?
            The parabola touches the horizontal axis at exactly one point, its vertex, without crossing.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why one floor can have two answers, you can derive the quadratic formula by completing the square without looking it up, you can state what the discriminant predicts in all three cases, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can say precisely when the textbook formula loses precision and write the stable version from Vieta's product relation.
