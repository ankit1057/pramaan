The story

Neha ships firmware over a flaky serial link. Every image carries a checksum — the sum of all the bytes, truncated to 16 bits — and the device refuses anything that does not match.

A device comes back bricked. The checksum had matched.

She pulls the image off the device and diffs it against the original. Two bytes are swapped. Byte 4019 holds what byte 4020 should, and vice versa. The sum is identical, because addition does not care what order you add things in.

That is the bug, and it is not really a bug in her code. It is a bug in her choice of algebra. She picked a structure whose operation is commutative, so the structure is blind to position. Any corruption that permutes bytes is invisible to it, forever, no matter how many bits of checksum she adds.

She switches to CRC32 and the swapped-byte corruption is caught instantly. CRC is not a cleverer sum. It is division — the message is treated as a polynomial whose coefficients live in a field with two elements, divided by a fixed generator polynomial, and the remainder is the check value. In that structure position is everything, because $x^{4019}$ and $x^{4020}$ are different terms.

And then the part that makes this a module rather than an anecdote. The same two-element field underlies AES. The same "division leaves a remainder" structure gives Hamming codes that correct errors rather than merely detecting them. The same idea of a group with a hard-to-invert operation gives Diffie-Hellman, Shamir's secret sharing and zero-knowledge proofs. Neha went looking for a better checksum and found the structure that most of applied computing is built on.

Say it simply

A group is a set with one operation that is associative, has an identity, and gives every element an inverse. That is enough structure to prove surprisingly strong things — Lagrange's theorem, that the size of any subgroup divides the size of the group, is the one that does most of the work.

A ring adds a second operation (multiplication), and a field is a ring where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse. Fields are where you can do all four arithmetic operations, which is why they are the setting for codes and cryptography.

The finite fields are the interesting ones for computing. $\mathrm{GF}(p)$ is arithmetic mod a prime. $\mathrm{GF}(2^n)$ is polynomial arithmetic mod 2 and mod an irreducible polynomial, which is exactly what CRC, Reed-Solomon and AES use.

Homomorphisms are structure-preserving maps, and they are what lets you prove two apparently different systems are the same one in disguise.

Say it precisely

Groups

A group $(G,\ast)$ satisfies:

  1. Closure: $a\ast b\in G$
  2. Associativity: $(a\ast b)\ast c=a\ast(b\ast c)$
  3. Identity: some $e$ with $e\ast a=a\ast e=a$
  4. Inverses: each $a$ has $a^{-1}$ with $a\ast a^{-1}=e$

It is abelian when $a\ast b=b\ast a$. Note commutativity is not required — and Neha's checksum failed precisely because she used an abelian one.

Group Elements Operation Order Abelian?
$\mathbb{Z}_n$ $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ $+\bmod n$ $n$ yes
$\mathbb{Z}_p^\*$ $\{1,\dots,p-1\}$ $\times\bmod p$ $p-1$ yes
$S_n$ permutations composition $n!$ no for $n\ge3$
$D_n$ symmetries of an $n$-gon composition $2n$ no for $n\ge3$

The order of an element $a$ is the least $k>0$ with $a^k=e$. A group is cyclic when some element generates all of it; $\mathbb{Z}_n$ always is, and $\mathbb{Z}_p^\*$ is cyclic for every prime $p$ — a generator is called a primitive root.

Lagrange's theorem. If $H\le G$ and $G$ is finite, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$. Consequences:

  • The order of every element divides $|G|$
  • $a^{|G|}=e$ for every $a$ — which is Fermat's little theorem in $\mathbb{Z}_p^\*$, and Euler's theorem in $\mathbb{Z}_n^\*$
  • Every group of prime order is cyclic, and has no subgroups but the trivial ones

Rings and fields

A ring has two operations: $(R,+)$ abelian, $\times$ associative and distributing over $+$. A field additionally has $\times$ commutative with an identity, and an inverse for every non-zero element.

$\mathbb{Z}_n$ is a field iff $n$ is prime. For composite $n$ there are zero divisors — $2\times3=0$ in $\mathbb{Z}_6$ — and a zero divisor can never have an inverse.

Every finite field has order $p^n$ for a prime $p$, and for each such order there is exactly one field up to isomorphism, written $\mathrm{GF}(p^n)$.

GF(2) and GF(2^n)

$\mathrm{GF}(2)=\{0,1\}$ with addition = XOR and multiplication = AND. This is the whole reason binary computers and this algebra fit together so well: addition is subtraction is XOR, and there are no carries.

$\mathrm{GF}(2^n)$ is polynomials over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ of degree $<n$, modulo an irreducible polynomial of degree $n$. A byte is a polynomial:

$$\texttt{0b10001011} \;\longleftrightarrow\; x^7+x^3+x+1$$

  • Addition is XOR of the bit patterns — no carries anywhere.
  • Multiplication is polynomial multiplication followed by reduction mod the irreducible polynomial.

AES uses $\mathrm{GF}(2^8)$ with $x^8+x^4+x^3+x+1$ (0x11B). Irreducibility matters for exactly the reason primality did: a reducible modulus gives zero divisors and the inverses disappear.

CRC as polynomial division

Treat the message bits as a polynomial $M(x)$ over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$. Pick a generator $G(x)$ of degree $r$. The CRC is

$$R(x) = x^r M(x) \bmod G(x)$$

and the transmitted word $x^rM(x)+R(x)$ is divisible by $G(x)$. The receiver divides; a non-zero remainder means error.

What it catches follows from the algebra. An error pattern $E(x)$ is undetected iff $G(x)\mid E(x)$. So:

  • all single-bit errors, if $G$ has at least two terms
  • all odd-numbered-bit errors, if $(x+1)\mid G$
  • all burst errors shorter than $r$, since $G\nmid x^k\cdot(\text{short polynomial})$

A sum, by contrast, catches none of Neha's transpositions, because it never sees position at all.

Hamming codes

The $(7,4)$ Hamming code sends 4 data bits with 3 parity bits. With generator $G$ and parity-check matrix $H$ over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$:

$$c = mG, \qquad s = Hc^{T}$$

The syndrome $s$ is zero for a valid codeword, and for a single-bit error in position $i$ it equals column $i$ of $H$ — so arranging the columns as the binary numbers $1..7$ makes the syndrome literally spell out the error position. Minimum distance 3: corrects 1 error, detects 2. In general a code with minimum distance $d$ corrects $\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor$ errors.

Homomorphisms

A map $\varphi:G\to H$ is a homomorphism when $\varphi(a\ast b)=\varphi(a)\circ\varphi(b)$. It is an isomorphism when also bijective, and then the two groups are the same object with relabelled elements.

The kernel $\ker\varphi=\{a:\varphi(a)=e_H\}$ is a normal subgroup, and the first isomorphism theorem says $G/\ker\varphi\cong\mathrm{im}\,\varphi$. Concretely: $\exp:(\mathbb{R},+)\to(\mathbb{R}^{>0},\times)$ is an isomorphism, which is why logarithms turn multiplication into addition — and it is the model for what the discrete logarithm would be if it were easy.

Discrete log, Diffie-Hellman, Shamir, Schnorr

In $\mathbb{Z}_p^\*$ with generator $g$, computing $g^a\bmod p$ is fast (square-and-multiply, $O(\log a)$) while recovering $a$ from $g^a$ — the discrete logarithm — has no known polynomial algorithm. That asymmetry is the whole foundation.

Diffie-Hellman. Alice sends $A=g^a$, Bob sends $B=g^b$; both compute $g^{ab}$ as $B^a=A^b$. An eavesdropper sees $g,p,g^a,g^b$ and must solve discrete log (or the Diffie-Hellman problem) to get $g^{ab}$.

Shamir's secret sharing. Hide a secret $s$ as the constant term of a random degree-$(t-1)$ polynomial over $\mathrm{GF}(p)$; hand out points. Any $t$ shares determine the polynomial by Lagrange interpolation, and recover $s=f(0)$. Any $t-1$ shares leave every value of $s$ equally likely — information-theoretic security, not computational. This is M37's entropy claim made concrete: the conditional entropy of the secret given $t-1$ shares is exactly its prior entropy.

Schnorr identification (zero-knowledge). Peggy proves she knows $x$ with $y=g^x$ without revealing $x$:

  1. Commit: pick random $r$, send $t=g^r$
  2. Challenge: Victor sends random $c$
  3. Respond: Peggy sends $s=r+cx \bmod q$
  4. Verify: check $g^s = t\cdot y^c$

It is sound because anyone who can answer two different challenges on the same $t$ reveals $x$ (subtract the two responses). It is zero-knowledge because a simulator who knows $c$ in advance can produce an identically distributed transcript with no knowledge of $x$ at all — pick $s$ at random and set $t=g^sy^{-c}$. Nothing in the transcript can therefore carry information about $x$.

A pinch of history

Évariste Galois invented group theory to settle which polynomials are solvable by radicals, and wrote much of it down the night before the duel that killed him at 20 in 1832. Lagrange's theorem predates the group concept. Finite fields are called Galois fields for him.

Richard Hamming, frustrated by weekend batch jobs that crashed on a parity error and discarded everything, built the first error-correcting code in 1950. W. Wesley Peterson described CRCs in 1961. Diffie and Hellman published public key exchange in 1976 — Clifford Cocks and Malcolm Williamson had found equivalent ideas at GCHQ a few years earlier but could not publish. Adi Shamir's secret sharing is from 1979, Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff defined zero-knowledge in 1985, and Claus Schnorr's protocol is from 1989.

Why it's true

Result 1: Lagrange's theorem. Let $H\le G$. For $a\in G$ the left coset $aH=\{ah:h\in H\}$ has three properties:

  • Every element is in some coset, since $a=ae\in aH$.
  • Cosets are disjoint or identical. If $c\in aH\cap bH$ then $c=ah_1=bh_2$, so $a=bh_2h_1^{-1}$, and then $aH=bh_2h_1^{-1}H=bH$ since $h_2h_1^{-1}H=H$.
  • All cosets have the same size, because $h\mapsto ah$ is a bijection $H\to aH$ (its inverse is multiplication by $a^{-1}$).

So the cosets partition $G$ into equal-sized blocks of size $|H|$. Hence $|G|=[G:H]\cdot|H|$ and $|H|$ divides $|G|$. Applying this to $H=\langle a\rangle$ gives $\mathrm{ord}(a)\mid|G|$, hence $a^{|G|}=e$ — and that is Fermat's little theorem, which needed no number theory at all.

Result 2: $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is a field iff $n$ is prime. If $n=ab$ with $1<a,b<n$ then $a\cdot b\equiv0$ while $a,b\ne0$. Were $a$ invertible, $b=a^{-1}(ab)=a^{-1}\cdot0=0$, a contradiction — so $a$ has no inverse. Conversely if $n=p$ is prime and $a\not\equiv0$, then $\gcd(a,p)=1$, so Bézout gives $ua+vp=1$, i.e. $ua\equiv1\pmod p$: $u$ is the inverse. Exactly the same argument, with "irreducible" for "prime", shows $\mathrm{GF}(2)[x]/(f)$ is a field iff $f$ is irreducible.

Result 3: why the Hamming syndrome names the error position. Let $H$ be the $3\times7$ matrix whose $i$-th column is the binary representation of $i$. A codeword satisfies $Hc^T=0$. If a single error flips position $i$, the received word is $c+e_i$, so

$$H(c+e_i)^T = Hc^T + He_i^T = 0 + (\text{column } i) = \text{binary}(i)$$

The syndrome is the position, read as a binary numeral. This works because the columns are distinct and non-zero, which is also exactly the condition for minimum distance 3: any two columns are different, so no two single-bit errors share a syndrome.

Result 4: Shamir's scheme is information-theoretically secure. Given $t-1$ points of a degree-$(t-1)$ polynomial over $\mathrm{GF}(p)$, take any candidate secret $s'$. The $t-1$ points plus $(0,s')$ are $t$ points with distinct $x$-coordinates, and over a field there is exactly one polynomial of degree $<t$ through $t$ such points (Vandermonde is invertible). So every $s'$ is consistent with exactly one polynomial, and all are equally likely. The posterior equals the prior — no computational assumption anywhere, which is what separates this from Diffie-Hellman.

Result 5: Schnorr is sound and reveals nothing.

Soundness. Suppose a prover answers two challenges $c_1\ne c_2$ on the same commitment $t$, with responses $s_1,s_2$. Both verify, so $g^{s_1}=ty^{c_1}$ and $g^{s_2}=ty^{c_2}$. Dividing, $g^{s_1-s_2}=y^{c_1-c_2}$, so $y=g^{(s_1-s_2)(c_1-c_2)^{-1}}$ and the secret is $x=(s_1-s_2)(c_1-c_2)^{-1}\bmod q$. Anyone who can answer two challenges knows $x$.

Zero knowledge. A simulator that gets to pick $c$ first chooses $s$ uniformly, sets $t=g^sy^{-c}$, and outputs $(t,c,s)$. This verifies by construction, and $(t,c,s)$ has exactly the same distribution as a real transcript — in both, $s$ is uniform and $t$ is determined by $s$ and $c$. Since the transcript can be produced without $x$, it cannot contain information about $x$.

Oracle lab

The oracle verifies structure by exhaustive check rather than by assertion.

Group axioms are confirmed over every triple. Lagrange's theorem is checked by enumerating every subgroup of several groups and confirming each order divides. Field axioms for $\mathrm{GF}(2^4)$ are verified against brute-force inverse search. The CRC is computed two independent ways — bitwise shift-and-XOR, and honest polynomial long division over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ — which must agree bit for bit.

The mutant uses a reducible polynomial as the $\mathrm{GF}(2^4)$ modulus, which still gives closure and associativity but silently destroys the field.

def test_homomorphisms_and_isomorphisms():
    Z6 = (list(range(6)), lambda a, b: (a + b) % 6)
    Z3 = (list(range(3)), lambda a, b: (a + b) % 3)
    Z2 = (list(range(2)), lambda a, b: (a + b) % 2)
    # reduction mod 3 is a homomorphism Z6 -> Z3
    phi = lambda a: a % 3
    assert is_homomorphism(phi, Z6[0], Z6[1], Z3[1])
    assert kernel(phi, Z6[0], 0) == {0, 3}
    # the first isomorphism theorem: |G| / |ker| = |image|
    assert len(Z6[0]) // len(kernel(phi, Z6[0], 0)) == 3
    # it is onto but not injective, so not an isomorphism
    assert not is_isomorphism(phi, Z6[0], Z6[1], Z3[0], Z3[1])
    # Z6 IS isomorphic to Z7* via 3^k, which is the discrete log in reverse
    Z7s = (list(range(1, 7)), lambda a, b: (a * b) % 7)
    psi = lambda k: pow(3, k, 7)
    assert is_homomorphism(psi, Z6[0], Z6[1], Z7s[1])
    assert is_isomorphism(psi, Z6[0], Z6[1], Z7s[0], Z7s[1])
    assert kernel(psi, Z6[0], 1) == {0}, "an isomorphism has trivial kernel"
    # a map that is not a homomorphism
    assert not is_homomorphism(lambda a: (a + 1) % 3, Z6[0], Z6[1], Z3[1])
    # parity is a homomorphism Z6 -> Z2
    assert is_homomorphism(lambda a: a % 2, Z6[0], Z6[1], Z2[1])
    assert kernel(lambda a: a % 2, Z6[0], 0) == {0, 2, 4}

        assert gf2_mul(a, 0, MOD, BITS) == 0
        for b in range(16):
            assert gf2_mul(a, b, MOD, BITS) == gf2_mul(b, a, MOD, BITS)
    for a in range(16):
        for b in range(16):
            for c in range(4):
                left = gf2_mul(gf2_mul(a, b, MOD, BITS), c, MOD, BITS)
                right = gf2_mul(a, gf2_mul(b, c, MOD, BITS), MOD, BITS)
                assert left == right, (a, b, c)
    # distributivity
    for a in range(16):
        for b in range(16):
            for c in range(4):
                assert gf2_mul(a, gf2_add(b, c), MOD, BITS) == \
                    gf2_add(gf2_mul(a, b, MOD, BITS), gf2_mul(a, c, MOD, BITS))
    try:
        gf2_mul(1, 1, 0b1011, 4)
        assert False, "a modulus of the wrong degree must raise ValueError"
    except ValueError:

def test_irreducibility_decides_whether_it_is_a_field():
    # x^4+x+1 and x^4+x^3+1 are irreducible; x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1 is not
    assert is_irreducible_gf2(0b10011, 4) is True
    assert is_irreducible_gf2(0b11001, 4) is True
    # x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1 LOOKS like it should factor and does not: it has no
    # root in GF(2) (five ones sum to 1) and is not the square of x^2+x+1.
    assert is_irreducible_gf2(0b11111, 4) is True
    assert is_irreducible_gf2(0b10101, 4) is False     # (x^2+x+1)^2
    assert is_irreducible_gf2(0b10001, 4) is False     # (x+1)^4
    assert is_irreducible_gf2(0b111, 2) is True        # x^2+x+1
    assert is_irreducible_gf2(0b100, 2) is False       # x^2
    assert is_irreducible_gf2(0b100011011, 8) is True  # the AES polynomial
    # with an irreducible modulus every non-zero element inverts
    for mod in (0b10011, 0b11001, 0b11111):
        for a in range(1, 16):
            b = gf2_inverse(a, mod, 4)
            assert gf2_mul(a, b, mod, 4) == 1, (mod, a, b)
    # with a REDUCIBLE one, some element has no inverse at all
    missing = []
    for a in range(1, 16):
        try:
            gf2_inverse(a, 0b10101, 4)
        except ValueError:
            missing.append(a)
    assert missing == [7, 9, 14], missing
    # 7 is x^2+x+1, and 7 * 7 = 0 in this ring: a zero divisor, so no inverse
    assert gf2_mul(7, 7, 0b10101, 4) == 0, "a reducible modulus creates zero divisors"
    try:
        gf2_inverse(0, 0b10011, 4)
        assert False, "zero has no inverse"
    except ValueError:
        pass

def test_crc_catches_what_a_sum_cannot():
    import random
    GEN = 0b100000111
    rng = random.Random(1961)
    r = GEN.bit_length() - 1

    def word_for(data):
        bits = bytes_to_bits(data)
        c = crc(bits, GEN)
        return bits + [(c >> (r - 1 - i)) & 1 for i in range(r)]

    # EVERY single-bit error is caught: that is a theorem, not a statistic
    data = [rng.randrange(256) for _ in range(8)]
    w = word_for(data)
    for pos in range(len(w)):
        bad = list(w)
        bad[pos] ^= 1
        assert crc_check(bad, GEN) is False, pos
    # transpositions of bytes are caught, where a sum sees nothing
    caught = 0
    for _ in range(200):
        d = [rng.randrange(256) for _ in range(8)]
        i, j = 0, 1
        while d[i] == d[j]:
            i, j = rng.randrange(8), rng.randrange(8)
        swapped = list(d)
        swapped[i], swapped[j] = swapped[j], swapped[i]
        assert sum(swapped) == sum(d), "a sum is blind to this"
        if crc(bytes_to_bits(swapped), GEN) != crc(bytes_to_bits(d), GEN):
            caught += 1
    assert caught >= 198, caught
    # every burst shorter than the degree is caught
    for _ in range(200):
        d = [rng.randrange(256) for _ in range(8)]
        w = word_for(d)
        start = rng.randrange(len(w) - r)
        bad = list(w)
        pattern = rng.randrange(1, 1 << (r - 1))
        for k in range(r - 1):
            bad[start + k] ^= (pattern >> k) & 1
        if bad != w:
            assert crc_check(bad, GEN) is False

            Misconceptions
              

            • "A wider checksum is a stronger checksum." Not against the errors it is structurally blind to. Neha's sum misses every transposition at 16 bits, at 32, and at 1024, because the failure is commutativity, not width.
              • 

            • "CRC is a hash, so it is a bit like a cryptographic one." CRC is linear: $\mathrm{CRC}(a\oplus b)=\mathrm{CRC}(a)\oplus\mathrm{CRC}(b)$. That makes it excellent against random noise and useless against an adversary, who can adjust the message to keep any CRC fixed.
              • 

            • "Addition in GF(2^n) is addition." It is XOR. There are no carries, every element is its own additive inverse, and $a+a=0$.
              • 

            • "Any polynomial works as the GF(2^n) modulus." It must be irreducible, for exactly the reason $\mathbb{Z}_n$ needs $n$ prime: a reducible modulus creates zero divisors and the inverses vanish.
              • 

            • "Lagrange's theorem says every divisor of |G| is the order of some subgroup." The converse is false. $A_4$ has order 12 and no subgroup of order 6. Lagrange constrains; it does not construct.
              • 

            • "Diffie-Hellman gives you authentication." It gives a shared secret with someone. Without authentication that someone may be a machine-in-the-middle running two exchanges.
              • 

            • "Zero knowledge means the verifier learns nothing at all." The verifier learns the statement is true. What is proved is that they learn nothing beyond that — formalised by the existence of a simulator.
              • 

            • "Shamir's secret sharing is computationally secure." It is information-theoretically secure: with $t-1$ shares every secret remains exactly as likely as before. No amount of computation helps, which is strictly stronger than what Diffie-Hellman offers.
              • 

            • "Hamming codes correct any two errors because the distance is 3." Distance $d$ corrects $\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor=1$. Two errors are detected but silently mis-corrected.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            CRC's linearity is its fatal flaw against adversaries, and it was exactly how WEP was broken: the integrity check was a CRC, so an attacker could flip ciphertext bits and patch the CRC to match. Use a MAC when an adversary is in the picture.
            

            The textbook Schnorr and Diffie-Hellman here are deliberately minimal. Real deployments need: authenticated public values, a prime-order subgroup (otherwise small-subgroup attacks apply), and a never-reused nonce. The $r$-reuse extraction in the boss exercise is not a theoretical concern — it is how Sony's PlayStation 3 ECDSA signing key was recovered in 2010, and the same bug has appeared in Bitcoin wallets.
            

            Brute-force discrete log is here for illustration only. Baby-step giant-step runs in $O(\sqrt{p})$ and the number field sieve in sub-exponential time, so real parameters need thousands of bits — and Shor's algorithm breaks all of it on a sufficiently large quantum computer, which is why post-quantum schemes move to lattices rather than groups.
            

            Shamir's scheme needs genuinely random coefficients from a cryptographic source, and it provides no verifiability: a malicious participant can submit a wrong share and corrupt the reconstruction undetectably. Verifiable secret sharing exists for that reason.
            

            More broadly, finite fields are the easy case. Infinite rings, non-commutative rings and modules all behave differently, and most of the convenient facts used here — unique factorisation, every non-zero element invertible, a cyclic multiplicative group — are specific to finite fields.
            In your real work
            Every storage and network layer you use runs on this. Ethernet, PCIe, USB, SATA and ZIP all carry CRCs. RAID-6 and every erasure-coded object store use Reed-Solomon over $\mathrm{GF}(2^8)$. ECC memory uses Hamming-family codes to correct single-bit errors in place, and QR codes survive being partly obscured because of Reed-Solomon.
            

            AES is $\mathrm{GF}(2^8)$ throughout: MixColumns is multiplication by a fixed polynomial, and the S-box is the multiplicative inverse in that field followed by an affine map. "Why is AES hard to break?" has an algebraic answer.
            

            Group structure shows up where people do not expect it. A CRDT's merge operation must be a commutative monoid, or replicas diverge. Hash table probing sequences are generated by a group action. Rubik's cube solvers are group theory with a search on top, and the symmetry group of a crystal or a molecule determines its spectrum.
            

            And in a code review, two questions from this module pay for themselves repeatedly. What operation is this, and is it commutative? — because if order matters to correctness and the check is commutative, the check is blind. And is there an adversary? — because CRC against noise is excellent engineering and CRC against an attacker is a vulnerability.
            Flash cards
            What are the four group axioms?
            Closure, associativity, an identity, and an inverse for every element. Commutativity is NOT required — abelian groups are the special case.
            State Lagrange's theorem and one consequence.
            The order of any subgroup divides the order of the group. Hence the order of every element divides |G|, so a^|G| = e — which IS Fermat's little theorem in Z_p*.
            Why does Lagrange's theorem hold?
            The left cosets of H partition G into disjoint blocks all of size |H|, because x -> ax is a bijection from H onto aH.
            When is Z_n a field?
            Exactly when n is prime. A composite modulus has zero divisors, and a zero divisor can never be invertible.
            What are addition and multiplication in GF(2^n)?
            Addition is XOR of the bit patterns, with no carries. Multiplication is polynomial multiplication reduced modulo an IRREDUCIBLE polynomial of degree n.
            What is a CRC, algebraically?
            The remainder of x^r M(x) divided by a generator polynomial G(x) over GF(2). An error E(x) goes undetected exactly when G(x) divides E(x).
            Why does a plain checksum miss transpositions, whatever its width?
            Because addition is commutative, so the sum cannot see order. It is a structural blindness, not a shortage of bits.
            Why does the Hamming syndrome identify the error position?
            The columns of H are the binary numerals 1..7, and a single error at position i gives syndrome equal to column i — the position written in binary.
            What does a code of minimum distance d guarantee?
            It corrects floor((d-1)/2) errors and detects d-1. Distance 3 corrects one and detects two.
            What is a homomorphism, and what is its kernel?
            A map preserving the operation: phi(ab) = phi(a)phi(b). The kernel is what maps to the identity; it is a normal subgroup, and G/ker is isomorphic to the image.
            Why is Shamir's secret sharing information-theoretically secure?
            With t-1 shares, every candidate secret is consistent with exactly one polynomial of degree < t, so all remain equally likely. The posterior equals the prior, whatever computation is available.
            Why is Schnorr zero-knowledge, and what breaks it?
            A simulator who knows the challenge first can produce an identically distributed transcript without the secret, so the transcript carries no information. Reusing the commitment r with two different challenges reveals x outright.
            Stop when
            You can state the group axioms and give a non-abelian example, you can prove Lagrange's theorem from the coset partition, you can say why $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is a field exactly for prime $n$ and why the same argument needs "irreducible" for $\mathrm{GF}(2^n)$, you can compute in $\mathrm{GF}(2^8)$ by hand, you can explain a CRC as polynomial division and say precisely which errors it must catch, you can decode a Hamming word by reading the syndrome as a position, you can explain why Neha's checksum was doomed whatever its width, you can explain why Shamir is stronger than Diffie-Hellman in the security it offers, and you pass the boss exercise without hints.
