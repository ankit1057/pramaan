The story

In the old Prussian city of Königsberg, the river split around two islands, and seven bridges joined the four pieces of land. The Sunday game was to find a walk that crossed every bridge exactly once.

Everybody tried. Nobody managed it. The townspeople assumed they were not being clever enough.

Leonhard Euler, in 1736, did something that looks like giving up. He threw away the city. He said the shape of the islands does not matter, the length of the bridges does not matter, and the distances do not matter. What matters is only: four pieces of land, and which pairs are joined, and how many times.

He drew four dots and seven lines.

Then he made the observation that ended the game. Think about any piece of land that is not where you start or finish. Every time you walk into it you must later walk out of it, on a different bridge. So its bridges get used in pairs — which means it must have an even number of bridges.

He counted. All four pieces of land had an odd number. Not one of them, all four. So at most two could be allowed (the start and the end), and there were four.

Here is the surprise: he did not fail to find a route. He proved that no route exists, for anyone, forever — and he did it by deleting almost all the information. The question was never about Königsberg. It was about four dots and seven lines, and the answer took one paragraph about parity.

Say it simply

A graph is dots (vertices) and lines (edges). That is the whole definition, and its emptiness is the point: anything that is "things, and connections between them" is a graph, so one theorem serves a road network, a social network, a dependency tree and a state machine.

The degree of a vertex is how many edges touch it. Euler's parity argument generalises into a complete answer for which graphs can be traversed edge-by-edge without repetition.

The second half of the module is about counting things that are defined in terms of themselves. A recurrence says what the current answer is in terms of earlier ones; solving it means finding a closed form you can evaluate in one step instead of calculating every step. Generating functions are the machine for doing that: you pack the whole infinite sequence into one algebraic object, do algebra on it, and read the answer back out.

Both halves meet in the Catalan numbers, which count an astonishing variety of structures and which we will compute three independent ways.

Say it precisely

Graphs

Imagine a map showing islands (we call them vertices or dots) and bridges between them (we call them edges or lines). A graph is just that collection of dots and lines.

If there are no bridges from an island back to itself, and no two bridges connecting the exact same pair of islands, it's a simple graph.

The degree of an island is just how many bridges touch it.

Handshake lemma: If you count the bridges touching every island and add them all up, you will get exactly double the total number of bridges. Why? Because every bridge has two ends! It's like counting people by counting their hands: you'll always get an even number. Because of this, the number of islands with an odd number of bridges must always be even.

A walk is jumping from island to island using the bridges. A path is a walk where you never visit the same island twice. A cycle is a path that loops back to where you started. If you can walk to any island from any other island, the graph is connected.

A tree is a connected graph with absolutely no cycles (no loops). If you have a tree with a certain number of islands, you always have exactly one fewer bridge than islands. If you cut any bridge, the tree splits into two disconnected pieces.

Traversals

An Euler circuit is like a snowplow that must clear every single bridge (edge) exactly once and return to its garage. An Euler path clears every bridge exactly once but finishes somewhere else.

The rule: You can only have an Euler circuit if every single island has an even number of bridges. You can have an Euler path if exactly zero or two islands have an odd number of bridges.

A Hamiltonian path/cycle is different. It's like a mailman trying to visit every single island (vertex) exactly once. Even though the rule for the snowplow (Euler) is incredibly easy to check, the rule for the mailman (Hamiltonian) is one of the hardest problems in math (called NP-complete). Changing the goal from "visit every bridge" to "visit every island" changes it from a child's puzzle to a supercomputer's nightmare.

Bipartite and colouring

Imagine trying to paint every island so that no two islands connected by a bridge share the same colour.

If you can do this using exactly two colours (like black and white), the graph is bipartite. This only works if your graph has no "odd cycles" (no loops of 3, 5, or 7 bridges), because an odd loop always forces you to use a third colour.

The chromatic number is the minimum number of colours you need to paint the whole graph safely.

Planarity

A graph is planar if you can draw it on a flat piece of paper without any lines crossing over each other.

For these flat drawings, there's a magical rule called Euler's formula: If you count the islands (the number of islands), subtract the bridges (the number of bridges), and add the enclosed regions or "faces" (the number of faces, including the big ocean outside), it always equals 2: Islands minus bridges plus faces always equals 2.

Because roads can't cross, a planar graph can't have too many edges. In fact, every planar graph can be painted safely using at most 4 colours. This was a famous puzzle (the Four Colour Theorem) that was finally solved in 1976 using a computer!

Recurrences

A recurrence is a rule for building a sequence of numbers, where today's number depends on yesterday's numbers. It's like a recipe: "To know how many cookies we bake on any given day, add the cookies from the day before and two days before."

This specific cookie recipe gives the Fibonacci numbers: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, and so on.

But what if you want to know how many cookies you'll bake on day 100, without calculating all 99 days before it? Mathematicians use a trick: they guess the answer looks like multiplying by a special number every day. When they plug this guess into the cookie recipe, it creates a "characteristic equation". Solving that equation gives a magic formula (like Binet's formula for Fibonacci) that lets you jump straight to day 100 in one step using the golden ratio!

Generating functions

Imagine you have an endless sequence of numbers, like the cookies baked every day. A generating function is like an infinite train, where the first carriage holds day 0's cookies, the second holds day 1's, and so on.

Instead of looking at the numbers one by one, we pack them all into a single mathematical box (a "formal power series") that looks like this: Train = Box 0 + Box 1 + Box 2 + Box 3 and so on. The tag isn't a number we care about; it's just a tag on the train carriage to keep everything in order.

The magic is that we can do algebra on the entire train at once. We can add trains, multiply them, or squish them into neat little fractions. For example, if every carriage holds the number 1, the whole infinite train squishes down to just 1 / (1 - x). By doing algebra on these squished formulas, we can easily find the rule for any number in the sequence.

Catalan numbers

The Catalan numbers (1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, and so on) are like a master key that unlocks dozens of different counting puzzles.

They count: - How many ways you can arrange parentheses so they always match: ()(), (()) - How many ways you can slice a polygon into triangles. - How many paths a bug can take on a grid without ever crossing the diagonal.

Even though these puzzles sound completely different, they are all the exact same problem wearing different disguises!

A pinch of history

Euler's 1736 Königsberg paper is usually called the first in graph theory, and his polyhedron formula Islands - Bridges + Faces = 2 came in 1750–1752. Hamilton's "Icosian game" (1857) gave the Hamiltonian cycle its name. Kuratowski's planarity characterisation is from 1930. The four-colour theorem, conjectured in 1852, was finally proved by Appel and Haken in 1976 using extensive computer case-checking, which caused a genuine philosophical argument about what a proof is.

The Catalan numbers are named for Eugène Catalan (1838) but are older: Minggatu, a Mongolian mathematician working in China, used them in the 1730s in series expansions, and Euler found them while counting triangulations of polygons.

The Fibonacci recurrence appears in India well before Fibonacci's 1202 Liber Abaci: Virahanka (c. 600–800 CE) derived it while counting the ways to compose a metre of a certain number of beats from syllables of length 1 and 2, and the rule is also attributed to Gopala and to Hemachandra (1150).

Why it's true

Result 1: the handshake lemma. Imagine every bridge has two ends, right? If you ask everyone on every island to stand by the end of their bridge and wave a flag, how many flags will you see? If you count by island, you add up the number of bridges at each island. If you count by bridges, you see exactly two flags per bridge (one at each end). Because you're counting the exact same flags, the two ways of counting must be equal! So, adding up all the connections equals double the number of bridges.

Result 2: Euler's circuit condition. Why do you need an even number of bridges for a snowplow to clear everything and come home? Imagine you are the snowplow. Every time you enter an island, you clear one bridge. To leave that island, you must clear a different bridge. You always use bridges in pairs: an "in" bridge and an "out" bridge. Even the start island works this way: you use an "out" bridge at the very beginning and an "in" bridge at the very end. So, every island's bridges get paired up perfectly. That means every island must have an even number of bridges!

Result 3: a tree on any number of vertices has exactly one fewer edge. Imagine building a tree from scratch. 1. You start with 1 island. It has 0 bridges. (1 island, 1-1=0 bridges). 2. To add a new island to your tree, you build exactly 1 bridge to connect it to the old islands. 3. You can't build 2 bridges to the new island, because that would create a loop (a cycle), and trees aren't allowed to have loops! 4. So, every time you add 1 island, you add exactly 1 bridge. If you do this until you have a certain number of islands, you will have built exactly one fewer bridge.

Result 4: bipartite iff no odd cycle. Why can't you paint an "odd cycle" (like a triangle of 3 islands) with just two colours? Imagine painting island A black. The next island in the loop, B, must be white. The next, C, must be black. But wait! If it's a triangle, C connects back to A! Now C is black and A is black, and they share a bridge. Boom, the rules are broken! In any odd-numbered loop, the alternating colours will always clash when they meet at the end. That's why a 2-colourable map can never have odd cycles.

Result 5: solving a linear recurrence. Why does guessing a multiplier solve our cookie recipe (recurrence)? Because in a "linear" recipe, we only ever add previous days together or multiply them by constant numbers. This perfectly matches how exponents work. If we guess that the sequence grows by multiplying by that number every step, the recipe turns into a simple algebra equation. Once we find the magic number that solves the equation, we've solved the whole infinite sequence! It's not a magic trick; it's just finding the natural speed limit of the recipe.

Oracle lab

The oracle computes the same quantities two or three genuinely independent ways.

For Fibonacci: iterating the recurrence (pure addition, exact integers) against Binet's closed form (irrational arithmetic with the square root of five). Nothing is shared; they agree only if the characteristic-root analysis is right.

For Catalan numbers: the convolution recurrence, the binomial closed form, and a brute-force count of balanced bracket strings — three routes, one combinatorial, one algebraic, one by enumeration.

For trees: the edge count against a direct cycle check and connectivity check.

Theory makes a sharp, checkable claim about Euler traversals: a connected graph has an Euler circuit exactly when every degree is even, and an Euler path exactly when zero or two degrees are odd. That is a prediction about every graph, so we test it by brute force — exhaustively searching for a traversal on many small graphs and comparing the search's verdict with the degree test's.

    try:
        series_product([1], [1], 0)
        assert False, "zero terms must raise ValueError"
    except ValueError:
        pass

            

            Misconceptions
              

            • "Euler and Hamiltonian problems are similar." Euler is about edges and has a one-line criterion decidable in linear time; Hamiltonian is about vertices and is NP-complete. The superficial similarity of the statements hides a vast computational gap.
              • 

            • "A connected graph with all even degrees might still have no Euler circuit." The condition is sufficient as well as necessary — that is the content of the theorem, and the experiment verified it by exhaustive search.
              • 

            • "Bipartite means the two sides are the same size." It means every edge runs between the sides. A star with one centre and nine leaves is bipartite with sides of size 1 and 9.
              • 

            • "A tree is a graph that looks like a tree." A tree is connected and acyclic, and those two conditions plus any one of "has one fewer edge than vertices" determine the other. A disconnected acyclic graph is a forest, not a tree.
              • 

            • "Any graph can be drawn without crossings if you are clever." The complete graph on 5 vertices and the complete bipartite graph on 3 and 3 vertices cannot, and Kuratowski's theorem says they are the only obstructions.
              • 

            • "A generating function must converge." It is a formal power series: the variable is a placeholder and convergence is irrelevant to the algebra. The sum of factorials converges nowhere and is still a perfectly good generating function.
              • 

            • "Binet's formula is the fast way to compute Fibonacci." It is O(1) arithmetic but in floating point, and it loses accuracy for large n — beyond about step 70 a computer cannot represent the Fibonacci number exactly. Fast exact computation uses matrix exponentiation or fast doubling.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Everything in the graph half assumed undirected, unweighted, simple graphs. Directed graphs need in-degree and out-degree, and the Euler condition becomes "in-degree equals out-degree at every vertex, and the graph is strongly connected on its non-isolated part". Weighted graphs bring shortest paths and minimum spanning trees. Multigraphs allow parallel edges, which Königsberg genuinely has.
            

            The characteristic-root method needs constant coefficients and linearity. A recurrence such as the current term is the square of the previous term plus 1, or one with coefficients depending on the step number, is outside the method entirely, and most such recurrences have no closed form at all.
            

            Repeated roots change the form: a sequence with repeated roots has the double root 1, and the solution includes the step number. The exercise deliberately refuses that case rather than returning a wrong answer. Complex roots are fine mathematically — they produce oscillating solutions via M19 — but the exercise's real-arithmetic implementation cannot represent them.
            

            Numerically, Binet's formula fails exactly where you would want it: for a large number of steps, the exponentiation overflows and the rounding error exceeds 1 before that. And the brute-force bracket enumeration in the demo grows exponentially, so it stops being usable around step 10 — the dynamic-programming version in the exercise is the one that scales.
            In your real work
            Graphs are the natural model for dependency resolution (a build graph, a package manager, an import graph), and "is there a cycle" is the question behind every circular-dependency error. Topological sort — ordering a DAG so every edge points forwards — is the scheduling algorithm behind build systems and task runners.
            

            Bipartite detection by 2-colouring is matching: assigning jobs to workers, requests to servers. Graph colouring is register allocation in a compiler, and channel assignment in wireless. Connectivity and components are how you find isolated subsystems in a service mesh.
            

            Recurrences are the running time of recursive algorithms: the time for mergesort grows in proportion to n log n. The Catalan numbers count the shapes a binary tree can take, which is why they appear in parser and expression-tree analysis.
            

            And the Euler-versus-Hamilton contrast is the most practically important idea here. Before writing a search, ask whether a structural theorem decides the question — because the difference between a parity check and an exponential search is the difference between a feature and an outage.
            Flash cards
            State the handshake lemma and one immediate corollary.
            The degrees sum to twice the number of edges. Therefore the number of odd-degree vertices is always even.
            When does a connected graph have an Euler circuit, and when an Euler path?
            A circuit exactly when every degree is even; a path exactly when zero or two vertices have odd degree.
            Why did Euler's argument settle Königsberg?
            All four landmasses had odd degree, and at most two odd vertices are permitted, so no traversal exists for anyone.
            How do Euler and Hamiltonian problems differ computationally?
            Euler is decided in linear time by a degree check; deciding Hamiltonicity is NP-complete.
            Give three equivalent definitions of a tree.
            Connected and acyclic; connected with n-1 edges; exactly one path between every pair of vertices.
            What characterises a bipartite graph?
            It is 2-colourable, equivalently it contains no odd cycle.
            State Euler's formula for planar graphs and one consequence.
            V - E + F = 2 for a connected planar drawing. A consequence is E <= 3V - 6, which rules out K5.
            How do you solve a linear recurrence with constant coefficients?
            Substitute r^n to get the characteristic equation, find its roots, and fit a combination of r_i^n to the initial conditions.
            What does "formal" mean in formal power series?
            Convergence is irrelevant. The series is a container for the sequence, and x is bookkeeping, so divergent series are perfectly usable.
            What do the Catalan numbers count, and what is the closed form?
            Balanced bracket strings, binary trees, polygon triangulations and much else. C_n = C(2n,n)/(n+1).
            Stop when
            You can explain Euler's Königsberg argument to a child, you can prove the handshake lemma by double counting and the n-1 edge count by induction, you can decide Euler traversability and bipartiteness by hand and say why neither has a Hamiltonian analogue, you can turn a linear recurrence into its characteristic equation and solve it, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you habitually ask whether a structural theorem can replace a search.
