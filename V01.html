The story

Imagine you have a big wooden box with a slit in the top, and your big sister tells you that inside the box lives a magical golden rabbit that turns dry grass into peppermint candy. Every morning you slip three blades of fresh green grass into the slot. Every afternoon you slide open the tiny door at the bottom, and there sits a wrapped peppermint candy. You test this on Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday. Does your five-day experiment prove that a magical golden rabbit lives inside the box?

A six-year-old might happily shout yes, but a careful thinker pauses. What if your father is quietly dropping peppermints into the box while you are eating lunch? What if the box contains a simple mechanical spring, or a clockwork dispenser loaded with fifty candies last week?

No matter how many times green grass goes in and peppermint candy comes out, you have only observed an association, not the rabbit. A million white swans cannot prove the rule that all swans are white, because tomorrow morning a black swan may paddle around the river bend in Australia. Deductive proof gives certainty inside a closed game with fixed rules, but the moment you step into the real world, you are dealing with theories, models, and evidence. You can never prove a theory true once and for all; you can only test it harder, try to break it, and see if it survives.

Say it simply

We seek knowledge in three distinct ways: deductive proof, empirical science, and mathematical modelling. In pure mathematics, a theorem is deduced from axioms and definitions with complete logical certainty. A single counterexample destroys a false conjecture.

In the physical world and software engineering, reality is not an axiomatic game. We cannot prove theories; we can only falsify them or gather evidence. A model is never reality itself—the map is not the territory. In computing, passing ten thousand unit tests does not prove a program has no bugs; it only proves it behaved correctly on those specific inputs.

Say it precisely

  1. The Game Rules (Axioms and Theorems): In a game of checkers, the rules are perfect. You never have to guess if a piece can move backwards—the rulebook (the Axiom) tells you exactly what is true! A Theorem is just a clever winning move you figure out by strictly following the rulebook.
  2. The Broken Promise (Counterexamples): If you promise "all apples are red" (a Conjecture), finding a billion red apples doesn't prove your promise is perfect. But finding just one single green apple (a Counterexample) instantly breaks the promise forever!
  3. The Fair Bet (Falsifiability): A good science rule must make a fair bet that can be tested. If you say "invisible magic pushes my car," we can't test that! But if you say "gasoline pushes my car," we can empty the gas tank and watch the car stop. Science must be testable.
  4. The Muddy Footprints (Abduction): If you see muddy dog footprints in the kitchen, it could be a burglar wearing fake dog shoes. But the smartest, best guess is just that a dog walked in!
  5. The Toy Map (Box's Dictum): A paper map of your town is not the real town. You can't live inside the paper map! "All maps are fake, but maps are still super useful for finding the library." We build models (maps) to understand the real world.

Why it's true

Why can't science ever be 100 percent perfectly sure about anything in the real world?

  1. The Unopened Boxes (The Inductive Gap): Math promises that every single box in the universe is perfectly empty. Science is like a person who opens 10 boxes, sees they are empty, and just hopes the rest are empty too. Even if you open a million boxes, the very next box might have a toy inside!
  2. The Bug Hunt: Testing a computer program is like searching a dark room for bugs with a flashlight. If you shine the flashlight in a corner and don't see a bug, that just proves there are no bugs in that one corner. It doesn't prove the room is completely bug-free!
  3. The Power of One (Falsification): To prove "all boxes are empty," you would have to open every box on Earth. That is completely impossible! But to prove the rule is broken, you only have to find ONE single box with a toy inside. Science is the endless hunt for that one broken box.
  4. The Bent Ruler (Model vs Calculation Error): Imagine measuring your height. If you add up the numbers perfectly in your head, your math is flawless (Zero Calculation Error). But if your wooden ruler was secretly bent in half the whole time, your final answer is still completely wrong (Model Error)! Science knows our rulers are always a little bit bent, so we can never measure the real world perfectly.

Experiment lab
Theory predicts that testing a finite number of random inputs can never guarantee the absence of bugs if the bug resides on a rare edge condition. We design an experiment where a function behaves correctly on 99.9% of inputs, but fails on a specific edge case. We simulate 500 independent testing campaigns of size 50. Theory predicts that the probability of missing the bug is about 95.12%. We measure the empirical miss rate across 500 campaigns and verify that the empirical rate lies within the theoretical 95% confidence interval.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "A passing test suite proves the software is correct." Tests sample a negligible fraction of the input space. Dijkstra's principle: testing can reveal the presence of defects, never their complete absence.
            • 

          • "Scientific theories become mathematical laws when proven." Scientific theories are never proven deductively; they are empirical models that remain open to future falsification.
            • 

          • "All models are equally valid opinions." While all models simplify reality, models differ vastly in predictive validity, empirical support, and falsifiable precision.
            • 

          • "If two metrics are highly correlated, one causes the other." Correlation does not entail causation. A latent confounding variable may drive both observed metrics.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Deductive reasoning breaks down when axioms fail to capture empirical reality (e.g., Euclidean geometry fails in curved relativistic spacetime). Empirical science breaks down when dealing with non-falsifiable claims (tautologies, unfalsifiable metaphysical assertions). In machine learning, training set validation breaks down under out-of-distribution domain shift where the empirical training distribution no longer models deployment reality.
          In your real work
          In mobile and systems development, engineers often assume API contracts are mathematical truths. In reality, OEM Android skins (Samsung One UI, Xiaomi MIUI) violate AOSP specifications by killing background services aggressively. Treating a platform API specification as a formal theorem rather than an empirical model leads to silent production crashes; real systems need defensive contracts and empirical telemetry.
          Flash cards
          What is the core epistemological difference between a mathematical theorem and a scientific theory?
          A theorem is deductively proven from axioms and cannot be falsified by empirical observation. A scientific theory is an empirical model supported by observations that remains fundamentally falsifiable.
          What is Hume's problem of induction?
          The logical impossibility of justifying general universal claims about unobserved instances based solely on finite past observations.
          What does George Box's aphorism "All models are wrong, but some are useful" mean?
          Every model is an abstraction that discards real-world complexity; its value lies not in literal physical identity with reality, but in useful predictive power.
          Why does Karl Popper argue that falsifiability, rather than verification, demarcates science?
          Because no finite number of confirming observations can prove a universal statement true, but a single counterexample can prove it false.
          What are the four stages of the mathematical modelling cycle?
          Formulate (abstract reality into math), Solve (calculate properties), Interpret (translate to real-world context), and Validate (compare against empirical observations).
          Why does passing 10,000 automated tests fail to equal a proof of program correctness?
          Tests evaluate only a finite subset of inputs; unless the input space is exhaustively checked or formally verified with invariants, untested inputs may contain defect-triggering states.
          Stop when
          You can clearly explain the difference between mathematical deduction and empirical falsification to a non-technical peer, pass the boss exercise without hints, and identify where a unit test suite makes unwarranted inductive assumptions about production inputs.
