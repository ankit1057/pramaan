The story

Nikhil's grandmother has a wall clock with twelve hours and no minute hand, and she uses it to teach him a game.

"It is three o'clock. What is it in twenty-five hours?"

He counts round on his fingers and lands on four. Then he notices he did not need to count: twenty-five is twice round the clock plus one, and the two whole loops do not matter. Only the leftover matters. He has invented remainder arithmetic on his own.

Then she asks the question that stops him. "Start at twelve. Which number can I multiply by five to land exactly on one?"

He tries. Five fives is twenty-five, which is one. It works.

"Now try with six instead of five."

Six, twelve, eighteen, twenty-four, thirty — on the clock those are six, twelve, six, twelve, six. Six and twelve, forever. He can never reach one. He tries every multiplier and none of them work.

"Why does five have a partner and six does not?"

Nikhil stares at the clock and eventually sees it. Six shares a factor with twelve — they are both even, and both divide by six. Five shares nothing with twelve. The numbers that have partners are exactly the ones with no common factor with twelve.

Here is the surprise: on a clock, division sometimes works and sometimes does not, and whether it works is decided entirely by a greatest common divisor. That single observation — which numbers are invertible modulo $n$ — is the foundation of RSA, of hash tables, of error-detecting codes on your bank card, and of most of modern cryptography.

Say it simply

Modular arithmetic is arithmetic on a clock: you work with remainders after dividing by a fixed modulus $n$, and multiples of $n$ vanish.

Addition, subtraction and multiplication all behave normally. Division is the interesting one. Dividing by $a$ means multiplying by its inverse — a number $b$ with $ab\equiv1$ — and such a $b$ exists exactly when $\gcd(a,n)=1$. That is Nikhil's discovery, and the extended Euclidean algorithm finds the inverse when it exists.

Then come the two theorems that make cryptography possible. Fermat's little theorem says $a^{p-1}\equiv1$ for a prime $p$; Euler's theorem generalises it using the totient function. Combined with fast modular exponentiation — which computes $a^k \bmod n$ in about $\log k$ multiplications instead of $k$ — they let you raise enormous numbers to enormous powers cheaply, which is exactly what RSA does.

Finally, primality testing: how to tell whether a 2048-bit number is prime without factoring it, and why the obvious test can be fooled.

Say it precisely

Congruences

$a\equiv b\pmod n$ means $n$ divides $a-b$. Congruence respects arithmetic:

$$a\equiv b,\ c\equiv d \implies a+c\equiv b+d,\quad a-c\equiv b-d,\quad ac\equiv bd \pmod n$$

Cancellation fails in general: $2\cdot3\equiv2\cdot 9\pmod{12}$ but $3\not\equiv9$. You may cancel $a$ only when $\gcd(a,n)=1$.

GCD, Bezout and inverses

The Euclidean algorithm uses $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,a\bmod b)$ with $\gcd(a,0)=a$.

Bezout's identity: for any integers $a,b$ there exist $x,y$ with

$$ax+by=\gcd(a,b)$$

The extended Euclidean algorithm produces $x$ and $y$ alongside the gcd.

Modular inverse. $a$ has an inverse modulo $n$ iff $\gcd(a,n)=1$. Then Bezout gives $ax+ny=1$, so $ax\equiv1\pmod n$ and $x\bmod n$ is the inverse. Nikhil's clock: $\gcd(5,12)=1$ so 5 is invertible ($5\cdot5=25\equiv1$), while $\gcd(6,12)=6$ so 6 is not.

The invertible residues modulo $n$ form a group under multiplication, of size $\varphi(n)$.

Euler's totient, Fermat and Euler

$\varphi(n)$ counts the integers in $1..n$ coprime to $n$. It is multiplicative for coprime arguments, and

$$\varphi(p)=p-1,\qquad \varphi(p^k)=p^k-p^{k-1},\qquad \varphi(n)=n\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac1p\right)$$

Fermat's little theorem. For prime $p$ and $p\nmid a$:

$$a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$$

Euler's theorem. For $\gcd(a,n)=1$:

$$a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n$$

Fermat is the special case $n=p$. These are what let RSA decrypt: exponents can be reduced modulo $\varphi(n)$.

Fast modular exponentiation

Square-and-multiply computes $a^k\bmod n$ using the binary expansion of $k$:

$$a^k = \prod_{\text{bits } b_i=1} a^{2^i}$$

requiring $O(\log k)$ multiplications, each on numbers below $n^2$. Computing $a^k$ first and then reducing is catastrophic — $2^{1000}$ has over three hundred digits — so the reduction must happen at every step.

The Chinese Remainder Theorem

If $n_1,\dots,n_k$ are pairwise coprime with product $N$, the system

$$x\equiv a_i \pmod{n_i}$$

has a unique solution modulo $N$. Constructively, with $N_i = N/n_i$ and $M_i$ the inverse of $N_i$ modulo $n_i$:

$$x \equiv \sum_i a_iN_iM_i \pmod N$$

Primality testing

Trial division tests divisors up to $\sqrt n$: correct, and hopeless beyond about 20 digits.

Fermat test. Pick a base $a$; if $a^{n-1}\not\equiv1\pmod n$ then $n$ is definitely composite. If it is $1$, $n$ is "probably prime" — but there are composites that pass for every base coprime to them. These are the Carmichael numbers; the smallest is $561 = 3\times11\times17$. Fermat's test cannot be fixed by trying more bases.

Miller-Rabin repairs it. Write $n-1 = 2^s d$ with $d$ odd. $n$ is a strong probable prime to base $a$ if $a^d\equiv1$ or $a^{2^rd}\equiv-1$ for some $0\le r<s$. At most $\frac14$ of bases can fool a composite, so $k$ random bases give error at most $4^{-k}$. For $n<3{,}215{,}031{,}751$, testing bases $2,3,5,7$ is deterministic.

Hashing and check digits

Hashing modulo a prime. A table size that is a power of two uses only the low bits of the hash, so keys differing in the high bits collide. A prime modulus mixes all the bits in. Polynomial string hashing, $h=\sum s_ix^i \bmod p$, is standard.

Luhn's algorithm (bank cards): from the right, double every second digit, subtract 9 if the result exceeds 9, sum everything; valid iff the total is $\equiv0\pmod{10}$. It catches all single-digit errors and almost all adjacent transpositions — but not $09\leftrightarrow90$. Verhoeff's scheme uses the dihedral group $D_5$ instead of modular addition and catches all single-digit errors and all adjacent transpositions; the Aadhaar number uses it.

A pinch of history

The Euclidean algorithm is in Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BCE). In India the same procedure is the kuttaka ("pulveriser") of Aryabhata (499 CE), developed for astronomical problems and used by Brahmagupta (628) and Bhaskara II to solve linear Diophantine equations — essentially Bezout's identity, a millennium before Bezout.

The Chinese Remainder Theorem appears in Sun Zi's Suanjing (3rd–5th century CE) and was generalised by Qin Jiushao in 1247; Aryabhata's kuttaka solves the same problems. Fermat stated his little theorem in a 1640 letter; Euler published the first proof in 1736 and the generalisation in 1763.

Carmichael numbers were found by Robert Carmichael in 1910, and it was proved only in 1994 (Alford, Granville and Pomerance) that there are infinitely many. The Miller-Rabin test is Gary Miller's (1976) and Michael Rabin's (1980). Hans Peter Luhn patented his checksum in 1960; Jacobus Verhoeff published his in 1969.

Why it's true

Result 1: $a$ is invertible modulo $n$ iff $\gcd(a,n)=1$.

If. Bezout gives $x,y$ with $ax+ny=1$. Reducing modulo $n$ kills the $ny$ term, leaving $ax\equiv1$.

Only if. Suppose $ax\equiv1\pmod n$, so $ax-1=kn$ for some integer $k$, that is $ax-kn=1$. Any common divisor of $a$ and $n$ divides the left side, hence divides 1, so the gcd is 1.

The non-obvious direction is the first, and it is entirely Bezout's doing: the identity constructs the inverse rather than merely proving it exists, which is why the extended Euclidean algorithm is an algorithm and not an existence proof.

Result 2: Fermat's little theorem, by a counting argument.

  1. Let $p$ be prime and $p\nmid a$. Consider $a,2a,3a,\dots,(p-1)a$ modulo $p$.
  2. No two are congruent: if $ia\equiv ja$ then $p\mid a(i-j)$, and since $p\nmid a$ and $p$ is prime, $p\mid(i-j)$, impossible for $1\le i,j\le p-1$ distinct.
  3. None is $0$, for the same reason.
  4. So these $p-1$ values are a permutation of $1,2,\dots,p-1$ modulo $p$.
  5. Multiply them all: $a^{p-1}(p-1)! \equiv (p-1)!\pmod p$.
  6. $(p-1)!$ is coprime to $p$, so it may be cancelled, leaving $a^{p-1}\equiv1$.

Step 2 uses Euclid's lemma — if a prime divides a product it divides a factor — and that is exactly where primality enters. For composite $n$ the argument breaks, which is why the converse fails.

Result 3: why Carmichael numbers defeat the Fermat test. Korselt's criterion: $n$ is Carmichael iff $n$ is squarefree and $(p-1)\mid(n-1)$ for every prime $p\mid n$. Check $561=3\cdot11\cdot17$: it is squarefree, $n-1=560$, and $2\mid560$, $10\mid560$, $16\mid560$. So for any $a$ coprime to 561, Fermat's theorem modulo each prime factor gives $a^{560}\equiv1$ modulo 3, 11 and 17, and the Chinese Remainder Theorem assembles those into $a^{560}\equiv1\pmod{561}$. The test is passed for every legitimate base. More bases do not help.

Result 4: why Miller-Rabin is not fooled. For an odd prime $p$, the equation $x^2\equiv1\pmod p$ has only the solutions $x\equiv\pm1$, because $p\mid(x-1)(x+1)$ forces $p$ to divide one factor. So when you square your way up from $a^d$ to $a^{n-1}$, the first time you reach 1 you must have come from $-1$. Miller-Rabin checks exactly that. A composite $n$ has extra square roots of 1 — this is where it differs from a prime — and the test catches a sighting of 1 arriving from something other than $\pm1$.

Result 5: square-and-multiply is correct and fast. Write $k$ in binary. Then $a^k$ is the product of $a^{2^i}$ over the set bits, and the successive squares are computed by repeated squaring. There are $\lfloor\log_2 k\rfloor+1$ squarings and at most that many multiplications, so $O(\log k)$ operations. Reducing modulo $n$ after every step keeps every intermediate below $n^2$, which is what makes 2048-bit RSA feasible at all.

Oracle lab

The oracle computes modular inverses and powers two genuinely independent ways.

For the inverse: the extended Euclidean algorithm produces Bezout coefficients in $O(\log n)$ steps, while a brute-force search tries every residue until one multiplies to 1. One is algebraic, the other exhaustive; they share nothing.

For exponentiation: square-and-multiply against naive repeated multiplication.

For the totient: the product formula over prime factors against a direct count of coprime residues.

Where reality disagrees: the Fermat test is not merely usually fooled by 561 — it is fooled by every single one of the 320 bases coprime to it, which is why no probabilistic argument rescues it. That is a qualitative failure, not a bad error rate, and it is the reason real code uses Miller-Rabin. The hashing half fails just as completely: a power-of-two modulus put all one thousand keys in a single bucket, turning an $O(1)$ lookup into an $O(n)$ scan. Note, though, that the prime modulus is not magic either — it defends against structured keys, and a hash function that is already well mixed (as a cryptographic or a good non-cryptographic hash is) makes the table size much less critical. Java's HashMap uses power-of-two tables precisely because it first applies a bit-spreading step to the hash.

def test_congruence_respects_arithmetic():
    import random
    rng = random.Random(640)
    for _ in range(400):
        n = rng.randint(2, 50)
        a, b = rng.randint(-100, 100), rng.randint(-100, 100)

    assert congruent(25, 2, 12) is False

    assert luhn_check_digit("7992739871") == 3
    for body in ("7992739871", "123456789", "4111111111111"):
        d = luhn_check_digit(body)
        assert luhn_valid(body + str(d)) is True
    # single-digit errors are always caught
    good = "4539148803436467"
    for i in range(len(good)):
        for r in "0123456789":
            if r != good[i]:
                bad = good[:i] + r + good[i + 1:]
                assert luhn_valid(bad) is False, bad
    # Transpositions must be judged starting from a VALID number.
    # Every adjacent swap in this card number is caught...
    for i in range(len(good) - 1):
        if good[i] != good[i + 1]:
            assert luhn_catches_transposition(good, i) is True, i
    # ...but the famous blind spot is not: "0901" is valid and so is "9001".
    assert luhn_valid("0901") is True
    assert luhn_valid("9001") is True
    assert luhn_catches_transposition("0901", 0) is False
    try:
        luhn_catches_transposition("1234", 0)
        assert False, "an invalid starting number must raise ValueError"
    except ValueError:
        pass
    try:
        luhn_valid("12a4")
        assert False, "a non-digit must raise ValueError"
    except ValueError:
        pass

            

            Misconceptions
              

            • "Division works modulo $n$." Only by numbers coprime to $n$. On a 12-hour clock 6 has no inverse at all, and $\frac{1}{6}$ is meaningless there.
              • 

            • "You can always cancel a common factor from a congruence." $2\cdot3\equiv2\cdot9\pmod{12}$ does not give $3\equiv9$. Cancellation needs $\gcd(a,n)=1$.
              • 

            • "If $a^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n$ then $n$ is prime." The converse of Fermat's little theorem is false, and Carmichael numbers fail it for every coprime base. 561 is the smallest.
              • 

            • "Trying more bases fixes the Fermat test." Not for Carmichael numbers, which pass for all of them. The fix is a structurally different test, not more trials.
              • 

            • "Compute $a^k$, then reduce modulo $n$." $2^{1000}$ has 302 digits. Reduce at every step, or the computation is impossible for cryptographic sizes.
              • 

            • "Miller-Rabin might say a prime is composite." It is one-sided: a "composite" verdict is always correct, and only "probably prime" carries any doubt.
              • 

            • "Any table size works if the hash function is decent." With a power-of-two size you keep only the low bits, and structured keys collapse into one bucket — the experiment put all 1000 keys in a single slot. A prime modulus, or a bit-spreading step first, is what saves you.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Trial division is correct and useless at cryptographic scale: a 2048-bit number has about $10^{308}$ candidate divisors below its square root. Miller-Rabin is the practical answer, and its guarantee is probabilistic for random bases — with $k$ rounds the error is at most $4^{-k}$, which for $k=40$ is far below the chance of a hardware fault, but it is not zero. The deterministic small-base sets (such as $2,3,5,7$ below about $3.2\times10^9$) are verified results, not general theorems.
            

            A probable prime is not a proven prime. For contexts where certainty matters there are primality proofs — ECPP, or the polynomial-time AKS algorithm (2002) — which are far slower.
            

            Modular arithmetic says nothing about factoring. RSA's security rests on factoring being hard, and that is a conjecture, not a theorem; Shor's algorithm factors in polynomial time on a quantum computer, which is why post-quantum cryptography exists.
            

            In code, the hazard is overflow: a * b % n can overflow a 64-bit integer when $n$ approaches $2^{32}$, long before you reach cryptographic sizes. Python's unbounded integers hide this; C, Java and Dart do not. And a modular exponentiation whose timing depends on the secret exponent leaks it — constant-time implementations exist precisely because the straightforward square-and-multiply is a side channel.
            

            Finally, Luhn catches all single-digit errors and most adjacent transpositions, but not $09\leftrightarrow90$, as the exercise shows. Verhoeff's scheme, using the dihedral group $D_5$ rather than modular addition, catches all of them — which is why Aadhaar uses it.
            In your real work
            This module is the arithmetic underneath things you use hourly. TLS key exchange is modular exponentiation in a group; RSA is Euler's theorem with $e$ and $d$ inverse modulo $\varphi(n)$; Diffie-Hellman is the difficulty of the discrete logarithm, which M38 returns to. Generating a key pair means generating large probable primes with Miller-Rabin.
            

            Hash tables are the daily application of the prime-modulus result, and the experiment is exactly the failure mode behind a HashMap that mysteriously degrades to linear time on structured keys. Rolling hashes for substring search (Rabin-Karp) are polynomial hashing modulo a prime, and choosing a prime large enough to avoid collisions is this module's arithmetic.
            

            Check digits are everywhere: credit cards (Luhn), ISBNs (mod 11), IMEI numbers, and Aadhaar (Verhoeff). Implementing one correctly, and knowing which errors it does and does not catch, is a small piece of engineering that this module makes routine. The caveat about timing side channels is the kind of thing that distinguishes code that is correct from code that is safe.
            Flash cards
            When does a number have a multiplicative inverse modulo n?
            Exactly when it is coprime to n. The extended Euclidean algorithm constructs the inverse from Bezout's identity.
            Why can you not always cancel a factor from a congruence?
            Because cancellation is multiplication by an inverse, which exists only when the factor is coprime to the modulus.
            State Fermat's little theorem and say where primality is used in its proof.
            For prime p not dividing a, a^(p-1) = 1 mod p. Primality is used to conclude that p dividing a product divides a factor.
            What is a Carmichael number and why does it matter?
            A composite that passes the Fermat test for every coprime base. It shows the Fermat test cannot be repaired by trying more bases. The smallest is 561.
            How does Miller-Rabin improve on the Fermat test?
            It tracks the square roots of 1 encountered while squaring up to a^(n-1). A prime has only +-1 as square roots of 1; a composite may have others.
            Why must you reduce modulo n at every step of exponentiation?
            Because the intermediate powers grow astronomically. Reducing keeps every value below n^2 and makes the computation feasible.
            State the Chinese Remainder Theorem.
            For pairwise coprime moduli, a system of congruences has a unique solution modulo their product.
            What is Euler's totient and what theorem uses it?
            The count of integers up to n coprime to n. Euler's theorem says a^phi(n) = 1 mod n whenever a is coprime to n.
            Why do hash tables often use a prime modulus?
            A power-of-two modulus keeps only the low bits, so keys differing in high bits all collide. A prime mixes every bit into the index.
            Which error does the Luhn check fail to catch, and what fixes it?
            The adjacent transposition 09 to 90. Verhoeff's scheme, based on the dihedral group D5, catches all adjacent transpositions.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why 5 has a partner on a 12-hour clock and 6 does not, you can run the extended Euclidean algorithm by hand and read an inverse off it, you can state Fermat's little theorem and explain exactly why its converse fails, you can describe how Miller-Rabin differs structurally from the Fermat test, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can explain to a colleague why their hash table degraded on structured keys.
