The story

Ravi has buried a tin of marbles in the playground and wants to tell his friend Sita where, over the phone.

"Near the neem tree," he says. Sita asks which side. "Left." Left from where? They go round in circles.

So Ravi invents something. He says: stand at the gate. Walk three steps along the wall. Now turn and walk four steps straight out into the field. Dig.

Two numbers. Three and four. No arguing about "left", no landmarks that move. Every single spot in that playground now has its own pair of numbers, and nobody can confuse two spots.

Then Sita, who is cleverer than Ravi expected, asks a question. "How far is the tin from the gate? I want to know before I start walking."

Ravi has not walked it. But he draws the three steps and the four steps on paper, sees a right angle between them, and says: five. He is right, and he never left his chair.

Here is the surprise: once places have numbers, questions about space — how far, what is in the middle, is this flat piece of land a triangle or just three dots in a line — turn into questions about arithmetic. You can answer them with a pencil instead of your feet. That trade is the whole of coordinate geometry.

Say it simply

The Cartesian plane pins every point to a pair of numbers (x, y): how far across, then how far up. Once you have that, geometry becomes algebra.

A linear equation in two variables, like 2x plus 3y equals 12, is not one answer — it is infinitely many, and if you plot them all you get a straight line. So an equation is a line, and a line is an equation.

Two such equations are two lines, and two straight lines in a plane can do exactly three things: cross at one point, never meet, or lie exactly on top of each other. Those three cases are called consistent with a unique solution, inconsistent, and dependent, and you can tell which you have just by comparing the coefficients, without drawing anything.

The one-line takeaway: solving a pair of linear equations is finding where two lines cross, and the algebra and the picture are two views of one object.

Say it precisely

The Giant City Grid

Imagine the world is a giant piece of graph paper, like a city with perfectly straight streets and avenues. We call this the Cartesian plane. The center of the city is the origin point zero, zero. The streets going left and right make the x-axis. The avenues going up and down make the y-axis.

Because the streets cross, they cut the city into four big neighborhoods called quadrants. - Top-right is where both numbers are happy and positive! - Top-left has a grumpy minus for x but a happy y. - Bottom-left has two grumpies. - Bottom-right has a happy x but a grumpy y.

Finding Distances (The Crow's Flight): If you want to know how far a bird has to fly straight from point P to point Q, you just count how many steps left or right it is, and how many steps up or down it is. Square them, add them together, and find the square root. Just like our friend Pythagoras's triangle rule!

Meeting in the Middle: To find the exact middle between two spots, you just average their numbers! Add their left and right numbers and cut in half. Add their up and down numbers and cut in half.

Shoelace Area: To find the area of a triangle made by three points, you can use a magic trick called the "shoelace formula" that criss-crosses their numbers just like tying your shoes! If the area comes out to exactly zero, it means the three dots aren't a triangle at all — they are perfectly squished onto one single straight line!

Straight Lines and Crossings

A linear equation is a rule for drawing a perfectly straight line across the city. It has a mix of x and y.

If you draw two lines, three things can happen: 1. Intersecting: They cross at a single point (like a giant X). They only have ONE meeting spot! 2. Parallel: They run perfectly side-by-side forever, like train tracks. They NEVER meet! 3. Coincident: One line is perfectly hiding exactly on top of the other line. Every point is a meeting spot!

We can find their meeting spots using fun tricks: - Substitution (The Sneaky Swap): Solve one rule, and sneak it into the other! - Elimination (The Vanishing Trick): Line up both rules and subtract them until one letter completely disappears! - Cross-multiplication (The Magic Formula): A quick magic recipe to find the answer by criss-crossing the numbers!

Sometimes, a rule looks scary because the x and y are trapped under a fraction line. But we can just give them new names to make them flat and happy again!

Why it's true

Why is the distance formula just a hidden triangle?

Imagine you want to go straight from Point P to Point Q, but you are a little person who can only walk safely on the grid lines of the city. First, you walk perfectly straight across horizontally. Then, you turn exactly ninety degrees (a sharp right angle) and walk perfectly straight up. Your path makes the two flat sides of a perfect right-angled triangle! The straight line you actually wanted to fly (the crow's path) is exactly the long slanted side of the triangle. Since we know Pythagoras's rule for right triangles, we just square the horizontal steps, square the vertical steps, add them up, and find the square root to get the perfect flight distance!

The exact middle trick

Imagine a slanted line from point P to Q, and a dot called S right in the exact middle. If you shine a flashlight straight down from above, the shadow of S falls perfectly in the middle of P's shadow and Q's shadow on the ground! Because triangles are proportional, they stretch perfectly like rubber bands. The ratio of the slanted lengths is exactly the same as the ratio of the horizontal flat shadows! So the left and right coordinate of the middle dot is exactly the middle of the two shadows. The exact same magic trick works for the up and down shadows if you shine a light from the side! This shows that finding the middle of a slanty line is just finding the middle of its two straight shadows.

Oracle lab

The oracle computes the area of a triangle two genuinely independent ways.

The first is the shoelace formula, which works straight from the coordinates and never computes a single length. The second goes through Heron's formula: measure the three side lengths with the distance formula, form the semi-perimeter s equals a plus b plus c all divided by two, and take the square root of s times s minus a times s minus b times s minus c.

These are about as independent as two methods can be. One is a signed determinant; the other is a symmetric function of three distances and knows nothing about orientation or about coordinates at all. If the distance formula, the shoelace formula or the arithmetic were wrong, they would disagree. The mutant drops the factor of one half in the shoelace formula — the single most common slip — and the comparison catches it immediately.

Experiment lab
The section formula makes a prediction that can be measured rather than merely rearranged. If S divides PQ internally in the ratio m to n, then the distance from P to S should be exactly the fraction m divided by m plus n of the whole length PQ, and S should lie on the segment.


So we predict PS divided by PQ equals m divided by m plus n, compute S from the formula, then go and measure PS and PQ with the distance formula and compare. Nothing in the measurement reuses the ratio, so this is a genuine check rather than a restatement.

            Misconceptions
              

            • "A linear equation in two variables has one solution." It has infinitely many; they form a line. Only a pair of independent equations pins down a single point.
              • 

            • "No solution and infinitely many solutions are both just 'it did not work'." They are opposite situations. Parallel lines share nothing; coincident lines share everything. Both have zero determinant, which is exactly why the determinant alone cannot tell them apart — you must look at the constant terms.
              • 

            • "In the section formula, m goes with the first point." It goes with the second. The weight nearer a point multiplies the other point's coordinates, which is the opposite of most people's first guess. Check it with the midpoint case, where the symmetry hides the error.
              • 

            • "Collinear points form a very thin triangle." They form no triangle at all; the area is exactly zero. In floating point you must compare against a tolerance, and choosing that tolerance is a real decision, not a formality.
              • 

            • "The shoelace formula gives the area." It gives twice the signed area. Halve it, and take the absolute value, or you get a number that is both too big and sometimes negative.
              • 

            • "Any pair of equations in x and y that looks messy is nonlinear and hopeless." Many become linear after a substitution. Spotting that 1/x can be renamed u is a reusable move, not a trick.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            The coefficient-ratio test in the table assumes you can divide — if a2, b2 or c2 is zero the ratios are undefined and the test has to be rewritten using cross-products, which is why the exercise uses a1 times b2 minus a2 times b1 rather than a1 divided by a2.
            

            The determinant test is exact in integer arithmetic and treacherous in floating point. A system whose determinant is ten to the negative fourteenth power is not meaningfully different from a singular one, yet code that tests det != 0 will cheerfully divide by it and return an answer with no correct digits. This is the problem of conditioning: nearly parallel lines have a crossing point whose position is wildly sensitive to tiny changes in the coefficients. The honest test is against a tolerance scaled to the size of the coefficients, and the proper tools are in M35.
            

            The shoelace formula generalises to any simple polygon, but it fails for self-intersecting ones, where the two lobes contribute opposite signs and partly cancel.
            

            Finally, everything here is two-dimensional and flat. On a sphere "straight line" means great circle, two of them always meet, in two places, and the idea of parallel lines that never meet disappears entirely.
            In your real work
            This is the arithmetic under every touch handler and layout pass. Hit-testing asks whether a tap lies inside a rectangle or a triangle; the signed-area test is how you decide which side of an edge a point falls on, and three such tests decide containment. Gesture velocity is a slope. Interpolating a view between two positions is the section formula with m to n driven by an animation's progress. The conditioning warning above is not academic either: computing an intersection of two nearly parallel lines is a classic source of wild coordinates and off-screen artefacts.
            Flash cards
            What does a single linear equation in two variables represent geometrically?
            A straight line, that is, the infinite set of (x, y) pairs satisfying it.
            What are the three possible outcomes for a pair of linear equations, and what are they called?
            One crossing point (consistent, unique), no crossing (inconsistent, parallel), or the same line twice (dependent, infinitely many solutions).
            What quantity decides whether a 2x2 system has a unique solution, and what is its geometric meaning?
            The determinant a1b2 - a2b1. It is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the coefficient vectors, so a zero determinant means the two directions have collapsed into one.
            Why can the determinant alone not distinguish parallel from coincident lines?
            Because it uses only the coefficients of x and y. Whether the lines coincide depends on the constant terms as well.
            State the section formula and say which point the weight m multiplies.
            The point is ((mx2 + nx1)/(m+n), (my2 + ny1)/(m+n)). The weight m multiplies the coordinates of the second point.
            How do you test three points for collinearity using coordinates?
            Compute the triangle's area with the shoelace formula; the points are collinear exactly when it is zero.
            The distance formula is really which older theorem?
            Pythagoras, applied to the right triangle whose legs are the horizontal and vertical gaps between the points.
            How do you handle a system like 2/x + 3/y = 13?
            Substitute u = 1/x and v = 1/y to get a linear system, solve it, then invert u and v to recover x and y.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why two numbers fix a place and one does not, you can derive the distance and section formulas rather than recall them, you can classify any pair of linear equations as unique, parallel or coincident from the coefficients alone and say why the determinant cannot separate the last two by itself, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can say what goes wrong numerically when two lines are nearly parallel.
