The story

Meera and Kabir are sorting mangoes into two baskets: ripe and not ripe. Their teacher wants to know whether they sort the same way, because then she can send either child to the market alone.

She gives them the same 100 mangoes. Afterwards they compare: they put 90 mangoes in the same basket. "We agree 90 times out of 100!" says Kabir. "We are almost the same!"

But the teacher smiles and asks one more question. "How many mangoes did each of you call ripe?" It turns out that almost every mango that day was unripe, and both children said "not ripe" nearly every time. The teacher explains: "If you had both closed your eyes and just said not ripe to every mango, you would still have agreed about 90 times. Most of your agreement came free. It came from the kind of mangoes we had, not from your eyes."

So the teacher invents a better score. First she works out how often they would agree by luck alone, if each child kept their habits (how often they say ripe) but did not look at the mangoes at all. Then she asks: of the agreement that luck could not give you, how much did you actually get? If they get all of it, the score is 1. If they get only what luck gives, the score is 0. If they agree even less than luck would, the score goes below 0.

That score is called kappa. When Meera and Kabir are graded with kappa, their "90 out of 100" turns out to be close to 0. Luck explains nearly all of it. The children are surprised, and so was a grown-up engineer who once trusted a 90% agreement number.

Say it simply

Two people (or a person and a program) label the same items. Raw agreement is the share of items they label the same. It sounds like the right measure, but it hides a trap: when one label is very common, two labellers agree a lot even if neither of them is paying attention.

Cohen's kappa fixes this. It compares the agreement you saw with the agreement you would expect by chance, given how often each labeller uses each label:

  • kappa = 1 means perfect agreement.
  • kappa = 0 means no better than chance.
  • kappa below 0 means worse than chance: they systematically disagree.

Kappa is not accuracy. It says whether two labellers are interchangeable, not whether either is right. To know if a new labeller (say, an LLM) can replace a trusted human, you compute kappa against the human's labels on items the new labeller was never tuned on.

Say it precisely

Let two raters label the same $n$ items with categories $1, \dots, K$. Let $n_{ij}$ be the number of items rater A put in category $i$ and rater B put in category $j$. Write $p_{ij} = n_{ij}/n$, and the marginals $p_{i\cdot} = \sum_j p_{ij}$ (how often A uses $i$) and $p_{\cdot j} = \sum_i p_{ij}$ (how often B uses $j$).

Observed agreement: $p_o = \sum_i p_{ii}$.

Chance agreement: if A and B labelled independently, keeping their own label frequencies, they would both choose $i$ with probability $p_{i\cdot}\,p_{\cdot i}$, so $p_e = \sum_i p_{i\cdot}\, p_{\cdot i}$.

Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960):

$$\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}$$

It is undefined when $p_e = 1$, which happens only when both raters use one and the same single category for every item.

Weighted kappa for ordered categories: choose disagreement weights $w_{ij}$ with $w_{ii} = 0$, for example linear $w_{ij} = |i-j|/(K-1)$ or quadratic $w_{ij} = (i-j)^2/(K-1)^2$. Then

$$\kappa_w = 1 - \frac{\sum_{ij} w_{ij}\, p_{ij}}{\sum_{ij} w_{ij}\, p_{i\cdot}\, p_{\cdot j}}$$

With $w_{ij} = 1$ for every $i \ne j$ this is exactly Cohen's kappa.

Fleiss' kappa (Fleiss, 1971) for $N$ items each rated by the same number $r \ge 2$ of raters, where $n_{ij}$ now counts raters who put item $i$ in category $j$: per-item agreement $P_i = \frac{\sum_j n_{ij}^2 - r}{r(r-1)}$, mean $\bar P = \frac1N \sum_i P_i$, category shares $p_j = \frac{1}{Nr}\sum_i n_{ij}$, chance $\bar P_e = \sum_j p_j^2$, and $\kappa_F = (\bar P - \bar P_e)/(1 - \bar P_e)$. Fleiss pools all raters into one set of category shares, so with $r = 2$ it equals Scott's pi, not Cohen's kappa.

Krippendorff's alpha generalises the same idea, $\alpha = 1 - D_o/D_e$ (observed over expected disagreement), and also handles missing ratings and any number of raters. We name it here and do not build it.

Why it's true

We want to justify three things: the formula for $p_e$, the shape of kappa, and why kappa moves with prevalence.

Step 1: chance agreement is a product of habits. Imagine rater B's labels were shuffled among the items. Shuffling keeps B's habits, meaning how often B says each label, but destroys any link to the items. Pick one item at random. A says $i$ with probability $p_{i\cdot}$. B's shuffled label is $i$ with probability $p_{\cdot i}$, independently of A. So both say $i$ with probability $p_{i\cdot} p_{\cdot i}$. Summing over $i$ gives $p_e$. You can check this without any algebra: pair every one of A's labels with every one of B's labels ($n^2$ pairs) and count how many pairs match. That count divided by $n^2$ equals $p_e$ exactly. The Oracle below does this.

Step 2: kappa is a rescaling. The largest possible agreement is 1. Chance already gives $p_e$. So the agreement that luck cannot explain has room $1 - p_e$, and the raters achieved $p_o - p_e$ of it. The ratio is kappa. At $p_o = 1$, kappa is 1. At $p_o = p_e$, kappa is 0. At $p_o = 0$, kappa is $-p_e/(1-p_e)$, which is $-1$ when $p_e = 1/2$.

Step 3: prevalence changes kappa even for identical raters. Take two binary raters who each, independently, report the true label with probability $r$ and the wrong label otherwise, on items whose true "yes" rate is $\pi$. Then each says yes with probability $q = \pi r + (1-\pi)(1-r)$. They agree when both are right or both are wrong, so $p_o = r^2 + (1-r)^2$, and that does not depend on $\pi$. But $p_e = q^2 + (1-q)^2$ does. At $\pi = 1/2$, $q = 1/2$, $p_e = 1/2$, and $\kappa = 2p_o - 1 = (2r-1)^2$: for $r = 0.9$, $\kappa = 0.64$. At $\pi = 0.05$ the same raters give $q = 0.14$, $p_e = 0.7592$, $p_o = 0.82$ and $\kappa \approx 0.25$. The raters did not change. The world did. This is the prevalence paradox (Feinstein and Cicchetti, 1990): high raw agreement, low kappa. The companion bias paradox: when two raters use a label at different rates, $p_e$ falls and kappa can rise.

Step 4: why a held-out set. If you try many labelling rules and keep the one with the best kappa on a gold set, the winner's kappa is the maximum of many noisy numbers. The maximum of noisy numbers is biased upward, like picking the student who guessed best on one test. On fresh items the winner regresses toward its true skill. The Experiment measures this optimism directly.

Oracle lab

An oracle is a second, independent way to get the same answer. If two methods that share no code agree on hundreds of random inputs, a bug would have to fool both in the same way.

Here the two methods compute chance agreement $p_e$ differently. Method 1 uses the marginal formula $\sum_i p_{i\cdot} p_{\cdot i}$. Method 2 is brute force: pair every label of A with every label of B and count matches. They must agree to floating-point precision, and therefore the two kappas must agree too. The broken version uses the pooled marginals of Scott's pi, a real and common confusion, and the oracle catches it.

              def _table_from_two_raters(a, b, cats):
    return [[(x == c) + (y == c) for c in cats] for x, y in zip(a, b)]

def _scott_pi(a, b):
    n = len(a)
    po = sum(x == y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / n
    pooled = a + b
    pe = sum((pooled.count(c) / (2 * n)) ** 2 for c in set(pooled))
    return (po - pe) / (1 - pe)

              Misconceptions
                

              • "90% agreement means a good labeller." Only if chance agreement is low. With a rare label, 90% can be pure luck: the mango story, and Exercise 3.
                • 

              • "Kappa is accuracy." Kappa measures whether two raters are interchangeable. Two raters can agree perfectly and both be wrong. To measure correctness you need a trusted reference, and then kappa against that reference is agreement with it.
                • 

              • "A high kappa on the gold set proves the labeller works." Not if you tuned the labeller on that gold set. Selection inflates the score (Experiment 2). Only a held-out set that played no part in building the labeller can validate it.
                • 

              • "Running the same LLM twice and getting kappa 0.9 shows it is valid." That is reliability (it repeats itself), not validity (it matches the truth). A consistent labeller can be consistently wrong.
                • 

              • "Kappa 0.41 is moderate, so it is fine." The Landis and Koch labels (0.41 to 0.60 'moderate') are a convention its authors called arbitrary. Whether a kappa is good enough depends on what you will do with the labels, and on its confidence interval.
                • 

              • "Kappa is a correlation." For two binary raters kappa is close to, but not the same as, the phi correlation. They differ exactly when the raters' marginals differ.
                • 

              Where it breaks
              Kappa is a property of raters on a population, not of raters alone. Move the same raters to a population with a different prevalence and kappa changes (Why it's true, step 3). So never compare kappas across datasets with different base rates as if they were skill scores.
              

              With rare categories the number of informative items is tiny. Sixty items at a 12% rate hold about seven positives, so one flipped label moves kappa a lot. Always report the interval (Exercise 5), and prefer adding positives over adding items.
              

              Kappa is undefined when $p_e = 1$ and unstable as $p_e \to 1$. Alternatives such as Gwet's AC1 were designed to be less sensitive to prevalence, but they answer a slightly different question. Pick the statistic before you see the numbers.
              

              Kappa cannot tell you which rater is wrong, and it ignores why they disagree. Read the disagreements. Half of all labelling bugs are in the guideline, not the labeller.

              Flash cards
              Why can raw agreement be high when neither rater is paying attention?
              When one label is very common, two raters who both use it most of the time agree by chance. Chance agreement is the sum over labels of (A's share) × (B's share).
              Write Cohen's kappa.
              $\kappa = (p_o - p_e)/(1 - p_e)$, with $p_o$ the observed agreement and $p_e = \sum_i p_{i\cdot} p_{\cdot i}$ the chance agreement from the two raters' own marginals.
              What do kappa = 1, 0 and -1 mean?
              1 is perfect agreement, 0 is agreement exactly at chance level, and negative means systematically worse than chance. It reaches -1 only when $p_e = 1/2$ and $p_o = 0$.
              Two raters are each 90% accurate. Why is their kappa 0.64 at a 50% base rate but about 0.25 at 5%?
              Their observed agreement ($r^2 + (1-r)^2 = 0.82$) does not depend on prevalence, but chance agreement does. At 5% prevalence both say "no" so often that chance already explains most of the agreement.
              Why must a labeller be validated on a held-out set?
              If you tuned or selected it on the gold set, its gold score is biased upward (the best of many noisy scores). Only items that played no part in building it give an honest estimate.
              With two raters, what does Fleiss' kappa equal?
              Scott's pi, which uses one pooled set of category shares for both raters, not Cohen's kappa, which uses each rater's own shares.
              How do you bootstrap a kappa correctly?
              Resample items with replacement, keeping each item's pair of labels together. Recompute kappa each time and take the percentile interval. Resampling the raters separately destroys the pairing and drives kappa to about 0.
              What is the difference between reliability and validity for a labeller?
              Reliability means it gives the same answer again (self-agreement). Validity means its answers match the truth. A labeller can be perfectly reliable and consistently wrong.
              Stop when
              You can explain the mango story to a child and say why 90% agreement can be worthless. You can pass Exercise 5 without looking at the hints. And, looking at any agreement number an agent gives you, you ask at once: kappa, on which items, and were they held out?
