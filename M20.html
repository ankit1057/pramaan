The story

Meena's mother runs a sweet shop. This morning she has eighteen kilos of sugar and twelve kilos of ghee, and nothing else will arrive until tomorrow.

She makes two things. A tray of barfi takes three kilos of sugar and one kilo of ghee, and earns sixty rupees. A tray of halwa takes two kilos of sugar and three kilos of ghee, and earns fifty rupees.

Meena says: barfi earns more, so make only barfi. Six trays, that uses all the sugar, three hundred and sixty rupees. Her mother shakes her head — six trays of barfi uses only six kilos of ghee, and six kilos are sitting there doing nothing.

So Meena tries mixing. Four barfi and three halwa: sugar is twelve plus six, eighteen, all gone. Ghee is four plus nine, thirteen — too much. Doesn't work.

She tries again. Four barfi and two halwa. Sugar sixteen, ghee ten. Both left over. Three hundred and forty. Worse.

Her mother draws it on the back of a bill: sugar across, ghee up, and shades the region of plans she can actually afford. It is a lopsided patch with corners.

"Only check the corners," she says.

Meena protests — surely the best plan could be anywhere inside. Her mother tells her to try. Every inside point she tests, she can improve by sliding towards an edge, and every edge point by sliding towards a corner.

Here is the surprise: a problem with infinitely many possible plans has only a handful worth checking. The answer is always standing in a corner.

Say it simply

A linear inequality such as 3x plus 2y is at most 18 divides the plane into two halves. The line itself is the border; one side satisfies the inequality and the other does not.

Several inequalities together carve out a feasible region — all the plans you can actually afford. It is a polygon, possibly unbounded, and it is always convex: the straight line between any two feasible points stays feasible.

A linear program adds a goal: maximise or minimise a linear objective such as 60x+50y over that region.

The whole subject rests on one theorem. Because both the region and the objective are linear, the best value is always attained at a corner of the feasible region. So an infinite search becomes a finite one: list the corners, evaluate each, take the best.

Say it precisely

A linear inequality is like saying "you must be at least ten years old to ride the roller coaster." Solving it gives you a whole range of ages instead of just one answer. The one big trap: multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the rule!

A linear inequality with two things, like sugar and ghee, draws a straight line like a fence across a field. The answer is everything on one side of the fence. A system of these rules means you have to obey all the fences at once. The area trapped inside all the fences is the feasible region. Because the fences are straight, this area is always a perfect shape without weird dents, meaning if you draw a straight path between any two points inside, the path never leaves the yard.

A linear program has two parts: the rules of what you can afford, which draw the fences, and the goal you want to maximize, like making the most money.

The Corner-point theorem is a magic rule: if there is a best possible plan to make the most money, it will always be standing in one of the corners of the yard!

Every linear program comes with a twin brother, called its dual. If the original is about maximizing your profit from your ingredients, the twin is about minimizing the price someone else would have to pay to buy your ingredients off you. The twin tells you the secret true value of your ingredients.

Why it's true

Result 1: The feasible region. Imagine one straight fence. If Alice and Bob are both standing on the correct side of the fence, and you stretch a tightrope between them, the whole tightrope must also be on the correct side. The math says a point on the tightrope is an average of Alice and Bob. Because multiplying by positive averages does not break rules, the tightrope point perfectly obeys the fence rule. Since the yard is just the area trapped by several straight fences, and a tightrope never crosses any single fence, the tightrope stays completely in the yard.

Result 2: The corner-point theorem. Imagine the yard is a polygon. Any spot inside the yard can be thought of as a recipe mixed from the corners. If you are standing in the middle, you are just a weighted average of the corners. Because your money goal is linear, the money you make in the middle is the exact same weighted average of the money you would make at the corners. The obvious truth: a weighted average of scores can never be higher than the single highest score in the mix! If your corners score ten, fifty, and twenty, any average of them is at most fifty. Therefore, no point inside the yard can ever beat the best corner.

Result 3: Weak duality. Let us say a mother has a valid plan to bake sweets, and a buyer has a valid plan to price the ingredients. The buyer sets prices so that buying the raw sugar and ghee for one sweet costs at least as much as the sweet sells for. Therefore, if the buyer buys all the ingredients in the shop, the total cash paid must be at least as much as the mother would make by baking and selling everything. This gives you a magical upper bound. The buyer's offer proves that the mother can never make more profit than that offer, saving you from searching forever!

Oracle lab

The oracle solves the same linear program two genuinely independent ways.

The first is corner enumeration: take every pair of constraint boundaries, solve the resulting two-by-two system for their intersection, discard any intersection that violates some constraint, and evaluate the objective at the survivors. It is pure linear algebra and uses the theorem.

The second is a dense grid search over the bounding box: test many points, keep the feasible ones, and report the best objective value found. It knows nothing about corners, convexity or theorems — it just looks everywhere.

If the corner-point theorem were false, the grid would find something better than the best corner. The mutant below forgets to check that a candidate intersection is actually feasible, so it happily reports the objective at a point outside the region — the single most common bug when implementing this.

Experiment lab
Two predictions.


First, the corner-point theorem as a measurement. Generate random feasible points by the thousand, evaluate the objective at each, and compare the best of them with the best corner. The theory predicts the corner wins every time, and that random sampling never quite reaches it. If some random interior point beat the best corner, the theorem would be dead.


Second, weak duality. We predict that for any dual-feasible y — not just the optimal one — the dual objective b^Ty is an upper bound on the primal optimum, and that at the optimum the two values are exactly equal. We generate random dual-feasible points and check the bound never breaks.

Where reality disagrees: random search gets close and never arrives. With two hundred thousand samples it lands within a rupee or so of the optimum but cannot reach it, because a corner is a single point of zero area and a continuous sample hits it with probability zero. That is the practical argument for the theorem: the exact answer is unreachable by search and trivial by enumeration. Note also what the duality half gives you that the primal alone does not — a certificate. Any dual-feasible pair proves an upper bound without solving anything, so you can verify an optimum far more cheaply than you can find it.

            Misconceptions
              

            • "Multiplying an inequality by a number works like an equation." Only for positive numbers. A negative multiplier reverses the direction, and forgetting that is the single most common error in the whole topic.
              • 

            • "The optimum could be anywhere in the feasible region." For a linear objective it is always attainable at a corner, because every interior point is a weighted average of corners and an average never beats the best ingredient. For a non-linear objective this fails completely.
              • 

            • "An unbounded feasible region means an unbounded problem." Not at all. Minimising x plus y over x and y are positive has an infinite region and a perfectly good optimum at the origin. Unboundedness is a property of region and objective together.
              • 

            • "Every intersection of two constraint lines is a corner." Only the feasible ones. Two boundaries may cross far outside the region, and including such points is the standard bug — it was the demo's mutant.
              • 

            • "The greedy choice is optimal: make whichever product earns most." Meena's first instinct, and wrong. The constraints interact, and the optimum usually mixes.
              • 

            • "Duality is abstract theory with no use." The dual variables are prices. They tell you exactly what another kilo of a scarce resource is worth, and any dual-feasible point certifies an upper bound without solving anything.
              • 

            • "Linear programming solves optimisation." It solves linear optimisation over continuous variables. Require the answers to be whole numbers — three and a half trays is not a plan — and you have integer programming, which is NP-hard.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            The corner-point theorem needs linearity on both sides. A curved objective can have its optimum strictly inside the region, and a non-convex feasible set can have local optima that are not global — which is exactly why M36's non-convex landscapes are hard and linear programming is not.
            

            Enumerating corners is fine for two variables and hopeless in general: m constraints in n variables give up to combinations of m things taken n at a time candidate vertices, which explodes. That is the whole reason simplex exists, and even simplex has contrived inputs (the Klee-Minty cubes) on which it visits exponentially many vertices.
            

            Integrality is the real-world wall. The LP relaxation gives a bound, but rounding the fractional answer can be infeasible or far from optimal, and the honest tools are branch-and-bound or cutting planes.
            

            Numerically, degenerate vertices — where more than two constraints meet at one point — make the geometry ambiguous and can cause simplex to cycle without Bland's rule or a similar anti-cycling guard. And nearly parallel constraints make a vertex ill-conditioned, exactly as in M12: a tiny change in a coefficient moves the corner a long way.
            In your real work
            This is resource allocation with a proof attached. Scheduling jobs against machine capacity, assigning requests to servers under a latency budget, choosing a cache mix under a memory ceiling, and planning a build pipeline against CPU limits are all linear programs, and the shadow prices answer the question management actually asks — "what would another machine buy us?" Weak duality is the practically useful half: it gives a cheap certificate that your solution cannot be improved by more than so much, which is often worth more than the exact optimum. And the convexity result is the foundation M36 builds on: convex problems are the ones where a local optimum is automatically global, and knowing whether your problem is convex is the first question to ask before reaching for any optimiser.
            Flash cards
            What is the one rule that makes solving an inequality different from solving an equation?
            Multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality.
            State the corner-point theorem and the reason it is true.
            If a linear program has an optimum, some corner attains it. Every feasible point is a weighted average of corners, and a linear objective at an average is the average of the objectives, which cannot exceed the best corner.
            Why is the feasible region always convex?
            It is an intersection of half-planes, each convex, and intersections of convex sets are convex.
            Name the three possible outcomes of a linear program.
            Optimal, infeasible (no feasible point), or unbounded (the objective improves forever).
            Does an unbounded feasible region imply an unbounded optimum?
            No. Minimising x+y over the non-negative quadrant has an infinite region and an optimum at the origin.
            What does the simplex method do, and why does it not get stuck?
            It walks from corner to adjacent corner, improving each step. Convexity plus a linear objective means any local optimum is global.
            State weak duality and say what it is good for.
            Any dual-feasible point gives an upper bound on the primal maximum. It certifies optimality without solving the problem.
            What do the dual variables mean in practice?
            They are shadow prices: the improvement in the optimum per extra unit of each resource.
            Why is the greedy "make whatever earns most" plan usually wrong?
            Because the constraints interact. Using all of one resource on the most profitable product typically leaves another resource idle.
            What happens to linear programming when the variables must be whole numbers?
            It becomes integer programming, which is NP-hard. Rounding the LP answer may be infeasible or far from optimal.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why only the corners need checking, you can derive the corner-point theorem from convexity and the averaging argument, you can set up a linear program from a word problem and classify it as optimal, infeasible or unbounded, you can state what the dual variables mean in rupees, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can say why this all stops working the moment the variables must be integers.
