The story

Tara is standing on a hillside in thick fog. She cannot see the top. She wants to go up.

She can only do one thing: take a small step in some direction and feel whether the ground rose or fell.

She steps north: slightly up. East: steeply up. South: down. West: steeply down.

She is about to walk east, since that was the best of the four, when her grandfather stops her. "You tried four directions. There are infinitely many. Try north-east."

She does, and it is steeper than east.

He asks her how many directions she plans to test. She realises she could test forever and never be sure.

Then he tells her the thing that makes this a subject. "You do not need to test them all. Measure the slope going east, and the slope going north. Just those two. Every other direction's slope is fixed by those two numbers, and the steepest direction is the one that points the way those two numbers say."

Tara does not believe him and spends an hour checking. He is right every time.

Here is the surprise: a function of two variables has infinitely many directional slopes, and all of them are determined by two. Those two numbers, bundled together, are the gradient — and the fact that it points uphill most steeply is why every machine-learning model in the world is trained by following it.

Say it simply

A function of several variables, $f(x,y,\dots)$, has a slope in every direction. Partial derivatives measure the slopes along the axes: differentiate with respect to one variable, holding the others fixed.

The gradient $\nabla f$ collects the partials into a vector. Two facts make it central: the slope in any direction $u$ is $\nabla f\cdot u$, and the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, with magnitude equal to that steepest slope. Tara's grandfather's claim, exactly.

The Hessian is the matrix of second partials, and its eigenvalues classify a critical point: all positive is a minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle — a thing with no one-dimensional analogue, and the reason high-dimensional optimisation is hard.

The chain rule in several variables is the multiplication of Jacobian matrices, and that is precisely backpropagation.

Say it precisely

Partials, gradient, directional derivative

For $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, the partial derivative is

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(\dots,x_i+h,\dots)-f(\dots,x_i,\dots)}{h}$$

The gradient is the vector $\nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right)$.

For a unit vector $u$, the directional derivative is

$$D_uf = \nabla f\cdot u = \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta$$

which is largest when $\theta=0$ — that is, along $\nabla f$ itself — with value $\|\nabla f\|$. The gradient is also perpendicular to the level set through the point, because moving along a level curve does not change $f$, so $D_uf=0$ there.

A caution: having all partial derivatives does not imply differentiability. $f(x,y)=\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}$ (with $f(0,0)=0$) has both partials at the origin and is not even continuous there. True differentiability requires a good linear approximation in every direction at once.

Jacobian and the chain rule

For $F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$, the Jacobian is the $m\times n$ matrix $J_{ij}=\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j}$. It is the best linear approximation: $F(x+h)\approx F(x)+Jh$.

Multivariate chain rule. For $F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ and $G:\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}^p$,

$$J_{G\circ F}(x) = J_G(F(x))\;J_F(x)$$

Composition of maps becomes multiplication of Jacobians. For a scalar output composed through many layers this is a chain of matrix products, and multiplying it right-to-left (from the output backwards) is reverse-mode automatic differentiation — backpropagation. Doing it left-to-right is forward mode. For one output and many inputs, reverse mode costs about the same as one forward evaluation; forward mode costs one pass per input, which for a million parameters is the difference between possible and impossible.

Hessian and Taylor

The Hessian is $H_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}$. By Clairaut's theorem it is symmetric wherever the second partials are continuous, so M31's spectral theorem applies.

Taylor to second order:

$$f(x+h) \approx f(x)+\nabla f(x)^Th+\tfrac12 h^THh$$

At a critical point ($\nabla f=0$) the Hessian decides the type:

Hessian eigenvalues Point
all positive (positive definite) local minimum
all negative (negative definite) local maximum
mixed signs (indefinite) saddle
some zero inconclusive

A saddle goes up in one direction and down in another. In $n$ dimensions a random critical point is overwhelmingly likely to be a saddle rather than a minimum, which is the modern understanding of why training deep networks is hard — not local minima, but saddles and plateaus.

Lagrange multipliers

To optimise $f$ subject to $g=c$, the gradients must be parallel at the optimum:

$$\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g, \qquad g=c$$

Intuition: if $\nabla f$ had any component along the constraint surface, you could slide that way and improve. So at the optimum $\nabla f$ is entirely perpendicular to the surface — and $\nabla g$ is too.

Multiple integrals and change of variables

A double integral $\iint_R f\,dA$ sums $f$ over a region; by Fubini's theorem (for a continuous $f$ on a rectangle) it equals either iterated integral, in either order.

Change of variables. If $x=T(u)$ is a smooth invertible map,

$$\iint_{R} f(x)\,dx = \iint_{S} f(T(u))\,\left|\det J_T(u)\right|\,du$$

The Jacobian determinant is the local area (or volume) scale factor — precisely M26's result, now applied pointwise. For polar coordinates $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, $\det J = r$, giving the familiar $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$.

The Gaussian integral

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt{\pi}$$

There is no elementary antiderivative, so the one-dimensional approach fails. The trick is to go up a dimension: square it, turn the product of two integrals into a double integral, and switch to polar coordinates, where it becomes elementary. This is the standard example of a problem that is easier in higher dimensions.

A pinch of history

Partial derivatives appear in the eighteenth century with Euler, d'Alembert and Clairaut, who proved the symmetry of mixed partials in 1740. Lagrange introduced multipliers in the Mécanique analytique (1788). Hamilton's $\nabla$ symbol (1837) was named "nabla" after a Hebrew harp of similar shape, by Peter Guthrie Tait.

The Gaussian integral's polar trick is due to Laplace (1778), before Gauss. Otto Hesse's matrix dates from 1857, and Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi's determinant work from the 1830s and 1840s.

Reverse-mode automatic differentiation was described by Seppo Linnainmaa in his 1970 master's thesis, applied to neural networks by Paul Werbos in 1974, and popularised by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in 1986. The mathematics is nothing more than the chain rule above, organised to multiply the Jacobians in the efficient order.

Why it's true

Result 1: the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent.

  1. For a unit vector $u$, the directional derivative is $D_uf = \nabla f\cdot u$.
  2. By the dot-product formula of M21, $\nabla f\cdot u = \|\nabla f\|\|u\|\cos\theta = \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta$, since $u$ is a unit vector.
  3. $\cos\theta$ is largest, namely 1, when $\theta=0$ — that is, when $u$ points along $\nabla f$.
  4. So the maximum directional derivative is $\|\nabla f\|$, attained in the gradient's own direction; the minimum is $-\|\nabla f\|$, opposite to it.
  5. When $u$ is perpendicular to $\nabla f$, $\cos\theta=0$ and the slope vanishes — those are the directions along the level set.

The non-obvious step is 1, the claim that every directional slope is a dot product with one fixed vector. That is what differentiability buys, and it is exactly the fact Tara did not believe: infinitely many slopes, two numbers.

Result 2: the chain rule as Jacobian multiplication. Both $F$ and $G$ are locally linear: $F(x+h)\approx F(x)+J_Fh$ and $G(y+k)\approx G(y)+J_Gk$. Composing, with $k=J_Fh$,

$$G(F(x+h)) \approx G(F(x)) + J_G\,J_F\,h$$

So the best linear approximation of the composition is the product of the approximations. Composition of functions is multiplication of matrices — which is M26's Result 1 arriving from the other direction.

Result 3: why reverse mode is cheap. Consider $f = g_L\circ\dots\circ g_1$ with a scalar output. The gradient is

$$\nabla f^T = J_{g_L}J_{g_{L-1}}\cdots J_{g_1}$$

Matrix multiplication is associative, so you may bracket it as you like — but the cost depends on the bracketing. Left-to-right starts with a $1\times m$ row vector times an $m\times m$ matrix, which is $O(m^2)$ per step and keeps a vector throughout. Right-to-left starts with $m\times m$ times $m\times n$, keeping full matrices and costing $O(m^3)$-ish per step. For one output and many inputs, going backwards from the output keeps everything a vector. That single associativity choice is the whole efficiency of backpropagation.

Result 4: the second-derivative test. Near a critical point, Taylor gives $f(x+h)-f(x)\approx\frac12h^THh$. Since $H$ is symmetric, M31's spectral theorem writes $H=Q\Lambda Q^T$, and setting $z=Q^Th$ turns the quadratic form into $\frac12\sum\lambda_iz_i^2$. If every $\lambda_i>0$ this is positive for all $h\neq0$, so $f$ increases in every direction: a minimum. If the signs are mixed, $f$ increases along some eigenvectors and decreases along others: a saddle. The eigenvectors are the axes along which the behaviour is pure.

Result 5: the Gaussian integral. Let $I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx$. Then

$$I^2 = \left(\int e^{-x^2}dx\right)\left(\int e^{-y^2}dy\right) = \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA$$

Switch to polar coordinates, where $x^2+y^2=r^2$ and $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$:

$$I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\infty}e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\left[-\tfrac12e^{-r^2}\right]_0^{\infty} = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac12 = \pi$$

so $I=\sqrt{\pi}$. The decisive detail is the factor $r$ from the Jacobian: without it the inner integral would still have no elementary antiderivative, and with it the substitution $u=r^2$ finishes immediately.

Oracle lab

The oracle computes derivatives two genuinely independent ways.

The symbolic route applies the rules — partials, the gradient formula, the chain rule as a Jacobian product — and evaluates the resulting expressions.

The numerical route never uses a rule: it evaluates the function at nearby points and forms central differences.

For the chain rule specifically, this is the standard gradient check that every deep-learning practitioner runs when hand-writing a backward pass, and the demo performs it on a small two-layer network. Experiment lab
Three predictions.


First, saddles dominate. Theory says a critical point of a random function in $n$ dimensions is a minimum only if all $n$ Hessian eigenvalues happen to be positive. If signs were independent and equally likely, that is one chance in $2^n$. We generate random symmetric Hessians and count how the classification shifts as $n$ grows.


Second, reverse mode versus forward mode. For a scalar output and $n$ inputs, forward mode needs $n$ passes and reverse mode one. We count function evaluations.


Third, the Gaussian integral. $\int e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt{\pi}$, obtained by going up a dimension. We check it numerically and verify the polar-coordinate route.

Where reality disagrees: the saddle table is the striking one. In one dimension about half of random critical points are minima; by eight dimensions it is under two per cent, and essentially all of them are saddles. That is the empirical content of the modern view that training large models is not about escaping local minima — there are hardly any — but about getting past saddles and the flat regions around them. Note that the experiment assumes a random Hessian with independent entries, which real loss surfaces are not; the qualitative conclusion survives, but the exact $\frac{1}{2^n}$ figure does not, and the measured fractions fall faster than that because the eigenvalues of a random symmetric matrix are not independent.

        assert cos(t) + sin(t) <= value + 1e-12
    try:
        solve_lagrange_circle(0.0, 0.0)
        assert False, "a zero objective must raise ValueError"
    except ValueError:
        pass


def test_double_integrals():
    # integral of x*y over [0,1]x[0,2] is (1/2)(2) = 1
    assert abs(double_integral(lambda x, y: x * y, 0, 1, 0, 2) - 1.0) < 1e-6
    # a constant integrates to the area
    assert abs(double_integral(lambda x, y: 3.0, 0, 2, 0, 5) - 30.0) < 1e-6
    # Fubini: the order does not matter
    f = lambda x, y: x ** 2 + y
    a = double_integral(f, 0, 1, 0, 2)
    b = double_integral(lambda y, x: f(x, y), 0, 2, 0, 1)
    assert abs(a - b) < 1e-6
    # the area of a disc of radius 2, via polar coordinates
    assert abs(polar_integral(lambda r, th: 1.0, 2.0) - 4 * 3.141592653589793) < 1e-4


def test_the_gaussian_integral():
    from math import sqrt, pi
    direct = gaussian_1d(8.0)
    polar = gaussian_via_polar(12.0)
    assert abs(direct - sqrt(pi)) < 1e-8
    assert abs(polar - sqrt(pi)) < 1e-8
    assert abs(direct - polar) < 1e-7

    disc = polar_integral(lambda r, th: 2.718281828459045 ** (-r * r), 12.0)

    assert abs(sqrt(disc) - direct) < 1e-5
    try:

            Misconceptions
              

            • "Having all partial derivatives means the function is differentiable." It does not. $\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}$ has both partials at the origin and is not even continuous there. Differentiability needs a good linear approximation in every direction at once.
              • 

            • "The gradient points towards the minimum." It points in the direction of steepest ascent, locally. Gradient descent moves opposite to it, and even then only locally — the gradient knows nothing about where the minimum is.
              • 

            • "A critical point is a minimum or a maximum." In two or more dimensions it is usually a saddle, and the experiment measured how quickly minima become rare as the dimension grows.
              • 

            • "A positive trace of the Hessian means a minimum." You need every eigenvalue positive. A saddle with eigenvalues $+5$ and $-1$ has a positive trace, and the exercise's mutant exploits exactly that.
              • 

            • "Backpropagation is a special algorithm for neural networks." It is the chain rule, with the Jacobian product bracketed right-to-left. The only cleverness is the order of multiplication.
              • 

            • "Just use numerical gradients, they are simpler." A central-difference gradient costs $2n$ function evaluations. For a million parameters that is two million forward passes per step, against one for reverse mode.
              • 

            • "Change of variables just substitutes." You must also multiply by the absolute Jacobian determinant. Forgetting the $r$ in polar coordinates changes the answer, as the mutant shows.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Clairaut's symmetry of mixed partials requires the second partials to be continuous; there are standard counterexamples where $f_{xy}\neq f_{yx}$ at a point. The Hessian is symmetric in practice, but the theorem has hypotheses.
            

            The second-derivative test is silent when any eigenvalue is zero — the monkey saddle $x^3-3xy^2$ has a completely flat Hessian at the origin and is neither a maximum, a minimum, nor an ordinary saddle. Degenerate critical points need higher-order information.
            

            Lagrange multipliers need a regularity condition: $\nabla g\neq0$ at the optimum. Where the constraint surface has a cusp or a self-intersection, the method silently misses solutions. It also finds all stationary points of the Lagrangian, maxima, minima and saddles alike, so you must classify them afterwards.
            

            Numerically, finite-difference Hessians are far worse than gradients: the second difference divides by $h^2$, so rounding error is amplified by $\frac{1}{h^2}$ and the usable range of $h$ is narrow. That is the step-size trade-off of M22, squared, and it is why second-order optimisers avoid forming an explicit Hessian.
            

            Fubini's theorem needs absolute integrability. For a function that is not absolutely integrable the two iterated integrals can genuinely differ, which is the multivariable cousin of M33's conditionally convergent series.
            In your real work
            This module is the mathematics of training. A loss function of a million parameters is a function of a million variables; its gradient is what every optimiser consumes; and backpropagation is the chain rule organised efficiently. The gradient check in the demo is the standard practice when hand-writing a backward pass — and the mutant, a missing activation derivative, is the bug it catches.
            

            The saddle result changed how the field thinks. For years the folk explanation of hard optimisation was "local minima"; the dimensional argument says minima are vanishingly rare and saddles and plateaus are the real obstacle, which is why momentum and adaptive methods (M36) help so much.
            

            Outside machine learning: the Jacobian determinant is the area scale factor behind texture mapping and coordinate changes in graphics; Lagrange multipliers are the shadow prices of M20 in a non-linear setting; and the Hessian is the curvature that Newton's method uses and that tells you how well conditioned a local optimisation problem is.
            Flash cards
            Why do two numbers determine infinitely many directional slopes?
            Because the directional derivative is the dot product of the gradient with the unit direction, so one vector fixes them all.
            In which direction does the gradient point, and what is its magnitude?
            Steepest ascent, with magnitude equal to that steepest slope. Perpendicular to it, the slope is zero.
            Does having all partial derivatives imply differentiability?
            No. A function can have both partials at a point and not even be continuous there.
            What is the multivariate chain rule in one line?
            The Jacobian of a composition is the product of the Jacobians, evaluated at the right points.
            Why is reverse-mode differentiation cheap for a scalar loss?
            Because bracketing the Jacobian product from the output backwards keeps every intermediate a vector, so one pass gives all the partial derivatives.
            How does the Hessian classify a critical point?
            All eigenvalues positive is a minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle, any zero inconclusive.
            Why are saddles the dominant critical point in high dimensions?
            A minimum needs every one of n eigenvalues to be positive, which becomes exponentially unlikely as n grows.
            State the Lagrange condition and its intuition.
            grad f = lambda grad g at the optimum. If the objective's gradient had any component along the constraint surface you could slide that way and improve.
            What must you multiply by when changing variables in a multiple integral?
            The absolute value of the Jacobian determinant, the local volume scale factor. For polar coordinates it is r.
            How is the Gaussian integral evaluated, and why?
            Square it to get a double integral, switch to polar coordinates, and the Jacobian factor r makes the integrand elementary. The one-dimensional problem has no elementary antiderivative.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why two measured slopes fix every direction, you can prove the gradient is the steepest direction from the dot-product formula, you can write the chain rule as a Jacobian product and explain why reverse mode is cheap, you can classify a critical point from the Hessian's eigenvalues and say why saddles dominate in high dimensions, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you would run a gradient check before trusting a hand-written backward pass.
