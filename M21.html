The story

Arun's father is a torch-seller, and the shop has a bare wall at the back.

Arun points the torch straight at the wall. A perfect circle of light.

He tilts it a little. The circle stretches into an oval. He tilts more; the oval gets longer and more lopsided. He keeps going, and at one particular angle something odd happens — the shape stops closing. One end runs off the wall and never comes back. A moment later, tilting further, the light splits and there are two separate curves.

Arun has not changed the torch. He has not changed the wall. The beam is the same cone of light it always was. He only changed the angle of the slice.

His father tells him the names: circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola. Arun is annoyed that four shapes he learned separately in four different lessons turned out to be one thing seen from four angles.

Then his father asks the better question. "How would you tell me, on the phone, exactly where on the wall the edge of the light is?"

Here is the surprise: to say where, you need numbers and a direction — a thing with both size and heading. That is a vector. And once you have vectors, the four curves, the lines, the planes, and the whole of three-dimensional space all become arithmetic you can do with your eyes shut.

Say it simply

This module is the geometry of straight things and smooth curves, done with coordinates.

Lines come in several disguises — slope-intercept, point-slope, two-point, general — and they are all the same object. The useful questions are: what angle do two lines make, are they parallel or perpendicular, and how far is a point from a line?

Conics are the four curves you get by slicing a cone: circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola. Each has a focus, and each has a defining distance property.

Vectors are arrows: size and direction. Two products matter. The dot product measures how much two vectors agree, and gives you angles and projections. The cross product gives a vector perpendicular to both, and its length is an area.

Three dimensions then costs almost nothing extra. A line needs a point and a direction; a plane needs a point and a normal; and every distance and angle question reduces to a dot or cross product.

Say it precisely

A line on a graph is just a straight road. You can describe it in three ways. You can use the point-slope way if you know one spot on the road and exactly how steep it is. You can use the slope-intercept way if you know the steepness and exactly where the road hits the wall on the side. Or you can use the general form.

If you have two lines, you can find the angle between them using their steepness. If the lines run side-by-side like train tracks, their steepness is perfectly equal. If they crash exactly at a right angle, they are perpendicular.

Imagine a dog tied to a post while keeping an eye on a straight fence. The dog's path depends on a strict rule about its distances to the post and the fence. That rule is the eccentricity. If it is zero, the dog runs in a perfect circle around the post. If it is a small fraction, the dog runs in an egg-shape called an ellipse. If it is one, the path opens up completely like a cup called a parabola. If it is greater than one, the dog runs away in a wide sweeping curve called a hyperbola.

A vector is an arrow. It has a length and points somewhere. The dot product tells you how much two arrows agree with each other. If two arrows point exactly perpendicular to each other, they do not agree at all, and the dot product is exactly zero. The cross product makes a brand new arrow that points straight up, perfectly perpendicular to both original arrows, like a flagpole sticking out of the ground. The length of this new arrow tells you the exact area of the shape formed by the first two arrows!

Why it's true

Result 1: The distance from a point to a line. A line has a secret: its numbers actually make an arrow that points perfectly straight away from the line, which we call the normal vector. Pick any random point on the line. Draw an arrow from that point to your target point. This arrow is slanted. To find the true shortest distance, you just measure how much of this slanted arrow goes in the exact same direction as the normal vector. The dot product perfectly measures this shadow, giving the exact distance.

Result 2: The dot product. If you draw two arrows and then connect their tips, you make a triangle. The length of that connecting line is just the Cosine Rule from geometry. If you do the algebra with the vector coordinates, the giant messy part of the Cosine Rule perfectly collapses into a simple sum of multiplications. The dot product is literally just the Cosine Rule wearing a clever disguise, which is why it knows the angle!

Result 3: The cross product. The area of a shape made by two arrows is its base times its height. The base is the length of the first arrow. The height depends on the second arrow and the angle between them. If you square the big ugly cross product formula, all the letters reshuffle perfectly to match the squared area. The new arrow captures the area completely.

Result 4: The shortest distance between skew lines. Skew lines are like two airplanes flying at different heights in different directions: they never crash and are not parallel. To find the shortest distance between them, you need a measuring stick that is perfectly straight up-and-down between both paths. The cross product of their two directions gives you exactly this perfect measuring stick. Take any point on the first path and any point on the second path. Draw an arrow between them. Just like before, find the shadow of this slanted arrow onto your perfect measuring stick. That is your shortest distance.

Oracle lab

The oracle computes geometric quantities two genuinely independent ways.

The algebraic route uses formulas: the point-to-line distance formula, the dot-product definition of angle, the component formula for the cross product, the skew-line formula. The search route finds the same quantities by brute force: it walks finely along a line and reports the smallest distance it ever sees, and it builds the cross product by solving the perpendicularity conditions rather than quoting the determinant.

A minimisation by search knows nothing about normals or projections. If a formula had a wrong sign or a missing square root, the two would diverge. The mutant below drops the square root in the denominator of the point-to-line distance — the result still looks dimensionally plausible and is simply wrong.

Experiment lab
Theory makes a striking claim: the four conic sections are one family, distinguished only by eccentricity, and each obeys a distance property that holds at every point of the curve. We measure that directly.


For the ellipse we predict that the sum of the distances to the two foci is exactly 2a, the same number everywhere on the curve. For the hyperbola we predict the difference is 2a. For the parabola we predict the distance to the focus equals the distance to the directrix. And for all of them we predict the focus-directrix ratio is exactly the eccentricity.


Then we check Arun's claim literally: we slice an actual cone at varying angles and classify what comes out.

Where reality disagrees: the classification by discriminant is exact, but notice the boundary. At slope exactly 1 the plane is parallel to the cone's own slant, and the section is a parabola — a single, infinitely thin case between the closed ellipses and the open hyperbolas. In floating point you can never land on it: a slope of 1 \pm 10^{-16} gives an extremely long thin ellipse or a very flat hyperbola, and no finite computation can distinguish those from a parabola. The parabola is real and is measure-zero, which is exactly why Arun could not find the moment the light "stopped closing" by tilting slowly. Degenerate cases — the plane through the apex, giving a point, a line or a pair of lines — behave the same way.

            Misconceptions
              

            • "A vertical line has infinite slope." It has no slope; the formula divides by zero. Code that returns infinity will usually work by accident and then fail on a comparison. Represent it explicitly, as None.
              • 

            • "The dot product is a vector and the cross product is a number." The other way round. The dot product returns a scalar (hence "scalar product") and the cross product returns a vector perpendicular to both.
              • 

            • "The cross product works in two dimensions." It is special to three (and, in a different form, seven). In 2-D the analogous quantity a_1b_2-a_2b_1 is a scalar — the signed area of M12's shoelace formula.
              • 

            • "\vec a\times\vec b = \vec b\times\vec a." It is anticommutative: swapping gives the negative. The direction flips, which is why the right-hand rule matters.
              • 

            • "The ellipse and hyperbola formulas are nearly the same, so the sign hardly matters." c=\sqrt{a^2-b^2} puts the foci inside; c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} puts them outside. Confusing them is the standard exam disaster.
              • 

            • "Two lines in space either meet or are parallel." That is only true in a plane. In space they can be skew: not parallel and still never meeting. Most pairs of random lines in space are skew.
              • 

            • "\vec a\cdot\vec b = 0 means one of them is zero." It means they are perpendicular — or one is zero. Perpendicularity is the useful case.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Slope-based formulas break at vertical lines, and the angle formula \tan\theta = \left|\frac{m_1-m_2}{1+m_1m_2}\right| divides by zero exactly when the lines are perpendicular — the case you most want to detect. Vector formulations avoid both problems, which is why professional geometry code uses normals and directions rather than slopes.
            

            The conic classification by discriminant is exact in symbols and fragile in floating point, as the experiment showed: the parabola is a measure-zero boundary you cannot land on. Real code should not branch on exact equality here.
            

            Recovering an angle from acos of a dot product is ill-conditioned near 0 and \pi — the same problem as M15 — so for nearly parallel vectors atan2(|a×b|, a·b) is far better behaved.
            

            The skew-distance formula divides by |\vec d_1\times\vec d_2|, which approaches zero as the lines become parallel. Nearly parallel lines therefore give a numerically unstable answer, and the parallel case must be handled separately rather than reached by limit — which is why the boss exercise branches on it explicitly.
            

            Finally, all of this assumes flat Euclidean space. On a sphere "straight line" means great circle, every pair of them meets, and there are no parallels at all; in the curved spacetime of general relativity, conic-section orbits are only an approximation, and the leftover is the precession of Mercury's perihelion.
            In your real work
            Hit-testing, layout and graphics run on this directly. Deciding which side of an edge a touch falls on is a sign test on a cross product; the point-to-line distance decides whether a tap is close enough to a thin control; the dot product is how you compute whether a scroll gesture is more horizontal than vertical. A plane's normal is what lighting calculations need, and backface culling is a single dot-product sign.
            

            Ray tracing is this module almost verbatim: a ray is \vec r = \vec a+t\vec d, intersecting it with a plane is one dot product, and with a sphere is the quadratic of M13 — including its conditioning problem when the ray grazes. Camera projection, collision detection and the orientation maths behind a gyroscope all live here, and the conic family describes every ballistic arc and orbital path you will ever simulate.
            Flash cards
            What does the dot product measure, and what does the cross product measure?
            The dot product is a scalar measuring agreement, giving angles and projections. The cross product is a vector perpendicular to both, whose length is the area of the parallelogram they span.
            How do you find the distance from a point to a line or plane?
            Project the vector from any point on the line or plane onto the unit normal. In coordinates this is |A x0 + B y0 + C| over the length of the normal.
            What single number distinguishes the four conic sections?
            The eccentricity: 0 is a circle, below 1 an ellipse, exactly 1 a parabola, above 1 a hyperbola.
            For an ellipse, what is constant at every point of the curve?
            The sum of the distances to the two foci, equal to 2a. For a hyperbola it is the difference instead.
            Where are the foci of an ellipse and of a hyperbola?
            At plus and minus c on the major axis, with c = sqrt(a^2 - b^2) for the ellipse and sqrt(a^2 + b^2) for the hyperbola.
            What does a zero scalar triple product tell you?
            The three vectors are coplanar, so the parallelepiped they span has zero volume.
            What are skew lines and how do you detect them?
            Lines in space that are neither parallel nor intersecting. Their directions have a non-zero cross product and the joining vector has a non-zero component along it.
            Why is the cross product anticommutative?
            Because swapping the two vectors reverses the orientation of the parallelogram, so the perpendicular direction flips.
            Why do professional geometry routines avoid slopes?
            Slopes are undefined for vertical lines and the angle formula divides by zero exactly when lines are perpendicular. Direction and normal vectors have no such special cases.
            State Lagrange's identity and what it says.
            |a x b|^2 + (a . b)^2 = |a|^2 |b|^2. The two products split the information in a pair of vectors between them.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why one torch makes four different shapes, you can derive the point-to-line distance by projecting onto the normal, you can state what the dot and cross products each measure and prove the area interpretation, you can classify a pair of lines in space as parallel, intersecting or skew and compute the distance in each case, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can say why atan2 beats acos for a nearly parallel pair.
