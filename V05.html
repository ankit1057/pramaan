The story

Imagine you are playing coin-flip with a sneaky pirate on an old wooden ship. You bet five chocolate coins on heads. The pirate flips his shiny silver coin, and it lands on Tails! You flip again: Tails! Again: Tails! By the tenth flip, the pirate has rolled Tails ten times in a row, laughing heartily and collecting fifty of your chocolate coins.

You grab his arm and shout, "Your coin is fake and weighted!" The pirate winks and says, "Can you prove it, lad? Fair coins can land on Tails ten times in a row!"

He is right that a fair coin could land on Tails ten times. But how likely is that? In a world where the coin is completely honest and fair, the chance of seeing ten tails in a row is $(1/2)^{10}$, which is about 1 in 1,024. That tiny fraction—about 0.00097—is the p-value. It is not the probability that the pirate is a cheat. It is the probability that, assuming the pirate is an honest angel, you would see evidence at least this extreme purely by dumb luck. When that probability drops below our threshold of patience, we reject the pirate's claim of innocence.

Say it simply

Hypothesis testing formalises statistical decision-making under uncertainty. We pit a null hypothesis $H_0$ (no effect, fair coin, equal performance) against an alternative hypothesis $H_1$. A p-value is the probability, assuming $H_0$ is true, of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one computed from the data.

A p-value is NOT the probability that $H_0$ is true, nor is $1 - p$ the probability that the alternative is true. Decisions risk two errors: Type I error (false positive, rate $\alpha$) and Type II error (false negative, rate $\beta$). Testing multiple hypotheses without correction inflates the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER); corrections like Bonferroni, Holm step-down, or Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) control false claims. To prove an update caused 'no regression', standard difference tests fail; Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) for equivalence must be applied.

Say it precisely

Let sample data $X \sim P_\theta$. The test evaluates $H_0: \theta \in \Theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta \in \Theta_1$. 1. P-value Definition: For test statistic $T(X)$ where large values indicate departure from $H_0$: $$p(x) = \mathbb{P}_{H_0}(T(X) \ge T(x))$$ Under a continuous null hypothesis, the p-value is uniformly distributed on $[0, 1]$: $p \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$. 2. Error Rates: - Type I Error: $\alpha = \mathbb{P}_{H_0}(\text{Reject } H_0)$. - Type II Error: $\beta = \mathbb{P}_{H_1}(\text{Fail to reject } H_0)$. Power is $1 - \beta$. 3. Multiple Testing Adjustments for $m$ Hypotheses: - Bonferroni: Reject $H_{(i)}$ if $p_i \le \alpha / m$. Controls $\text{FWER} \le \alpha$. - Holm Step-down: Order $p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}$. Reject $H_{(i)}$ if $p_{(j)} \le \frac{\alpha}{m - j + 1}$ for all $j \le i$. Strictly more powerful than Bonferroni while controlling FWER. - Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR): Find largest $k$ such that $p_{(k)} \le \frac{k}{m} q^*$. Controls Expected False Discovery Rate $\mathbb{E}[V / R] \le q^*$. 4. Equivalence Testing (TOST): To establish that difference $\delta = \mu_1 - \mu_2$ lies within equivalence margin $[-\Delta, \Delta]$, test two simultaneous one-sided nulls: $$H_{01}: \delta \le -\Delta \quad \text{and} \quad H_{02}: \delta \ge \Delta$$ Reject both at level $\alpha \iff$ the $(1 - 2\alpha)$ confidence interval for $\delta$ falls strictly inside $[-\Delta, \Delta]$.

Why it's true

Why does testing multiple hypotheses without correction guarantee false discoveries, and why does the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure control the False Discovery Rate?

Step 1 (The probability of at least one false positive): Suppose an analytics team runs $m$ independent A/B tests on features that have zero true effect ($H_0$ is true for all $m$). If each test uses significance level $\alpha = 0.05$, the probability of correctly failing to reject a single test is $1 - \alpha = 0.95$. Because the tests are independent, the probability that all $m$ tests correctly fail to reject is: $$\mathbb{P}(\text{Zero False Positives}) = (1 - \alpha)^m$$ The Family-Wise Error Rate (the probability of at least one false alarm) is: $$\text{FWER} = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^m$$ For $m=20$ tests, $\text{FWER} = 1 - (0.95)^{20} \approx 1 - 0.358 = 64.2\%$. For $m=100$, it is $99.4\%$. Testing without correction guarantees that a team will celebrate phantom wins.

Step 2 (The False Discovery Rate framework): Controlling FWER with Bonferroni ($p \le \alpha/m$) becomes overly conservative when $m$ is large, destroying statistical power. Benjamini and Hochberg redefined the objective: instead of preventing any false positive, control the expected proportion of false positives among all rejected discoveries: $$\text{FDR} = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{V}{\max(R, 1)} \right]$$ where $V$ is false rejections and $R$ is total rejections.

Step 3 (The Benjamini-Hochberg step-up proof): Sort the p-values in ascending order: $p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}$. Find the largest index $k$ such that $p_{(k)} \le \frac{k}{m} q^*$. Reject all hypotheses $H_{(1)}, \dots, H_{(k)}$. Under independent null p-values, each null p-value is uniformly distributed. The expected number of null rejections below threshold $\frac{k}{m} q^*$ is $m_0 \frac{k}{m} q^* \le k q^*$. Dividing by total rejections $k$ yields $\mathbb{E}[V / R] \le \frac{m_0}{m} q^* \le q^*$, formally bounding the False Discovery Rate at $q^*$.

We demonstrate the inflation of the Family-Wise Error Rate under multiple comparisons. We simulate 1000 research teams.           Misconceptions
            

          • "A p-value is the probability that the null hypothesis is true." The null hypothesis is a fixed non-random proposition. The p-value is $\mathbb{P}(\text{Data} \ge \text{Observed} \mid H_0)$.
            • 

          • "Failing to reject the null hypothesis proves that there is no effect." Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A high p-value can simply reflect an underpowered study with inadequate sample size.
            • 

          • "A statistically significant result ($p < 0.05$) is practically important." With $n=1,000,000$, a microscopic 0.001% latency difference will achieve $p < 10^{-10}$, yet remain completely meaningless to real users.
            • 

          • "P-hacking requires conscious fraud or manipulation." Subconscious exploration—trying three slightly different outlier trimming rules, testing across sub-cohorts, or deciding when to stop collecting data—naturally inflates the false positive rate through the garden of forking paths.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Standard null hypothesis significance testing breaks down completely in big-data regimes ($N > 10^7$) where microscopic background noise and inevitable minor model misspecifications drive p-values to zero for virtually every hypothesis tested. In such high-powered regimes, trivial differences become statistically significant despite having zero practical engineering importance; point estimation of effect sizes and equivalence bounds must replace unadjusted p-value testing.
          In your real work
          When evaluating a rewrite — optimising a SQLite sync layer, or tuning a garbage collector — engineers often run an A/B test and conclude 'no performance regression' because $p = 0.35$ on a difference t-test. That is an invalid inference: a high p-value only means high uncertainty. Proving that an optimisation introduced no regression requires Two One-Sided Tests within a pre-declared equivalence margin.
          Flash cards
          What is the formal definition of a p-value?
          The probability, under the assumption that the null hypothesis $H_0$ is true, of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed statistic.
          What is a Type I error vs a Type II error?
          Type I error is a false positive (rejecting $H_0$ when it is true); Type II error is a false negative (failing to reject $H_0$ when the alternative is true).
          Why does the Bonferroni correction control the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER)?
          By Boole's inequality (union bound), $\mathbb{P}(\bigcup_i E_i) \le \sum_i \mathbb{P}(E_i) = m (\alpha/m) = \alpha$.
          How does the False Discovery Rate (FDR) differ from the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER)?
          FWER controls the probability of making even one false positive (stringent); FDR controls the expected proportion of false positives among all declared discoveries (practical for large $m$).
          Why is a standard t-test incapable of proving that two systems perform identically?
          Because failing to reject the null hypothesis merely indicates insufficient evidence to distinguish them, not proof of zero difference; equivalence testing (TOST) is required.
          What is 'the garden of forking paths' in data analysis?
          The phenomenon where data-dependent choices of preprocessing, metrics, and subgroup filters inflate false discovery rates even without conscious intent to p-hack.
          Stop when
          You can explain why p=0.05 does not mean 95% truth to an engineering director, implement Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction from memory, and run a TOST equivalence test to certify zero latency regression.
