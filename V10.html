The story

Imagine you are timing your little brother to see how fast he can sprint across the kitchen floor to grab a chocolate cookie from the cookie jar. The first time you yell "GO!", he is sitting tied to his chair with untied shoelaces, so he takes twelve seconds. The second time, he is standing right by the counter with his sneakers on, and he grabs the cookie in two seconds. The third time, your father walks into the kitchen and blocks the doorway, so he takes eight seconds.

If you write on your school notebook: "My brother runs at two seconds!", are you telling the truth? Or did you just pick the fastest run when everything was warm and nobody was in the way?

Now imagine a post office with one postal clerk. Ten customers arrive at 9:00 AM, but the clerk takes ten minutes to stamp each letter. The tenth customer stands in line waiting for ninety minutes before their letter is stamped in thirty seconds. If the clerk reports: "Each letter takes thirty seconds to stamp!", they have ignored the eighty-nine minutes the customer spent waiting in the cold rain! This trap is called Coordinated Omission.

Say it simply

Performance benchmarking measures how software consumes hardware resources under load. Key dimensions include latency (time to complete an individual unit of work) and throughput (units of work completed per unit time). Naive benchmarks fall victim to JIT warm-up effects, dead-code elimination, dynamic CPU frequency scaling, and coordinated omission.

Benchmarking requires reporting full distributions (p50, p95, p99) with non-parametric bootstrap confidence intervals, rather than bare arithmetic means. When comparing execution times across two software versions, interleaved paired runs eliminate temporal thermal and background noise. Crucially, speedup ratios across benchmark suites must be aggregated using the geometric mean, not the arithmetic mean.

Say it precisely

Let latency measurements $L_1, \dots, L_n$ be drawn from execution distribution $F_L$. 1. Percentile Estimators: For quantile $q \in (0, 1)$, $\hat{p}_q = L_{(\lceil n q \rceil)}$ from ordered samples $L_{(1)} \le \dots \le L_{(n)}$. 2. Coordinated Omission (Gil Tene): Closed-loop load generators wait for response $i$ before issuing request $i+1$. If request $i$ encounters a 10s GC pause, subsequent requests are delayed from entering the queue, hiding queuing delay. The corrected latency of a request scheduled at $t_{\text{sched}}$ and completed at $t_{\text{comp}}$ is $L_{\text{corr}} = t_{\text{comp}} - t_{\text{sched}}$. 3. Geometric Mean of Speedups: For speedups $S_i = T_{\text{baseline}, i} / T_{\text{optimized}, i}$ across $K$ benchmark programs: $$\text{GMean}(S) = \left( \prod_{i=1}^K S_i \right)^{1/K} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^K \ln S_i \right)$$ The geometric mean preserves symmetry: $\text{GMean}(A/B) = 1 / \text{GMean}(B/A)$. The arithmetic mean violates symmetry and allows deceptive re-normalization. 4. Interleaved Paired Testing: Alternating execution $A, B, A, B, \dots$ cancels monotonic environmental drift (thermal throttling, cache eviction).

Why it's true

Why does summarizing benchmark speedups with the arithmetic mean of ratios produce deceptive mathematical artifacts, and why does the geometric mean resolve it?

Step 1 (The re-normalization paradox): Suppose we compare two compilers, Compiler 1 and Compiler 2, on two benchmarks of equal importance. On Benchmark X, Compiler 1 runs in 1 second, and Compiler 2 runs in 10 seconds. On Benchmark Y, Compiler 1 runs in 10 seconds, and Compiler 2 runs in 1 second. The two compilers have symmetric, identical performance across the suite.

Step 2 (The arithmetic mean failure): If we normalize to Compiler 1, Compiler 2 has speedup ratios $1/10 = 0.1$ on X and $10/1 = 10.0$ on Y. The arithmetic mean of Compiler 2's speedup relative to Compiler 1 is: $$\text{AMean} = \frac{0.1 + 10.0}{2} = 5.05$$ An engineer celebrating this claims Compiler 2 is 5x faster than Compiler 1! Now normalize to Compiler 2: Compiler 1 has ratios $10/1 = 10.0$ on X and $1/10 = 0.1$ on Y. The arithmetic mean of Compiler 1's speedup relative to Compiler 2 is also $5.05$. Both claim to be 5x faster than each other!

Step 3 (The geometric mean resolution): Computing the geometric mean: $$\text{GMean}(S_2 / S_1) = \sqrt{0.1 \times 10.0} = \sqrt{1.0} = 1.0$$ $$\text{GMean}(S_1 / S_2) = \sqrt{10.0 \times 0.1} = \sqrt{1.0} = 1.0$$ The geometric mean reports ratio $1.0$, accurately reflecting that neither compiler holds an overall advantage. Only the geometric mean satisfies the property of inversion consistency: $G(x/y) = 1 / G(y/x)$.

Oracle lab

We compare two mathematically independent implementations of the geometric mean to establish a robust verification oracle. Method 1 computes the geometric mean in log-space using the closed-form formula $\exp(\frac{1}{K} \sum \ln x_i)$, preventing floating-point overflow on large suites. Method 2 computes the product iteratively and extracts the $K$-th root directly. On 50 random benchmark speedup vectors, both independent approaches must agree within strict floating-point tolerance. The broken mutant uses the arithmetic mean, which produces inconsistent inflated summaries and violates inversion symmetry.

Experiment lab
We demonstrate coordinated omission in load testing. A simulated server processes requests with a fixed 10ms execution time, but experiences a single 1000ms garbage collection pause. We compare a closed-loop benchmark (which sends the next request only after the previous returns) against an open-loop benchmark (which schedules requests at fixed 10ms intervals). In the closed-loop test, only 1 request experiences the 1000ms delay, and 99 requests report 10ms, reporting a misleading p95 of 10ms. In the open-loop test, 100 queued requests experience delays ranging from 10ms to 1000ms, correctly reporting a p95 over 500ms. We verify this massive divergence.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "Average latency is the most important performance metric." Users experience individual requests, not averages. A fast mean can easily mask severe 99th-percentile stalls caused by GC pauses or thread lock contention.
            • 

          • "Microbenchmarking a 5-line function in a tight loop reflects real-world performance." Compilers apply aggressive dead-code elimination, loop unrolling, and constant folding to microbenchmarks that never occur in production workloads.
            • 

          • "Speedup numbers across different benchmarks can be averaged with the arithmetic mean." The arithmetic mean of ratios violates inversion consistency; only the geometric mean provides a sound, symmetric aggregate speedup.
            • 

          • "Coordinated omission only happens with broken load testing tools." Most standard HTTP load tools (ApacheBench, wrk in default mode) operate in closed-loop mode, hiding massive queuing delays from the recorded percentiles.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Benchmarking distributions break down under multi-modal latency distributions (e.g. cache hit vs cache miss vs remote disk I/O) where percentiles fluctuate discontinuously across bimodal peaks. In virtualized cloud environments (AWS EC2, Docker), noisy neighbors and hypervisor CPU throttling induce non-stationary background noise that invalidates short benchmark runs.
          In your real work
          In mobile development — database migrations, on-device model loading — claiming a '4x average speedup' in a pull request using arithmetic means will not survive a senior review. Measuring tail latency (p95/p99) with coordinated-omission correction, and reporting geometric mean speedups, is what a systems reviewer expects to see.
          Flash cards
          What is Coordinated Omission in latency benchmarking?
          A measurement flaw where a slow response stalls a closed-loop load generator, preventing subsequent requests from entering the system and hiding queuing delay from latency percentiles.
          Why must speedup ratios be summarized with the geometric mean rather than the arithmetic mean?
          The geometric mean is inversion consistent ($G(A/B) = 1 / G(B/A)$), preventing arbitrary re-normalization tricks from making two identical systems look mutually superior.
          What is the difference between latency and throughput?
          Latency is the duration required to complete a single unit of work; throughput is the rate at which units of work are completed over time.
          Why are interleaved paired runs necessary when comparing two software versions?
          They cancel out time-dependent environmental drift such as thermal CPU throttling, background OS tasks, and cache state pollution.
          What is dead-code elimination in microbenchmarks?
          An optimization where the compiler notices a calculated result is never consumed externally and deletes the entire benchmarked loop, measuring zero nanoseconds.
          Why are p95 and p99 percentiles preferred over mean and standard deviation for latency?
          Latency distributions are heavily skewed and multi-modal; standard deviation assumes normal symmetry and obscures catastrophic tail latency spikes.
          Stop when
          You can explain coordinated omission using a post office line analogy, calculate the geometric mean of speedup ratios from memory, and design an interleaved paired microbenchmark harness.
