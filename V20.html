The story

In the central courtyard of a legendary university of sciences, an eager graduating scholar stands before the venerable chancellor. The scholar holds an empty leather notebook with gilt-edged parchment and asks: "Chancellor, over four years I have mastered mathematical proofs, statistical inference, software systems, and scientific writing. Now tell me: how do I discover something genuinely new that no human mind has ever known?"

The chancellor smiles warmly and points through the open archway toward the horizon: "Research is not a bolt of lightning that strikes lucky geniuses. Research is an unbending engineering discipline. You do not begin with answers; you begin with an honest question that is both novel and feasible."

The chancellor counts on her fingers: "First, you search the literature systematically so you do not foolishly reinvent an old wheel. Second, you pre-register your hypothesis and analysis plan before touching raw data, so your hopes cannot seduce your eyes into seeing patterns that do not exist. Third, you construct a calibrated measurement tool and test it against a gold standard. Fourth, you run a quiet pilot study to catch the gremlins while they are small. And finally, when the evidence has spoken, you publish your data, your code, and your limitations so that anyone on earth can verify your truth. That is how humanity pushes back the dark boundary of ignorance: one disciplined inquiry at a time."

Say it simply

An empirical research project translates an uncertain inquiry into validated scientific knowledge. The research lifecycle requires formulating a question balancing novelty and feasibility, conducting systematic literature reviews using PRISMA flow protocols, pre-registering hypotheses to prevent post-hoc bias (HARKing), building a calibrated gold set with verified precision and recall, executing a pilot study, collecting logged empirical data, and synthesizing findings into an ethical, reproducible publication.

Say it precisely

Imagine you want to prove to your friends that you are the fastest runner in the neighborhood. If you just run in your backyard and say "I was really fast!" no one will believe you. You need a strict, fair plan!

When scientists do big projects, they use a special checklist so everyone knows they didn't cheat: 1. The Perfect Question: It must be possible to test, super interesting, new, safe, and actually matter to people. 2. The Lock Box: Before you start running your experiment, you write down exactly what you are going to do, put it in a sealed envelope, and show everyone. This proves you didn't change the rules at the end just to win. 3. The Score Tracker: You count exactly how many clues you found, how many you threw away, and how many you kept. No hiding the bad clues! 4. The Quality Check: Before using a measuring tape to measure the race, you have to check if the tape is actually marked correctly. If your tool is broken, your whole experiment is broken.

Why it's true

Why do so many huge, expensive science projects fail completely? It is rarely because the scientists weren't smart enough; it's almost always because they tried to take shortcuts and didn't follow the plan!

For example, sometimes researchers ask a huge, blurry question like "Is food good for you?" You can't test that! Instead, they need a laser-focused question like "Do carrots make you see better in the dark?"

Another big mistake is the "Texas Sharpshooter" trick. Imagine someone shoots an arrow into a blank wall, and then draws a target around the arrow and yells, "Look, a bullseye!" That is totally cheating. Some scientists look at all their messy data, find one lucky coincidence, and pretend they predicted it the whole time. Writing down the plan before the experiment (putting it in the lock box) makes this cheating impossible.

Finally, if a scientist is searching for what other people have already done, they must be super organized. If they just Google around for five minutes, they might miss the fact that someone else already solved the exact same puzzle five years ago! By using a strict plan and checking all your tools, science becomes a powerful engine for discovering the real truth.

Write audit_prisma_flow(identified, duplicates, title_excluded, full_text_excluded):
Follows the PRISMA 2020 protocol for systematic reviews:
1. N_{\text{screened}} = \text{identified} - \text{duplicates}
2. N_{\text{assessed}} = N_{\text{screened}} - \text{title_excluded}
3. N_{\text{included}} = N_{\text{assessed}} - \text{full_text_excluded}


Requirements:
- All inputs must be non-negative integers.
- \text{duplicates} \le \text{identified}
- \text{title_excluded} \le N_{\text{screened}}
- \text{full_text_excluded} \le N_{\text{assessed}}
- Raise ValueError if any constraint is violated.
Return a dictionary:
{'screened': N_screened, 'assessed': N_assessed, 'included': N_included, 'inclusion_rate_pct': round((N_included / identified) * 100.0, 2)}.

Inputs:
- interview_transcripts_codes: List of lists of string thematic codes from sequential interviews.
- lookback_window: Number of consecutive trailing interviews that must yield 0 new codes to declare saturation (default 2).
    

    Rules:
1. Maintain the cumulative set of discovered codes.
2. For each interview i:
   - Record new_codes_count: number of codes in transcript i never seen in interviews 0 \dots i-1.
3. Saturation is achieved if at least lookback_window + 1 interviews have been processed, and the last lookback_window interviews each discovered exactly 0 new codes.
4. If saturation is achieved, identify the saturation_index (the 0-based interview index after which no new codes ever appeared). If not achieved, saturation_index is None.
Return dictionary:
{'total_codes': int, 'unique_codebook': list[str], 'discovery_counts': list[int], 'is_saturated': bool, 'saturation_index': int | None}.
Raise ValueError if interview_transcripts_codes is empty or lookback_window < 1.

      Write audit_preregistration(prereg_spec, execution_log):
Inputs:
- prereg_spec: Dict defining locked study plan:
  - 'primary_dv': str (primary dependent variable)
  - 'treatment_iv': str (independent variable)
  - 'min_sample_size': int
  - 'planned_test': str (e.g. 't_test', 'mann_whitney')
- execution_log: Dict logging executed study details:
  - 'reported_dv': str
  - 'tested_iv': str
  - 'sample_size': int
  - 'executed_test': str
  - 'tested_other_dvs': list of str (additional outcome variables tested)
      

      Requirements:
Detect the following deviations (HARKing / p-hacking flags):
1. 'DV_MISMATCH': if reported_dv != primary_dv.
2. 'IV_MISMATCH': if tested_iv != treatment_iv.
3. 'UNDERPOWERED_SAMPLE': if sample_size < min_sample_size.
4. 'TEST_CHANGED': if executed_test != planned_test.
5. 'UNDISCLOSED_MULTIPLICITY': if len(tested_other_dvs) > 0 and reported_dv != primary_dv.
      

      Return dictionary:
- 'compliant': Boolean True if zero deviations found.
- 'deviations': Sorted list of detected deviation flag strings.
- 'severity': 'CLEAN' if 0 deviations, 'MINOR' if only UNDERPOWERED_SAMPLE, else 'CRITICAL'.
Raise ValueError if required keys are missing from either dict.

        End-to-end research project capstone execution and synthesis engine
        
not tried
        

        Implement execute_research_capstone(gold_data, treatment_samples, baseline_samples, alpha=0.05):
Inputs:
- gold_data: List of tuples (gold_boolean, measurement_boolean) evaluating measurement instrument accuracy.
- treatment_samples: List of float outcome measurements from the intervention group.
- baseline_samples: List of float outcome measurements from the control/baseline group.
- alpha: Significance threshold (default 0.05).
        

        Pipeline Steps:
1. Instrument Calibration: Compute F_1 score on gold_data:
   - If F_1 < 0.70, the measurement instrument is uncalibrated and invalid. Return:
     {'status': 'ABORT_UNCALIBRATED_MEASUREMENT', 'instrument_f1': round(f1, 4), 'p_value': None, 'effect_size_cohen_d': None, 'verdict': 'INVALID'}.
2. Hypothesis Testing: Conduct two-sample independent Welch's t-test between treatment and baseline:
   - Compute Welch's t-statistic and two-tailed p-value using scipy.stats.ttest_ind(treatment, baseline, equal_var=False).
3. Effect Size (Cohen's d):
   d = \frac{\bar{X}T - \bar{X}_B}{s{\text{pooled}}}, \quad s_{\text{pooled}} = \sqrt{\frac{s_T^2 + s_B^2}{2}}
4. Conclusion:
   - If p < \alpha and d > 0: 'STATISTICALLY_SIGNIFICANT_IMPROVEMENT'
   - If p < \alpha and d < 0: 'STATISTICALLY_SIGNIFICANT_REGRESSION'
   - If p \ge \alpha: 'NO_SIGNIFICANT_DIFFERENCE'
        

        Return dictionary:
{'status': 'SUCCESS', 'instrument_f1': round(f1, 4), 'p_value': round(float(p), 4), 'effect_size_cohen_d': round(float(d), 4), 'verdict': verdict}.
Raise ValueError if treatment_samples or baseline_samples has length < 2.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "A good research project requires millions of dollars in compute and years of time." Breakthrough research often comes from sharp, focused, inexpensive empirical questions with clean gold-standard calibration.
            • 

          • "Pre-registration prevents researchers from exploring interesting unexpected patterns." Pre-registration clearly separates confirmatory findings from exploratory discoveries, preserving scientific integrity for both.
            • 

          • "Qualitative analysis is subjective unscientific guesswork." Rigorous thematic qualitative research uses systematic codebooks, inter-coder reliability, and strict saturation thresholds.
            • 

          • "A negative or null result means the research project failed." A rigorously pre-registered, well-powered null result that disproves an accepted myth is a high-value scientific contribution.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          The structured empirical research project lifecycle breaks down under extreme open-world non-stationarity (e.g. evaluating an algorithmic trading model during a black-swan market crash), where past gold standards and static pilot datasets no longer represent the shifting operational distribution. In non-stationary environments, online continual validation and adaptive monitoring architectures are required.
          In your real work
          In research engineering and serious feature development, a new model or synchronisation algorithm goes through this exact capstone lifecycle: a literature review of prior art, pre-registered A/B test criteria, gold-standard human validation on real records, a low-traffic canary pilot, and a final peer-reviewed engineering post-mortem.
          Flash cards
          What are the FINER criteria for a research question?
          Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, and Relevant.
          What does PRISMA stand for in systematic reviews?
          Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses; standardizes transparent paper search and exclusion flows.
          What is HARKing and why is it scientifically invalid?
          Hypothesizing After Results are Known: presenting a post-hoc finding found by data dredging as if it were a pre-planned hypothesis, inflating false-positive rates.
          Why must a measurement instrument be calibrated against a gold set before running experiments?
          If the measurement tool has low F1 accuracy, experimental results reflect the instrument's noise rather than true treatment effects.
          What is qualitative thematic saturation?
          The point during iterative qualitative data analysis where incoming interview transcripts yield no new thematic codes.
          What is the purpose of an experimental pilot study?
          To test logistical feasibility, identify unhandled edge cases, and estimate baseline variances before committing full resources.
          Stop when
          You can audit PRISMA systematic review flows, evaluate qualitative thematic saturation, verify pre-registration compliance, and execute an end-to-end research capstone pipeline.
