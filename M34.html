The story

Meera's team runs a web service. The dashboard says the average response time is 120 milliseconds, and everyone is pleased.

Then the complaints start. Users say the site is slow. Not sometimes — often enough that they mention it.

Meera pulls the raw numbers instead of the average. Most requests finish in about 40 milliseconds. A smaller group takes 200. And a thin tail — maybe one request in fifty — takes two or three seconds.

She works out what the average is doing. The few enormous values are dragging it upwards, so 120 milliseconds describes almost nobody: it is far above the typical request and far below the slow ones. Nobody experiences 120 milliseconds.

Her colleague suggests collecting more data so the average settles down. Meera tries it. With ten times the data the average does not settle — it jumps around, and occasionally jumps up, because a new record-breaking slow request arrived and pulled it.

Here is the surprise: for some distributions, collecting more data does not make the average more trustworthy. The familiar comfort that "averages converge" has conditions, and when the tail is heavy enough those conditions fail. Knowing which distribution you are in — and which theorems therefore apply — is the difference between a number that means something and a number that does not.

Say it simply

A random variable attaches numbers to outcomes. Its distribution says how the probability is spread: a PMF for discrete values, a PDF for continuous ones, and a CDF for both.

A handful of named distributions cover most situations: Bernoulli and binomial for counting successes, geometric for waiting, Poisson for rare events in a window, uniform, exponential for waiting times, and the normal for almost everything that is a sum of many small effects.

Three results do the heavy lifting. Linearity of expectation always holds. The law of large numbers says sample averages converge to the true mean — if that mean exists. The Central Limit Theorem says sums of many independent things look normal, almost regardless of what they were.

And the inequalities — Markov, Chebyshev, Hoeffding — turn "probably" into a number you can quote, which is what makes all of this engineering rather than hope.

Say it precisely

Shapes of Chance (Distributions)

When you roll dice, wait for a bus, or measure raindrops, numbers happen randomly. A distribution is the shape of that randomness. - PMF (Discrete): Used for counting things (like dice or coins). It tells you the exact chance of rolling a 4. - PDF (Continuous): Used for measuring things (like time or weight). Because time is perfectly smooth, the chance of a bus arriving at exactly 4.0000001 seconds is zero. Instead, we measure the chance it arrives within a 1-minute window. - CDF (Cumulative): The "running total". It answers: "What is the chance the bus arrives in 5 minutes or less?"

Meet the family: - Binomial: Counting "successes" (like how many heads in 10 coin flips). - Poisson: Counting rare lightning strikes in a thunderstorm. - Exponential: Measuring the waiting time between those lightning strikes. It is memoryless: waiting 10 minutes doesn't make the next strike happen any sooner. The universe forgets you are waiting. - Normal (Bell Curve): The shape of almost everything in nature.

Joint, Marginal, Conditional (Looking at Two Things)

Imagine a giant chart of every person's Height and Weight. - Joint: The chance of being exactly 6 feet tall AND 180 pounds. - Marginal: If you blur your eyes and ignore weight, what's the chance of being 6 feet tall? You just "squashed" the chart flat. - Conditional: If you know someone is 180 pounds, then what is the chance they are 6 feet tall?

Covariance measures if two things move together. If tall people are heavier, covariance is positive. If they are completely independent, covariance is zero.

Combining Randomness (Expectation and Variance)

Expectation (Mean): The balance point of the seesaw. The most beautiful rule in probability is that averages always add up. If you average 10 dollars on scratch tickets, and your friend averages 5 dollars, together you average 15 dollars. This works even if your tickets are secretly linked.

Variance (Wobbliness): How much the seesaw swings around. If you and your friend combine your money, the wobbliness only adds up cleanly if you are completely independent. If you always win when your friend wins, the wobbliness explodes!

Inequalities (Making Guesses Safe)

If you only know a few facts about a distribution, what can you safely promise? - Markov: If the average test score is 50, it is physically impossible for 60% of the class to score 100. (Requires non-negative numbers). - Chebyshev: If you know the variance (the "wobble") is small, you can guarantee very few people score far from the average. - Hoeffding: If you flip thousands of coins, the chance they average out to 90% Heads is exponentially tiny. It gives an incredibly tight lock on the average.

The Big Two Theorems

Law of Large Numbers: If you flip a coin enough times, the fraction of Heads will eventually settle exactly on 50%. The sample average hones in on the true average.

Central Limit Theorem (The Plinko Board): If you add up thousands of tiny, independent random bounces, the final result will always look like a beautiful Normal Bell Curve. It doesn't matter if the individual bounces were weirdly shaped. When you sum them up, the weirdness washes away into a perfect bell.

Simulation (Faking Randomness)

Computers can only roll flat, uniform numbers between 0 and 1. How do we fake a bell curve? - Inverse CDF: You take the uniform spinner, and map it backwards through the running total (CDF) curve. It bends the flat numbers into the right shape. - Box-Muller: A clever geometry trick to bend two uniform numbers into a perfect 2D bell curve. - Monte Carlo: If you want to measure the area of a weirdly shaped lake, don't use a grid. Just throw 10,000 random darts at a map and count how many hit water. In high dimensions, random darts are magically faster than grids.

Heavy Tails (When Averages Break)

Most things (like human height) have thin tails. Nobody is 500 feet tall. But things like internet delays, or city populations, or wealth, have heavy tails. One billionaire walking into a room breaks the "average wealth" of the room completely.

The Pareto distribution models this. If a tail is heavy enough, the variance explodes to infinity (CLT fails). If it's even heavier, the mean itself is infinite (Law of Large Numbers fails). No matter how much data you collect, the average will never settle. When tails are heavy, averages are lies. You must use quantiles (like the 99th percentile) instead.

A pinch of history

The normal distribution appears in de Moivre (1733) as an approximation to the binomial, and in Laplace and Gauss (1809) for measurement error. Laplace stated a general Central Limit Theorem in 1810. Chebyshev proved his inequality in 1867, and his student Markov the simpler one that bears his name. Kolmogorov's 1933 axioms put it all on a rigorous footing.

Box and Muller published their transform in 1958. Monte Carlo methods were developed at Los Alamos by Ulam, von Neumann and Metropolis in the 1940s, and named after the casino. Vilfredo Pareto described his distribution in 1896 studying the distribution of wealth, and Benoit Mandelbrot argued from the 1960s that heavy tails are the rule rather than the exception in economics and nature.

Why it's true

Result 1: Averages always add up, even without independence. If you have a chart of outcomes for X and Y, the average of X plus Y is just adding up every cell X plus Y times its probability. You can split this into adding all the X parts, plus adding all the Y parts. Squashing the grid horizontally gives the marginal average of X, and squashing vertically gives Y. The dependencies wash out.

Result 2: Variance needs independence. Variance is the average of the squared distances from the mean: the sum squared. When you expand this, you get X squared plus Y squared plus twice X times Y. The X squared becomes the variance of X, the Y squared becomes the variance of Y. But the twice X times Y part is the covariance. If X and Y are independent, they don't affect each other, and this cross-term becomes zero.

Result 3: Markov's Promise. If a classroom's average score is 50, what's the maximum number of students who could have scored 100? If 60% of the class scored 100, then those students alone would bring the class average to 60 (since 0.60 times 100 equals 60), which breaks the rule that the average is 50! Thus, the chance of scoring at least 100 must be at most 50/100 = 50\%.

Result 4: Why does a sample average get sharper? If you average multiple independent measurements, the true mean stays the same. What happens to the variance? Because you divide the sum by the amount of data, the variance gets multiplied by the square of one over the amount. But you added all those variances together. they cancel out to leave one over the amount. So the variance of the average shrinks proportionally. The standard deviation (the actual spread) is the square root of variance, so it shrinks by the square root of the amount of data. To make your aim twice as good, you need four times as many arrows!

Result 5: Inverse CDF. If a weather app says there's a 70% chance of rain, and your computer spins a random number 0.65, it rains! The Inverse CDF just maps that flat 0-1 uniform probability backwards onto the actual values of the distribution. Because the random number is uniform, the chance it lands below 0.70 is exactly 70%.

Oracle lab

The oracle computes distribution properties two genuinely independent ways.

The analytic route uses the closed-form mean and variance from the table, and the closed-form PMF or CDF.

The empirical route simulates a large sample and measures. It never consults a formula.

A third check runs underneath: variables generated by the inverse CDF are compared against their theoretical CDF at many points, which tests the simulation itself rather than trusting it. Three predictions.


First, the Central Limit Theorem. Sums of independent variables should look normal regardless of the original shape — provided the variance is finite. We average samples from a wildly non-normal distribution and watch the shape.


Second, the one over the square root of n law. The standard error of a mean should shrink like one over the square root of n, so a hundredfold increase in data buys a tenfold improvement and no more.


Third, and the one that matters for Meera: heavy tails break both. For a Pareto with alpha is 1 or less the mean does not exist, the sample mean never settles, and no amount of extra data helps.

def test_hoeffding_is_exponentially_tighter():
    import random
    rng = random.Random(1963)
    n = 200
    # sum of n coin flips, deviation from the mean of n/2
    trials = 4000
    deviations = []
    for _ in range(trials):
        s = sum(1 if rng.random() < 0.5 else 0 for _ in range(n))
        deviations.append(abs(s - n / 2))
    for t in (15.0, 20.0, 30.0):

            def test_box_muller_produces_standard_normals():
    import random
    rng = random.Random(1958)
    vals = []
    for _ in range(100000):
        u1 = rng.random()
        while u1 == 0.0:
            u1 = rng.random()
        z1, z2 = box_muller(u1, rng.random())
        vals.append(z1)
        vals.append(z2)
    m = sum(vals) / len(vals)
    v = sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in vals) / (len(vals) - 1)
    assert abs(m) < 0.02, m
    assert abs(v - 1.0) < 0.02, v
    # about 68% lie within one standard deviation, 95% within two
    within1 = sum(1 for x in vals if abs(x) <= 1) / len(vals)
    within2 = sum(1 for x in vals if abs(x) <= 2) / len(vals)
    assert 0.66 < within1 < 0.70, within1
    assert 0.94 < within2 < 0.96, within2
    try:
        box_muller(0.0, 0.5)
        assert False, "u1 = 0 must raise ValueError"
    except ValueError:
        pass


def test_standard_error_and_monte_carlo():
    import random
    from math import sqrt, sin, cos
    assert abs(standard_error(2.0, 100) - 0.2) < 1e-12
    # quadrupling the data halves the error
    assert abs(standard_error(1.0, 400) - standard_error(1.0, 100) / 2) < 1e-12
    rng = random.Random(1946)
    # integral of x^2 over [0, 1] is 1/3
    for n in (1000, 100000):
        draws = [rng.random() for _ in range(n)]
        est = monte_carlo_integrate(lambda x: x * x, 0.0, 1.0, draws)
        assert abs(est - 1 / 3) < 4.0 / sqrt(n), (n, est)
    # a bigger sample is usually better: compare the average error
    errs = {}
    for n in (100, 10000):
        total = 0.0
        for _ in range(200):
            draws = [rng.random() for _ in range(n)]
            total += abs(monte_carlo_integrate(lambda x: x * x, 0.0, 1.0, draws) - 1 / 3)
        errs[n] = total / 200
    assert 6.0 < errs[100] / errs[10000] < 15.0, errs


def test_pareto_and_the_existence_of_moments():
    import random
    rng = random.Random(1896)

    for x in (1.5, 2.0, 4.0):

            Misconceptions
              

            • "The average summarises the data." Only for a roughly symmetric distribution without a heavy tail. Meera's mean of 120 ms described no request at all, and most requests were faster than it.
              • 

            • "More data always makes the average more reliable." It does when the variance is finite. For a Pareto with alpha is 1 or less there is no mean to converge to, and the sample mean wanders forever.
              • 

            • "Zero correlation means independent." False. X uniform on minus one, zero, one and Y is X squared have zero covariance and Y is determined by X. Correlation detects only linear association.
              • 

            • "The Central Limit Theorem applies to everything." It needs a finite variance. For heavy-tailed data with alpha is 2 or less the sums converge to a stable law with infinite variance, not a normal.
              • 

            • "Variance adds." Only without covariance. Positively correlated variables have a variance of the sum larger than the sum of the variances — which is why correlated failures are so much worse than independent ones.
              • 

            • "A PDF value is a probability." A density can exceed 1; only its integral over an interval is a probability, and the probability of any single point is zero.
              • 

            • "Chebyshev's bound is the actual tail probability." It is a bound, often very loose: for a normal it gives 25% at two standard deviations where the truth is about 4.6%. Its virtue is that it needs almost no assumptions.
              • 

            • "Monte Carlo is inefficient because the error only falls like one over the square root of n." That rate is independent of dimension, which is why it wins in high dimensions where grid methods need n to the power of d points.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Everything rests on moments existing. The Cauchy distribution has no mean at all: the sample mean of n Cauchy draws has exactly the same Cauchy distribution as a single draw, so averaging accomplishes nothing whatsoever. That is not an edge case but the clearest statement of the limit of averaging.
            

            The CLT says nothing about how fast convergence happens. Berry-Esseen bounds the error by a fraction depending on the square root of n, and for a skewed distribution with a long tail the normal approximation can be poor even when n is in the thousands — exactly where people reach for it to justify a confidence interval.
            

            Independence is assumed throughout and routinely false. Correlated samples inflate the effective variance, so a confidence interval computed as if the data were independent is too narrow — the usual sin in time-series and A/B analysis.
            

            Simulation has its own hazards. The inverse CDF method needs an invertible CDF. Floating point truncates the tails: a 64-bit uniform cannot produce a normal beyond about 8 standard deviations, so rare-event simulation needs importance sampling rather than brute force. And a linear congruential generator has correlations that will quietly corrupt a Monte Carlo result.
            

            Finally, the empirical tail is only as good as your sample. A p99.9 estimated from a thousand samples rests on one observation, and reporting it as though it were a measurement is a fiction.
            In your real work
            Meera's problem is the daily one. Latency is heavy-tailed almost everywhere — a mixture of cache hits, cache misses, retries and timeouts — so the mean is the wrong summary and p50, p95, p99 are reported instead. The queueing explanation for why the tail explodes is M40.
            

            The one over the square root of n law prices every experiment: to halve the confidence interval of an A/B test you need four times the traffic, and that calculation decides whether a test is worth running. Hoeffding's inequality is what turns "we observed a 2% lift" into "with 95% confidence the lift is at least 1.4%".
            

            Sampling is everywhere in practice: synthetic load generation, randomised algorithms, dropout, and the reservoir sampling behind trace collection. Knowing that the exponential is memoryless tells you that a Poisson arrival process does not "build up", which is the right model for independent user requests and the wrong one for a thundering herd after a restart.
            

            And the independence caveat is the one that bites hardest. "Three replicas at 99.9% gives nine nines" assumes independent failures; a shared config service, a shared rack or a shared deploy makes them correlated, and the variance-of-sums formula says precisely how much worse that is.
            Flash cards
            Why can the mean be a bad summary?
            A heavy tail drags it above almost every observation. For skewed data the median and high quantiles describe reality; the mean describes nobody.
            When does the law of large numbers fail?
            When the mean does not exist, as for a Pareto with alpha at most 1 or a Cauchy. The sample mean then never settles.
            What does the Central Limit Theorem require?
            Independent identically distributed variables with FINITE variance. The shape of the original distribution is irrelevant.
            Does zero correlation imply independence?
            No. Correlation measures linear association only. X uniform on {-1,0,1} and Y = X^2 have zero covariance and Y is a function of X.
            Give the standard error of a sample mean and its practical consequence.
            sigma over root n. To halve the error you need four times the data.
            State Markov's and Chebyshev's inequalities and what each assumes.
            Markov: P(X >= a) <= E[X]/a, needing only non-negativity. Chebyshev: P(|X-mu| >= k sigma) <= 1/k^2, needing a finite variance. Chebyshev follows from Markov applied to the squared deviation.
            What makes Hoeffding stronger than Chebyshev?
            It assumes bounded variables and in exchange gives an exponentially small bound rather than a polynomial one.
            How does the inverse CDF method work, and when can you not use it?
            If U is uniform then F inverse of U has CDF F. It needs an invertible CDF, which the normal lacks — hence Box-Muller.
            Why is Monte Carlo integration used in high dimensions?
            Its error falls like 1/sqrt(n) independently of dimension, while grid methods need exponentially many points.
            What is special about the exponential distribution?
            It is memoryless: having waited tells you nothing about the remaining wait. It is the only continuous distribution with that property.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a colleague why the average latency describes nobody, you can derive Chebyshev from Markov and the weak law from Chebyshev, you can say exactly which hypothesis fails for heavy-tailed data and what that means for a dashboard, you can simulate any invertible distribution by inverse CDF and explain why the normal needs Box-Muller, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you reach for quantiles rather than a mean whenever a distribution is skewed.
