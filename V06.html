The story

Imagine you are a detective searching for a hidden mouse in a cavernous, pitch-black underground castle. You carry a tiny handheld candle that casts a faint glow only two feet around your boots. You spend ten minutes walking around the cavern, you see no mouse, and you report to the king: "Your Majesty, I have proven there are no mice in this castle!"

The king laughs and points out: "With that tiny candle, a mouse could be sitting five feet away in the shadows and you would never see it! Your flashlight was not strong enough to find the mouse."

In statistics, the brightness of your flashlight is called statistical power. If an effect is real—if there really is a bug in the code or a performance improvement in the engine—statistical power is the probability that your experiment will actually detect it. If you test a new app version on only twenty users, your candle is so dim that you will almost certainly miss real improvements. Worse yet, if you do happen to find an improvement in an underpowered study, it is almost certainly a massive exaggeration called the Winner's Curse.

Say it simply

Statistical power ($1 - \beta$) is the probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis. Power depends on four coupled parameters: significance level $\alpha$, sample size $n$, effect size (such as Cohen's $d$ or Cohen's $h$), and population variance. Specifying any three uniquely determines the fourth.

An underpowered study has low probability of detecting real effects. Furthermore, due to conditioning on statistical significance, statistically significant results from underpowered studies suffer from the 'Winner's Curse' (type M error or magnitude exaggeration), heavily overestimating the true effect size. For non-parametric comparisons, Vargha-Delaney $A_{12}$ and Cliff's $\delta$ quantify stochastic dominance and effect magnitude without normality assumptions.

Say it precisely

Let $H_0: \theta = 0$ vs $H_1: \theta = \delta > 0$. Under test statistic $Z = \frac{\hat{\theta}}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$, decision rule rejects if $Z \ge z_{1-\alpha}$. 1. Statistical Power: $$\text{Power} = \mathbb{P}_{H_1}(Z \ge z_{1-\alpha}) = \Phi\left( \frac{\delta \sqrt{n}}{\sigma} - z_{1-\alpha} \right) = 1 - \beta$$ 2. Sample Size Determination: $$n = \frac{(z_{1-\alpha} + z_{1-\beta})^2 \sigma^2}{\delta^2}$$ 3. Parametric Effect Sizes: - Cohen's d (continuous): $d = \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_2}{\sigma_{\text{pooled}}}$. - Cohen's h (proportions): $h = 2 \arcsin(\sqrt{p_1}) - 2 \arcsin(\sqrt{p_2})$. 4. Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE): The smallest effect size detectable with power $1 - \beta$ at level $\alpha$: $$\text{MDE} = (z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}) \sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}$$ 5. Non-parametric Effect Sizes: - Vargha-Delaney $A_{12}$: Probability that a randomly chosen observation from group 1 is greater than from group 2: $$A_{12} = \frac{R_1 - n_1(n_1 + 1)/2}{n_1 n_2}$$ - Cliff's Delta $\delta$: Degree of overlap between two distributions: $\delta = 2 A_{12} - 1$.

Why it's true

Why do underpowered studies inevitably suffer from the 'Winner's Curse' (magnitude exaggeration), and why does $n=40$ per group fail to detect typical software improvements?

Step 1 (Conditioning on the selection filter): Suppose a team tests 100 features. The true effect size of a successful feature is small: Cohen's $d = 0.20$. With $n=40$ per group, standard error is $\text{SE} = \sqrt{2/40} = 0.2236$. The critical difference required for two-sided significance at $\alpha = 0.05$ is: $$\text{Threshold} = 1.96 \times \text{SE} = 1.96 \times 0.2236 \approx 0.438$$

Step 2 (The magnitude gap): Notice that the critical threshold $0.438$ is more than double the true effect size $0.20$! For a sample to be declared statistically significant, the observed sample mean must be at least $0.438$. Therefore, any study that achieves $p < 0.05$ MUST, by mathematical necessity, over-estimate the true effect by at least $119\%$.

Step 3 (The Winner's Curse expectation): Let $D$ be the observed sample effect. By conditioning on significance: $$\mathbb{E}[D \mid D \ge 1.96 \cdot \text{SE}] = d + \text{SE} \cdot \frac{\phi((1.96 \cdot \text{SE} - d)/\text{SE})}{1 - \Phi((1.96 \cdot \text{SE} - d)/\text{SE})} \gg d$$ The published or celebrated estimate is heavily biased upward. When the team ships the feature to production, the observed gain disappears or drops by half, causing confusion. The feature was not broken; the initial measurement was merely a victim of the Winner's Curse in an underpowered experiment.

For baseline conversion $p_1 = 0.10$ and improved conversion $p_2 = 0.20$, Cohen's $h = 2 \arcsin(\sqrt{0.20}) - 2 \arcsin(\sqrt{0.10}) \approx 0.2838$. Method 1 computes sample size via the Fleiss-Levin formula: $n \approx 2 (z_\alpha + z_\beta)^2 / h^2$. Method 2 runs a Monte Carlo simulation of 1000 A/B trials at that calculated $n$ and measures empirical rejection rate. The empirical power must match the nominal $80\%$ target within statistical tolerance. The broken mutant uses Cohen's d formula on proportions, which severely underestimates the required sample size.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "If an experiment is statistically significant, it was sufficiently powered." An underpowered study can still reach significance through lucky sampling noise; however, its effect size estimate will be heavily exaggerated by the Winner's Curse.
            • 

          • "A sample size of $n=40$ per group is sufficient for A/B testing user conversions." With $n=40$ and baseline $p_1 = 0.075$, the minimum detectable effect is a massive conversion jump to $p_2 \approx 0.25$; typical 1-2% software improvements have less than 10% statistical power.
            • 

          • "Non-parametric tests have zero power compared to parametric tests." The asymptotic relative efficiency of the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test compared to Student's t-test is 95.5% on normal data, and can be infinitely higher on heavy-tailed distributions.
            • 

          • "Statistical power only matters before running the experiment, not when interpreting results." Retrospective awareness of design power is critical: failing to reject $H_0$ in an underpowered study provides near-zero evidential support for the null hypothesis.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Classical power calculations break down when sample observations violate the independence assumption due to temporal autocorrelation, user clustering across devices, or network interference spillovers. In modern continuous A/B testing where dashboards update metrics continuously, peeking at p-values without alpha-spending functions drastically inflates false positive rates and invalidates standard fixed-sample power calculations.
          In your real work
          When benchmarking Flutter frame rendering speeds or LLM prompt latency, developers frequently compare $n=30$ runs between two branches. If a real performance optimization improves render speed by 2%, that sample size has statistical power below 12%. When the developer finds no statistically significant difference, they mistakenly abandon a valid optimization. Calculating MDE prior to benchmarking prevents premature abandonment.
          Flash cards
          What is statistical power?
          The probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis when the alternative hypothesis is true ($1 - \beta$).
          What is Cohen's d vs Cohen's h?
          Cohen's d measures standardized mean differences between continuous variables ($d = \Delta / \sigma$); Cohen's h measures differences between proportions using the arcsin transformation ($h = 2 \arcsin\sqrt{p_1} - 2 \arcsin\sqrt{p_2}$).
          What is the Winner's Curse in scientific experiments?
          The phenomenon where statistically significant results from low-powered studies systematically overestimate the true effect size because only extreme positive noise crosses the significance threshold.
          What is the Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)?
          The smallest true effect size that an experiment with sample size $n$ has a specified probability (e.g. 80%) of detecting at significance level $\alpha$.
          What does Vargha-Delaney $A_{12} = 0.70$ mean?
          There is a 70% probability that a randomly drawn observation from group 1 will be strictly greater than a randomly drawn observation from group 2 (with ties counting as 0.5).
          Why does an underpowered experiment waste engineering resources?
          Because it has a high probability of missing real improvements (Type II error) and an extreme probability of exaggerating noise if significance is achieved (Winner's Curse).
          Stop when
          You can compute the sample size required for an 80% powered A/B test without consulting a cheat sheet, explain the Winner's Curse to product leadership, and calculate Cliff's delta on skewed latency logs.
