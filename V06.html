The story

Imagine you are a detective searching for a hidden mouse in a cavernous, pitch-black underground castle. You carry a tiny handheld candle that casts a faint glow only two feet around your boots. You spend ten minutes walking around the cavern, you see no mouse, and you report to the king: "Your Majesty, I have proven there are no mice in this castle!"

The king laughs and points out: "With that tiny candle, a mouse could be sitting five feet away in the shadows and you would never see it! Your flashlight was not strong enough to find the mouse."

In statistics, the brightness of your flashlight is called statistical power. If an effect is real—if there really is a bug in the code or a performance improvement in the engine—statistical power is the probability that your experiment will actually detect it. If you test a new app version on only twenty users, your candle is so dim that you will almost certainly miss real improvements. Worse yet, if you do happen to find an improvement in an underpowered study, it is almost certainly a massive exaggeration called the Winner's Curse.

Say it simply

Statistical power (one minus beta) is the probability of correctly detecting a real difference when one exists. Power depends on four closely linked parameters: the significance false-alarm threshold alpha, sample size n, effect size measuring how large the real improvement is, and population variance. Specifying any three uniquely determines the fourth.

An underpowered study has low probability of detecting real effects. Furthermore, due to conditioning on statistical significance, statistically significant results from underpowered studies suffer from the 'Winner's Curse' (magnitude exaggeration), heavily overestimating the true effect size. For non-parametric comparisons, Vargha-Delaney A12 and Cliff's delta quantify stochastic dominance and effect magnitude without requiring normal bell curves.

Say it precisely

  1. The Flashlight Beam (Statistical Power): Imagine searching for a toy mouse hidden in a dark basement. If you bring a tiny birthday candle, you can stare right at the mouse and never notice it! Statistical power is bringing a giant stadium spotlight: if a real mouse is sitting in the room, your light is guaranteed to spot it eighty or ninety percent of the time.
  2. The Size of the Treasure (Effect Size): Spotting a giant elephant standing in your backyard is very easy even with a dim flashlight, but spotting a tiny ladybug requires thousands of careful looks. Effect size measures the actual physical magnitude of a real-world difference, rather than whether a computer calculated a tiny p-value.
  3. The Fishing Net Mesh (Minimum Detectable Effect): If you take a fishing net with three-inch gaps into the river, you cannot catch one-inch minnows no matter how long you stand in the water! The Minimum Detectable Effect is the smallest prize that an experiment of your specific size can realistically catch.
  4. The Trophy Lie (The Winner's Curse): If twenty children fish with loose, broken nets, nineteen children catch nothing. But if one lucky child accidentally snags a prize-winning giant bass by pure chance, everyone boasts that the pond is filled with prize fish! Small experiments that happen to hit statistical significance are almost always massive exaggerations of the truth.

Why it's true

Why do underpowered studies inevitably suffer from the Winner's Curse, exaggerating tiny improvements into giant illusions?

  1. The High Jump Bar: Imagine an athletic contest where an athlete only receives a gold medal if they clear a high-jump bar set at four feet high. But the true physical ability of every contestant is only two feet high! Under normal circumstances, nobody would ever win a medal.
  2. The Lucky Gust of Wind: Every once in a while, a contestant runs toward the bar just as a freak upward gust of wind catches their shirt, lifting them clear over the four-foot bar! The judges throw confetti, call the television news, and crown a new superstar. But that athlete cannot jump four feet; their true jump was two feet, inflated by two extra feet of pure lucky turbulence.
  3. The Selection Filter: Because the experiment only reports results that clear the strict significance threshold, it throws away all ninety-nine honest, average measurements and celebrates the single freak, wind-assisted measurement.
  4. The Production Heartbreak: When that feature rolls out to millions of real users, the lucky gust of wind is gone! The true improvement is modest, and engineers wonder why their celebrated breakthrough suddenly shrank. The math proves that whenever a study tests too few users, any result lucky enough to win significance is mathematically guaranteed to exaggerate the truth.

Oracle lab

We compare the analytical Cohen's h sample size formula for proportions against empirical power simulation across repeated experimental trials. For a baseline conversion rate of ten percent and an improved conversion rate of twenty percent, Cohen's h measures the standardized difference in proportions via the arcsin transformation. Method one computes required sample size using the standard analytical formula based on normal critical values. Method two runs a Monte Carlo simulation of one thousand independent A/B trials at that exact calculated sample size and measures the empirical rejection rate. The empirical power matches the nominal eighty percent target within statistical tolerance. The broken mutant uses a naive continuous effect size formula directly on raw proportions, which severely underestimates the required sample size and produces underpowered experiments.

          Misconceptions
            

          • "If an experiment is statistically significant, it was sufficiently powered." An underpowered study can still reach significance through lucky sampling noise; however, its effect size estimate will be heavily exaggerated by the Winner's Curse.
            • 

          • "A sample size of forty users per group is sufficient for A/B testing user conversions." With forty users per group and a baseline conversion of 7.5%, the minimum detectable effect is a massive conversion jump to approximately 25%; typical 1-2% software improvements have less than 10% statistical power.
            • 

          • "Non-parametric tests have zero power compared to parametric tests." The asymptotic relative efficiency of the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test compared to Student's t-test is 95.5% on normal data, and can be infinitely higher on heavy-tailed distributions.
            • 

          • "Statistical power only matters before running the experiment, not when interpreting results." Retrospective awareness of design power is critical: failing to reject the null hypothesis in an underpowered study provides near-zero evidential support for the claim that no difference exists.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Classical power calculations break down when sample observations violate the independence assumption due to temporal autocorrelation, user clustering across devices, or network interference spillovers. In modern continuous A/B testing where dashboards update metrics continuously, peeking at p-values without alpha-spending functions drastically inflates false positive rates and invalidates standard fixed-sample power calculations.
          In your real work
          When benchmarking Flutter frame rendering speeds or LLM prompt latency, developers frequently compare thirty runs between two branches. If a real performance optimization improves render speed by 2%, that sample size has statistical power below 12%. When the developer finds no statistically significant difference, they mistakenly abandon a valid optimization. Calculating MDE prior to benchmarking prevents premature abandonment.
          Flash cards
          What is statistical power?
          The probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis when the alternative hypothesis is true, equal to one minus beta.
          What is Cohen's d vs Cohen's h?
          Cohen's d measures standardized mean differences between continuous variables (difference divided by pooled standard deviation); Cohen's h measures differences between proportions using the arcsin transformation.
          What is the Winner's Curse in scientific experiments?
          The phenomenon where statistically significant results from low-powered studies systematically overestimate the true effect size because only extreme positive noise crosses the significance threshold.
          What is the Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)?
          The smallest true effect size that an experiment with a given sample size has a specified probability (such as 80%) of detecting at a chosen significance level alpha.
          What does Vargha-Delaney A12 = 0.70 mean?
          There is a 70% probability that a randomly drawn observation from group 1 will be strictly greater than a randomly drawn observation from group 2 (with ties counting as 0.5).
          Why does an underpowered experiment waste engineering resources?
          Because it has a high probability of missing real improvements (Type II error) and an extreme probability of exaggerating noise if significance is achieved (Winner's Curse).
          Stop when
          You can compute the sample size required for an 80% powered A/B test without consulting a cheat sheet, explain the Winner's Curse to product leadership, and calculate Cliff's delta on skewed latency logs.
