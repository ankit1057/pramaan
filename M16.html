The story

Before the wedding feast, little Ira is told to find out whether there are enough chairs for the children. She cannot count past twenty and there are far more children than that.

Her uncle shows her a trick. "Do not count anything. Just tell every child to go and sit on a chair."

The children scatter. Every child finds a chair. No chair is left empty.

"Now you know," says her uncle. "Same number. You never counted."

Ira is delighted and starts matching things all afternoon. Spoons to bowls. Then she tries something cheeky: she matches every whole number to its double. One to two, two to four, three to six. Every whole number gets partnered, and every even number gets used. So there are as many even numbers as numbers altogether — even though the evens are only half of them.

She tells her uncle, expecting to be corrected. He says she is right, and that infinity is simply like that.

Then he asks the question that ends her afternoon. "Try to match the whole numbers to all the decimals between nought and one."

She tries. She tries a hundred arrangements. And he shows her that whatever list she writes, he can always build a decimal that differs from her first number in the first place, her second in the second place, and so on forever — a number that cannot be anywhere on her list.

Here is the surprise: matching is the real meaning of "same size", it works for infinite collections too, and when you use it honestly you discover that some infinities are strictly bigger than others.

Say it simply

A set is a collection of distinct things, with no order and no repeats. That is almost too simple to be useful, and yet nearly all of mathematics is built on it.

Sets combine: union (everything in either), intersection (everything in both), complement (everything not in it). Draw them as overlapping circles and the rules become obvious.

A relation says which things are connected to which — it is just a set of ordered pairs. A function is a relation with one rule added: every input gets exactly one output. That single restriction is what makes functions worth a name.

Ira's trick is the punchline. A function that pairs two sets up perfectly — nothing missed, nothing doubled — is a bijection, and that is the definition of "the same size". Applied to infinite sets it gives genuinely strange and completely rigorous answers.

Say it precisely

Sets

A set is just a bag of things. It does not matter what order they are in, and you cannot have two of the exact same thing in a set. - Roster form is just listing everything in the bag: A equals curly bracket two, three, five, seven curly bracket. - Set-builder form is putting a rule on the bag: A equals curly bracket x, where x is all prime numbers under ten curly bracket.

Some special bags: the empty set is a bag with absolutely nothing in it. The universal set U is a giant bag that holds absolutely everything we are talking about right now. If every toy in bag A is also inside bag B, then A is a subset of B.

Operations (Playing with bags): - Union: Dump both bags into one giant bag! Everything from A and B combined. - Intersection: Only keep the toys that were in both bags at the exact same time. - Complement: Everything in the giant Universal bag that is not in bag A.

De Morgan's laws are two cool rules about opposites: 1. If something is NOT in the giant dump-everything bag (Union), then it must be NOT in A and NOT in B. 2. If something is NOT in the shared-toys bag (Intersection), then it must be NOT in A or NOT in B.

Relations

A relation is a rule that connects things in one set to things in another. Like drawing lines between children and their favorite colors. When a relation connects a set to itself, we can check three special rules: 1. Reflexive: Is everyone connected to themselves? 2. Symmetric: If Alex is connected to Bailey, is Bailey connected to Alex? (Like a two-way street). 3. Transitive: If Alex is connected to Bailey, and Bailey is connected to Charlie, does the rule automatically connect Alex to Charlie?

If a relation passes all three tests, it is called an equivalence relation. It works like a giant sorting machine, perfectly sorting everyone into separate, non-overlapping groups (called equivalence classes).

Functions

A function is a very strict relation. It is a machine where you put exactly one thing in (from the domain bag), and exactly one thing comes out (into the codomain bag). You can never put in an apple and get two different answers!

  • Injective (One-to-one): Every input gets its very own unique output. Nobody shares!
  • Surjective (Onto): Every single thing in the output bag gets hit by the machine. Nothing is left out!
  • Bijective: The machine is both Injective and Surjective. It is a perfect pairing! Every input has exactly one partner, and no one is left alone.

Countability

Can we count to infinity? It depends! A set is countable if we can line everything up and point to them one by one: "You are number 1, you are number 2..." even if it takes forever. Surprisingly, you can do this with fractions! But the real numbers (every possible decimal) are so packed together that they are completely un-countable.

A pinch of history

A brilliant mathematician named Georg Cantor created "set theory" in the 1870s. He proved that some infinities are actually bigger than other infinities! At first, other mathematicians thought his ideas were crazy, but today, all of math is built on top of his rules.

Later, people realized you could not just throw anything into a set bag. A mathematician named Bertrand Russell asked a tricky question: "What if there is a bag of all bags that do not contain themselves? Does it contain itself?" It is a riddle that breaks the rules, so mathematicians had to invent stricter rules for how to build sets safely.

Why it's true

Why are there so many subsets? Imagine you have a bag with exactly ten toys, and you want to make a brand new smaller bag. For every single toy in the big bag, you flip a shiny coin: heads means it goes in the new bag, tails means it stays out. Since every toy has exactly two choices (in or out), and you make ten choices in a row, the total number of possible new bags you can make is two times two times two times two... ten times! That makes over a thousand different bags just from ten toys!

Why does De Morgan's Law work perfectly? Let us prove that NOT in (A dump B) is exactly the same as (NOT in A) AND (NOT in B). 1. If a toy is absolutely NOT in the giant dump-everything bag, that means it definitely did not come from bag A, and it definitely did not come from bag B. 2. So, it must be NOT in A, and it must be NOT in B! It is just plain common sense! It works perfectly in both directions, making it a magic unbreakable math rule.

Oracle lab

The oracle checks the algebra of sets two genuinely independent ways.

The first uses set operations: Python's own |, & and - on frozenset objects. The second uses membership predicates: for each element of the universe it evaluates a boolean expression and collects the elements for which it is true. One manipulates whole collections; the other reasons one element at a time with and, or and not. They have no step in common, and the fact that they agree is the correspondence between set algebra and boolean logic.

The power set is also built twice: once by recursion, once by bitmask enumeration — which is exactly the bijection from Result 1, made executable. Two predictions.


First, counting functions. Theory says that between a set of size m and a set of size n there are exactly n^m functions and n * (n - 1) * ... * (n - m + 1) injections. We do not trust that; we enumerate every function for small m and n and count.

def test_mod_three_is_an_equivalence_relation():

            Misconceptions
              

            • "The codomain and the range are the same thing." The codomain is declared; the range is what the function actually hits. f(x) = x^2 from real numbers to real numbers has range [0, infinity) and is not surjective — but the very same rule is surjective if you declare the codomain to be [0, infinity). Surjectivity is a property of the triple, not of the formula.
              • 

            • "The complement of A union B is complement A union complement B." Complementing swaps the operation to intersection. The same slip in code is writing !(a || b) as !a || !b.
              • 

            • "A relation that is symmetric and transitive must be reflexive." The usual bogus proof says aRb and bRa give aRa — but it needs some b related to a to start, and an element related to nothing breaks it. The empty relation on a non-empty set is symmetric and transitive but not reflexive.
              • 

            • "The rationals are more numerous than the integers, since they are dense." Both are countably infinite; there is an explicit bijection. Density and cardinality are unrelated.
              • 

            • "Infinity is infinity." Cantor's theorem says the size of the power set of A is always strictly greater than the size of A, so there is an endless hierarchy of infinities, and the reals are strictly more numerous than the naturals.
              • 

            • "f^{-1} exists whenever f has a formula you can rearrange." It exists exactly when f is a bijection. sine has no inverse until you restrict its domain, which is precisely what M15's principal values do.
              • 

            • "floor(-2.3) = -2." It is -3. The floor is the greatest integer not exceeding the number, and truncation towards zero is a different operation.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Naive set theory, where any describable collection counts as a set, is inconsistent — Russell's paradox is the proof. Working mathematics uses ZFC, which only permits sets built by specified constructions, and the cost is that you can no longer form "the set of all sets".
            

            Countability arguments can be delicate. The rationals are countable, and so are the algebraic numbers, and even the set of all computer programs — yet the reals are not. A consequence is that almost every real number is not computable: there are only countably many programs and uncountably many reals, so most reals have no finite description at all.
            

            In code, the mathematical idealisations leak. A set requires its elements to be hashable and to have consistent equality, and mutating an element after insertion corrupts the structure. Floating-point values make an awkward element type because nan != nan, so a set can contain several indistinguishable nans. And the mathematical function f(x) = 1/x has domain of real numbers excluding zero, a restriction that a language will happily let you forget until it raises at runtime — which is why the exercise makes the quotient's domain explicit.
            

            Finally, the equality of sets ignores order and multiplicity. When those matter you need sequences or multisets, and reaching for a set because it is convenient is a real source of bugs.
            In your real work
            This is the vocabulary of types and collections. A function's domain and codomain are its parameter and return types, and a nullable return is the honest admission that the range does not fill the codomain. Set, Map and their operations are these definitions; De Morgan is daily work in simplifying conditionals and database predicates. Equivalence relations are what "group by" means, and the partition theorem is the guarantee that grouping assigns every row to exactly one bucket. Injectivity is the question behind every unique index and every hash-collision worry. And the countability hierarchy is the ancestor of undecidability: there are more problems than programs, which is why some problems have no program.
            Flash cards
            What is the difference between the codomain and the range?
            The codomain is the declared target set; the range is the set of values actually attained. Surjective means they coincide.
            Why does a set with n elements have 2^n subsets?
            Each element is independently in or out, giving a bijection between subsets and binary strings of length n.
            State both De Morgan laws in one sentence.
            Complementing a union gives the intersection of the complements, and complementing an intersection gives the union of the complements.
            What three properties make an equivalence relation, and what do they buy you?
            Reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Together they partition the set into disjoint equivalence classes covering everything.
            Give a relation that is symmetric and transitive but not reflexive.
            The empty relation on a non-empty set. The usual "proof" that symmetry plus transitivity implies reflexivity needs an element to be related to something first.
            Sketch Cantor's diagonal argument.
            Assume the reals in [0,1] are listed. Build a number differing from the k-th listed number in its k-th digit. It is in [0,1] but on no row, contradicting completeness of the list.
            Are the rationals countable? What about the reals?
            The rationals are countable, by enumerating pairs along diagonals. The reals are not, by the diagonal argument.
            When does a function have an inverse?
            Exactly when it is a bijection: injective so the inverse is well defined, and surjective so the inverse is defined everywhere on the codomain.
            What is the floor of -2.3, and why do people get it wrong?
            Minus 3. The floor is the greatest integer not exceeding the value, which is not the same as truncating towards zero.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why matching beats counting and why that makes some infinities bigger, you can prove the power-set count and both De Morgan laws, you can show that an equivalence relation partitions its set and say exactly where each of the three properties is used, you can run Cantor's diagonal argument and explain why adding the new number to the list does not save it, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can say why almost every real number has no finite description.
