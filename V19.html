The story

In the grand architectural council of an ancient imperial metropolis, hundreds of builders and stone carvers gather to submit proposals for the great harbor bridge. A young stone carver steps onto the podium holding a rolled parchment ninety feet long. He begins to read in an excited, breathless voice, describing the poetic beauty of the seagulls, the philosophical spirit of stone masonry, the history of his grandfather's chisel, and fifty pages of rambling equations without labels.

After forty minutes of exhausting confusion, the imperial chief architect gently raises her hand: "Young man, stop. Who did you write this document for?"

The carver blinks in surprise: "I wrote it for myself, to show the brilliance of my vision!"

The chief architect opens her own leather folder, revealing a crisp two-page design document. At the very top, two lines clearly state the context and customer problem. Below it, three bullet points list explicit non-goals. A clean table contrasts three architectural alternatives, with estimated tonnage, construction duration, and collapse risks.

"A technical document," she says quietly, "is not a diary or a stage for your ego. You are writing for an exhausted, busy reader who has fifteen minutes between crisis meetings. If your reader cannot grasp what problem you are solving, what you explicitly decided NOT to do, and what evidence proves your bridge will stand within three minutes of scanning, your document has failed—no matter how brilliant your chisel."

Say it simply

Technical and scientific writing is reader-focused engineering communication. Academic papers follow the IMRaD structure (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion), anchoring every claim to measurable empirical evidence. Engineering design documents follow a strict pattern: Context, Goals, Non-Goals, Proposed Architecture, and Alternatives Considered. Clear writing uses plain language, honest figures without misleading truncated axes, and structured peer review rebuttals. Authorship credit is formally governed by the 14 CRediT roles, with mandatory disclosure of LLM tools.

Say it precisely

Let $D$ be a technical communication artifact (paper, design document, or RFC). 1. IMRaD Structure: - Introduction: Why does this problem matter? (Context $\to$ Knowledge Gap $\to$ Hypothesis). - Methods: How was the hypothesis tested? (Sufficiently detailed for replication). - Results: What did the measurement reveal? (Objective facts without commentary). - Discussion: What do the results mean? (Interpretation, limitations, and threats to validity). 2. Design Document Standard (Google L5): - Context & Problem Statement: Business/technical motivation. - Goals & Non-Goals: Explicit scope boundaries (defining non-goals is critical). - Architectural Specification: Detailed system decomposition and sequence diagrams. - Alternatives Considered: Rigorous evaluation of at least two rejected alternatives with explicit trade-offs. - Cross-Cutting Concerns: Security, privacy, observability, and failure modes. 3. Readability & Plain Language: Measured via Flesch Reading Ease: $$\text{FRE} = 206.835 - 1.015 \left(\frac{\text{words}}{\text{sentences}}\right) - 84.6 \left(\frac{\text{syllables}}{\text{words}}\right)$$ 4. CRediT Taxonomy: 14 contributor roles (e.g. Conceptualization, Software, Formal Analysis, Writing).

Why it's true

Why do technical ideas fail in high-stakes organizations despite having flawless underlying engineering? In software engineering and scientific research, brilliant prototypes die in review because the author failed to communicate with empathy for the reader. An engineer who writes a forty-page design document without an executive summary or non-goals forces their team to spend dozens of hours guessing the scope, leading to review stalemates and delayed launches.

Technical writing failures stem from four pervasive anti-patterns: 1. The Missing Non-Goals Trap: When an author fails to list non-goals, every reviewer projects their own favorite feature into the project, derailing the proposal with scope creep. 2. Visual Misdirection in Figures: Truncating the y-axis (e.g. starting a latency bar chart at 95ms instead of 0ms) visually exaggerates a 1% performance variation into an apparent 10x breakthrough. 3. Defensive Rebuttal Syndrome: When reviewers identify genuine weaknesses, immature authors write emotional, defensive replies. Professional authors construct structured rebuttal matrices: acknowledging valid critique with gratitude, citing concrete textual changes, and presenting additional empirical measurements. 4. The Curse of Knowledge: Authors assume readers know acronyms and internal constraints that exist only in the author's head.

Structuring technical communication using plain language, unambiguous design docs, and empirical evidence matrices turns architectural conflict into collaborative engineering progress.

Compute Flesch Reading Ease score for plain-language technical writing

not tried


Write flesch_reading_ease(text) to compute the readability of technical prose:
$$\text{FRE} = 206.835 - 1.015 \left(\frac{\text{total\_words}}{\text{total\_sentences}}\right) - 84.6 \left(\frac{\text{total\_syllables}}{\text{total\_words}}\right)$$
Rules:
1. Sentence splitting: Split text by sentence terminators (., !, ?). Non-empty trimmed tokens count as sentences.
2. Word splitting: Extract words containing alphanumeric characters. Ignore punctuation.
3. Syllable counting: For each word, count vowel sequences (a, e, i, o, u, y, case-insensitive). Do not count silent terminal 'e' if length > 2 and word does not end in 'le'. Every word has at least 1 syllable.
4. If total_words == 0 or total_sentences == 0, raise ValueError.
5. Return float FRE rounded to 2 decimal places.

Inputs:

4. For 'MAJOR' comments: author cannot dismiss with 'CLARIFY' without providing at least one modified line number.
Return dictionary:
- 'rebuttal_accepted': Boolean True if all conditions met.
- 'unaddressed_comments': List of comment IDs without responses.
- 'violations': List of dicts: {'comment_id': str, 'reason': str}.

    res = reconcile_rebuttal(comments, responses)
    assert res['rebuttal_accepted'] is True
    assert res['violations'] == []
    assert res['unaddressed_comments'] == []

def test_rebuttal_short_disagreement():
    comments = [{'id': 'C1', 'reviewer': 1, 'severity': 'MINOR', 'text': 'Use Python instead of C'}]
    responses = [{'comment_id': 'C1', 'decision': 'DISAGREE_WITH_EVIDENCE', 'explanation': 'No, C is better.', 'line_numbers': []}]
    res = reconcile_rebuttal(comments, responses)
    assert res['rebuttal_accepted'] is False
    assert any('20 words' in v['reason'] for v in res['violations'])

def test_rebuttal_unaddressed():
    comments = [{'id': 'C1', 'reviewer': 1, 'severity': 'MAJOR', 'text': 'Flaw'}, {'id': 'C2', 'reviewer': 2, 'severity': 'MINOR', 'text': 'Small'}]
    responses = [{'comment_id': 'C1', 'decision': 'AGREE', 'explanation': 'Done', 'line_numbers': [1]}]
    res = reconcile_rebuttal(comments, responses)
    assert res['rebuttal_accepted'] is False
    assert 'C2' in res['unaddressed_comments']

          

          Misconceptions
            

          • "Good technical writing means using complex vocabulary and passive voice." Modern scientific and technical communication demands plain language and active voice to maximize comprehension speed.
            • 

          • "Design documents are only needed for juniors." Senior and principal engineers write design documents to align cross-functional teams and identify hidden architectural risks before writing code.
            • 

          • "Non-goals are optional and just waste space." Non-goals are the most critical section of any design doc; they protect the project from scope creep and unrealistic expectations.
            • 

          • "When a peer reviewer critiques your paper, you must accept every suggestion." Reviewers are frequently mistaken; authors can disagree respectfully by providing rigorous empirical counter-evidence.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Structured writing frameworks break down completely when organizational incentives reward page count, vanity metrics, or bureaucratic template compliance rather than engineering velocity and intellectual honesty. In high-stakes emergency incident response (SEV-0 outages), writing long design documents is actively counterproductive; concise incident timelines, terminal diffs, and rapid post-mortems take immediate precedence over elaborate architectural blueprints.
          In your real work
          At Google, Meta and comparable engineering organisations, L5+ promotion and architectural sign-off strictly require authoring peer-reviewed design documents with explicit Non-Goals and Alternatives Considered. Rebuttals to engineering directors and conference reviewers require line-numbered traceability matrices to guarantee every critique is resolved.
          Flash cards
          What is the IMRaD structure in scientific papers?
          Introduction (why), Methods (how), Results (what was found), and Discussion (what it means).
          Why are Non-Goals mandatory in Google L5 design documents?
          They explicitly delimit scope boundaries and prevent scope creep from derailing system delivery.
          What is the Flesch Reading Ease score?
          A quantitative readability metric based on average sentence length and syllable count per word; higher scores indicate clearer plain language.
          What is the CRediT taxonomy?
          A standardized 14-role taxonomy defining contributor authorship credit (e.g. Conceptualization, Software, Supervision).
          What three elements must appear in every peer-review rebuttal response?
          The reviewer's point, the author's decision with evidence, and the exact manuscript line numbers showing the modification.
          What makes a chart or figure misleading in technical documents?
          Truncated non-zero y-axes, missing error bars, dual non-aligned axes, and cherry-picked baseline timeframes.
          Stop when
          You can write an L5 design doc with explicit non-goals, calculate Flesch Reading Ease scores, and construct a professional peer-review rebuttal matrix.
