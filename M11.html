The story

Dadi gives Nila a rope one metre long and a pair of scissors. "Cut it into three equal pieces," she says.

Nila folds, measures, cuts. Three pieces. Then she tries to write down how long one piece is. Nought point three three three — and her pencil keeps going. The threes do not stop. She can hold the piece in her hand, but she cannot finish writing it.

"Fine," says Dadi. "Now make a square whose sides are one metre, and cut a rope to fit corner to corner."

Nila lays out the square and stretches the rope across the diagonal. It fits perfectly. It is a real length; she is touching it.

Then Dadi asks the hard question. "Can you write that one as a fraction? Any fraction at all. Top number, bottom number, both whole."

Nila tries. Seven fifths is too short. Ten sevenths is too long. Ninety-nine seventieths is very close, and still wrong.

Here is the surprise: it is not that Nila is not clever enough. No such fraction exists, and people can prove it. There are lengths you can lay out with a rope and point to with your finger, which can never be written as one whole number over another. The number line is far more crowded than counting ever suggested.

Say it simply

A rational number is one you can write as a ratio of two integers. An irrational number is a real number you cannot. Both kinds sit on the same number line, and together they make up the reals.

You can tell which kind you have by looking at its decimal. Rationals either stop (0.75) or fall into a repeating block forever (0.142857142857...). Irrationals do neither — they run on with no repeating block at all.

Polynomials are the same story one level up. A polynomial is built from a variable using only addition, subtraction and multiplication by whole-number powers. They behave remarkably like integers: you can divide one by another and get a quotient and a remainder, they factorise, and their "factors" correspond exactly to their roots. That correspondence — a number a is a root exactly when (x minus a) divides the polynomial — is the engine of this module.

Say it precisely

Perfect Slices and Wild Snakes

Think of a rational number like a pizza you can share evenly. If you have three pizzas and four friends, everyone gets a neat slice. You can write it as one number over another: three on top, four on the bottom. If you write it out in numbers with a dot, it either stops perfectly or repeats a fun pattern forever.

But an irrational number is like a wild, squiggly snake! You can never share it perfectly into equal pieces, and if you try to write it out, the numbers after the dot just keep going randomly without ever stopping or making a repeating pattern. Famous wild numbers are Pi (the circle number) and the square root of two.

Even though they are wild, they have an exact place on the number line playground! You can use a compass (the drawing tool, not the map one!) to draw a perfect curve and find exactly where the square root of two sits on the line.

Number Machines (Polynomials)

A polynomial is like a magic number machine. You put a mystery number (let us call it x) into the machine. Inside, the machine is only allowed to do simple things: it can stretch the number by multiplying, and it can add or take away. It cannot do anything weird like divide by x.

  • Degree: The highest number of times x multiplies itself. It tells you how powerful the machine is.
  • Root or Zero: This is a secret password! It is the magic number you feed the machine to make it spit out exactly zero.

The Magic Factor Trick: If your magic password is five (meaning putting five in gives you zero), then the machine has a piece inside it that looks like (x minus 5). This is amazing because finding the passwords tells you exactly how the machine is built inside!

Building with Blocks

Sometimes you want to build a big square or cube out of smaller blocks. There are standard recipes for this! If you have two blocks a and b and want to build a cube of size a plus b, you do not just get an a-cube and a b-cube. You need those, PLUS extra flat slabs to fill in the sides!

A Pinch of History

Thousands of years ago, people building fire altars already knew how to find the length of the square root of two super accurately using just ropes and fractions! Later, mathematicians discovered the shocking secret that it could never be a perfect fraction. This was such a mind-blowing secret that legend says the man who discovered it was thrown off a boat!

Why it's true

Why the square root of two is wild (irrational)

Imagine we could write the square root of two as a perfect fraction, like sharing pizza. We would write it as a top number and a bottom number, chopped down to their smallest possible pieces (so they do not both share a half).

  1. If we square it (multiply it by itself), we get exactly two.
  2. That means the top number squared must be twice as big as the bottom number squared.
  3. Because it is twice as big, the top number squared must be an even number (like two, four, six). And the only way to square a number and get an even result is if the number itself was even!
  4. So the top number is even. We can hide a 'two' inside it.
  5. If we do the math, that forces the bottom number to be even too!
  6. Oh no! We said they were chopped down to their smallest pieces, but if they are both even, we could chop them in half again!
  7. This breaks the rules. Our imagination was wrong: the square root of two just cannot be written as a perfect fraction.

Why some fractions stop and some repeat forever

Think of our numbers like building blocks. The number ten is built from a two-block and a five-block.

When you divide a pizza, if the number of friends at the bottom of the fraction is built only out of two-blocks and five-blocks (like four, five, or eight), the division will eventually stop nicely.

But if a friend brings a three-block or a seven-block to the bottom of the fraction, ten does not know how to divide by those! The math gets dizzy and spins in a circle, making the numbers repeat over and over again forever.

The magic password theorem

If you have a magic number machine (polynomial) and you try to divide it by a piece like (x minus a), you get a leftover piece (the remainder).

The amazing part: that leftover piece is exactly what you would get if you just fed the secret password a into the machine! And if there is no leftover piece (remainder is zero), then (x minus a) fits perfectly inside, which means a really is a magic zero-making password! This saves us from doing long, boring division.

The disappearing blocks

There is a fun puzzle: if you have three blocks a, b, and c, and they perfectly balance out to zero on a see-saw, then if you cube them all and add them up, the answer is exactly the same as just multiplying them together and times three! It works because the balancing perfectly cancels out all the messy extra pieces.

Oracle lab

The oracle measures the period of the repeating decimal of \frac{1}{q} two completely independent ways.

The first is long division, exactly as done on paper: keep track of the remainder at each step, and watch for a remainder you have seen before. The number of steps between the two sightings is the period. This method knows nothing about number theory; it just does the division.

The second is number theory: the period of \frac{1}{q} for q coprime to 10 is the multiplicative order of 10 modulo q — the smallest k with 10^k \equiv 1 \pmod q. This method never performs a division at all.

They share no helper, and they agree only if the arithmetic and the theory are both right. The mutant forgets to reduce the fraction to lowest terms before classifying it, which is the error that makes \frac{5}{10} look like a recurring decimal.

Experiment lab
Theory makes a sharp claim about repeating decimals: for a prime p other than 2 and 5, the period of \frac{1}{p} divides p - 1. This follows from Fermat's little theorem, which says 10^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p, so the smallest exponent achieving 1 must divide p-1. The theory does not claim the period equals p-1 — only that it divides it. Primes where it does reach the maximum are called full-reptend primes, and they are the ones that produce cyclic numbers.


We measure both claims by brute-force long division over every prime below 500.

Where reality disagrees: nothing here is statistical, so the agreement is exact rather than approximate — a nice contrast with the sampling experiments elsewhere in this track. The interesting disagreement is with the naive theory. It is tempting to generalise from \frac{1}{7}, whose period is the maximum 6, to "the period is always p-1". One look at \frac{1}{11} = 0.\overline{09}, with period 2, kills it. Whether a given prime is full-reptend is governed by whether 10 is a primitive root modulo p, and no simple formula predicts it; Artin's conjecture on the density of such primes remains open.

    # x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6 divided by x - 4
    dividend, divisor = [-6, 11, -6, 1], [-4, 1]
    q, r = divide(dividend, divisor)

            Misconceptions
              

            • "A decimal that goes on forever is irrational." Only if it never settles into a repeating block. \frac{1}{3} = 0.333\dots runs forever and is perfectly rational. Irrationality requires non-repeating and non-terminating.
              • 

            • "Whether a fraction terminates depends on the denominator you were handed." It depends on the denominator in lowest terms. \frac{5}{10} looks like a tenth and is really a half.
              • 

            • "\pi = \frac{22}{7}." That is an approximation, accurate to about two decimal places. \pi is irrational, and in fact transcendental, so no fraction equals it.
              • 

            • "The remainder theorem needs you to do the division." The entire point is that it does not. Evaluating p(a) gives the remainder directly, which is why synthetic division and Horner's method are so cheap.
              • 

            • "(a+b)^3 = a^3 + b^3." The cross terms 3a^2b + 3ab^2 are not optional. The equality holds only when ab(a+b) = 0.
              • 

            • "Cancelling a factor from a rational expression changes nothing." It changes the domain. \frac{x^2-1}{x-1} simplifies to x+1 everywhere except x=1, where the original is undefined and the simplified form is not.
              • 

            • "Between two rationals there must be some irrational gap you can land in." Both sets are dense: between any two reals, however close, lie infinitely many rationals and infinitely many irrationals.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            The neat correspondence between roots and linear factors is a statement about the number system you are working in. x^2 + 1 has no linear factors over the reals but splits completely over the complex numbers; x^2 - 2 has no factors over the rationals but does over the reals. "Irreducible" is never an absolute property — always ask, over what?
            

            The rational-root search in exercise 2 only finds roots that are integers dividing the constant term. It will miss \frac{1}{2}, and it will miss every irrational root. The full rational-root theorem needs divisors of the leading coefficient in the denominator, and beyond degree 4 there is no formula in radicals at all — that is Abel and Galois, and it is a theorem about impossibility, not a gap waiting to be filled.
            

            The polynomial division in exercise 3 uses floating-point arithmetic, so it inherits every floating-point hazard: dividing by a tiny leading coefficient amplifies error, and a remainder that should be exactly zero may come out as 10^{-16}. That is why the solution compares against a tolerance rather than to zero. For exact work, use fractions.Fraction instead, as the identities demo does.
            

            Finally, "irrational" is not the end of the hierarchy. \sqrt{2} is irrational but algebraic — it solves x^2 - 2 = 0. \pi and e solve no polynomial equation with integer coefficients at all, which is what transcendental means, and that is the fact that finally settled squaring the circle.
            In your real work
            Horner's method from exercise 2 is the standard way to evaluate a polynomial in any numerical library, because it uses n multiplications instead of n^2 and is better conditioned. The terminating-decimal theorem is the reason money is never stored in binary floating point: one tenth is not a sum of powers of two, so 0.1 is unrepresentable, and repeated addition drifts. That is why currency uses integer paise or a decimal type. Recognising this in a design review, and knowing the number-theoretic reason rather than just the folklore, is exactly the kind of grounded answer an L5 interview is probing for.
            Flash cards
            State precisely when a fraction has a terminating decimal expansion.
            When, reduced to lowest terms, its denominator has no prime factors other than 2 and 5.
            Why is a repeating decimal always rational?
            Because the repeating block lets you write it as a finite geometric series, whose sum is a ratio of integers.
            What is the remainder when p(x) is divided by (x - a), and why does that matter?
            It is p(a). It matters because it turns a division into a single evaluation.
            Give the factor theorem in both directions.
            (x - a) divides p(x) if and only if p(a) = 0. A root gives a factor, and a factor gives a root.
            For a quadratic ax^2 + bx + c with roots alpha and beta, what are their sum and product?
            The sum is -b/a and the product is c/a.
            Sketch the proof that the square root of 2 is irrational.
            Assume it is p/q in lowest terms, square to get p^2 = 2q^2, deduce p is even, substitute to deduce q is even, and contradict lowest terms.
            What is the period of the decimal expansion of 1/p for a prime p other than 2 or 5, and what does it divide?
            It is the smallest k with 10^k congruent to 1 modulo p, and it always divides p - 1.
            Why does cancelling (x-1) from (x^2-1)/(x-1) need a caveat?
            Because the original expression is undefined at x = 1 while x + 1 is defined there, so the simplification holds only for x not equal to 1.
            How do you rationalise 1/(sqrt(a) + sqrt(b))?
            Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate sqrt(a) - sqrt(b), which makes the denominator the integer a - b.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why some lengths can be pointed at but never written as a fraction, you can prove the irrationality of root 2 and the terminating-decimal theorem from scratch, you can state and use the remainder and factor theorems without performing a division, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can say why 0.1 is not exactly representable in binary floating point.
