The story

Ishita draws a circle on a sheet of rubber and then pulls the sheet — harder sideways than upwards. The circle becomes an ellipse.

She draws arrows from the centre out to the circle, all round, and watches what the stretch does to each one. Most arrows do two things at once: they get longer and they swing round to point somewhere new.

But not all of them. She finds one arrow — pointing along the direction she pulled hardest — that gets longer and does not turn at all. And another, at right angles to it, that also does not turn. Only those two.

Her teacher asks what is special about those directions. Ishita says they are the ones the stretching "agrees with". Pull the sheet along an arrow that already points the way you are pulling, and there is nothing to swing.

Then the teacher asks the question that turns this into a subject. "If I tell you only those two arrows and how much each one stretched — four numbers — could you work out what the stretch does to any arrow at all?"

Ishita thinks, and realises she could: break any arrow into its components along the two special directions, stretch each component by its own factor, add them back.

Here is the surprise: a transformation that seemed to need a whole table of numbers is completely described by a handful of special directions and one number each. Finding those directions is called the eigenproblem, and it is the single most useful thing in applied linear algebra.

Say it simply

An eigenvector of a matrix is a direction the matrix does not turn — it only scales it, by a factor called the eigenvalue. Ishita's two arrows.

If a matrix has enough independent eigenvectors, you can diagonalise it: in the basis of its own eigenvectors it becomes a diagonal matrix, and acting with it is just scaling each coordinate. Powers, exponentials and long-run behaviour all become easy.

Symmetric matrices are the well-behaved case, and they are the ones that arise in practice (covariances, Gram matrices, Hessians). The spectral theorem says they always have a full set of orthogonal eigenvectors with real eigenvalues — no exceptions.

The singular value decomposition extends this to every matrix, square or not: any matrix is a rotation, then a scaling along perpendicular axes, then another rotation. From it come least squares, PCA, best low-rank approximation and the condition number — the number that tells you how much a problem will amplify your errors.

Say it precisely

Determinant as signed volume

If a matrix is a machine that stretches rubber sheets, the determinant just tells you how much the sheet grew. If the determinant is 3, a 1-inch box became a 3-inch box! If it's a negative number, it means the sheet was flipped completely upside down like a pancake. If it's 0, it means the sheet was squashed totally flat into a line.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Imagine drawing lots of arrows on a rubber sheet before you stretch it. Most arrows get pulled sideways and end up pointing a completely new way. But a few special arrows don't turn at all; they just grow longer or shrink shorter while pointing exactly where they started. These special, un-turning arrows are eigenvectors. The number that tells you how much they grew (like "twice as long" or "half as long") is their eigenvalue.

Diagonalisation

If you can find enough of these special un-turning arrows to build a complete coordinate system, you can cast a spell called diagonalisation. When you look at the stretching machine through the glasses of its own eigenvectors, it suddenly looks like the simplest machine in the world: it just multiplies each direction by its eigenvalue. No messy mixing, just simple stretching! This makes doing the same stretch a million times as easy as multiplying a single number.

Orthogonality and the spectral theorem

Orthogonal just means perfectly at right angles, like the corners of a square. Orthonormal means they are at right angles AND exactly length 1 (a perfect measuring stick).

The spectral theorem is a beautiful guarantee: if a matrix is symmetric (it looks like a perfect reflection of itself across its diagonal), it is incredibly well-behaved. It ALWAYS has a full set of special un-turning arrows, and those arrows are ALWAYS perfectly at right angles to each other. No exceptions!

Gram-Schmidt and QR

If you have a messy pile of sticks and you want to turn them into a perfect set of right-angled measuring sticks (orthonormal), you use a trick called Gram-Schmidt. You take the first stick and make it length 1. Then you take the second stick, snap off whatever part points in the same direction as the first stick, and keep only the new direction. You keep doing this, snapping off any old directions, until you have a perfect set of independent, right-angled sticks!

Projection and least squares

Imagine shining a flashlight straight down on a messy cloud of dots to cast a shadow onto a flat wall. The shadow is the orthogonal projection. When we have a math puzzle with no perfect answer, we use this shadow trick (least squares). The shadow is the absolute closest you can get to the real dots while staying on the wall. The math equation that drops the shadow perfectly straight down is called the normal equation.

The SVD

The Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is the ultimate x-ray machine for matrices. It proves that EVERY single stretching machine, no matter how weird or rectangular, is actually just doing three simple steps in a row: 1. Rotate the rubber sheet. 2. Stretch it perfectly straight along its new axes (these stretch amounts are the singular values). 3. Rotate it again. That's it! Every matrix is just Rotate-Stretch-Rotate.

Because we know exactly how much it stretches, we can easily tell how many dimensions it's actually using (rank), and we can compress pictures and data (PCA) by throwing away the dimensions that barely stretched at all.

Power iteration and condition number

If you stretch a rubber sheet over and over again, the direction that stretches the fastest (the biggest eigenvalue) will eventually take over and swallow all the other directions. Power iteration just watches the sheet stretch until it finds this winner.

The condition number is a danger score. It is the biggest stretch divided by the smallest stretch. If a machine stretches one way by a million, and another way by a tiny fraction, its condition number is huge! This means if you make a tiny typo putting numbers into the machine, the machine will amplify your typo by a million and give you completely garbage answers.

A pinch of history

Euler and Lagrange met eigenvalues in the 1760s studying rotating bodies and planetary orbits; Cauchy proved in 1829 that a real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues. Hilbert coined Eigenwert around 1904, and the hybrid German-English eigenvalue stuck.

The SVD was discovered repeatedly: Beltrami (1873), Jordan (1874), Sylvester (1889), and in the infinite-dimensional setting Schmidt (1907) and Weyl (1912). The best-low-rank result is Schmidt's, rediscovered by Eckart and Young in 1936 and extended by Mirsky in 1960. A practical algorithm had to wait for Golub and Kahan (1965).

PCA is Karl Pearson's (1901), independently Harold Hotelling's (1933). The condition number is Turing's, from the same 1948 paper that gave the LU factorisation.

Why it's true

Result 1: eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are independent. Why can't a special un-turning arrow be built by adding two other special arrows together? Imagine a red arrow that doubles in size, and a blue arrow that triples in size. If you glued them together to make a purple arrow, what happens when you stretch? The blue part outruns the red part! The purple arrow doesn't just stretch; it completely bends towards the blue side. Because it bends, it's not an un-turning arrow! Only arrows that stretch by the exact same speed can be glued together without bending.

Result 2: a symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. Why are symmetric matrices so perfectly behaved? Imagine a perfectly balanced seesaw. Because the matrix is perfectly mirrored (symmetric), whatever it does to the left side, it does equally to the right side. This perfect mirror-image rule mathematically forces its special arrows to never point into imaginary-number land (they stay real), and forces them to sit exactly at 90-degree right angles to each other.

Result 3: the normal equations. Why is dropping a shadow straight down the best way to get close to the dots? Imagine holding a ball above the floor on a string. The shortest distance from the ball to the floor is a perfectly straight, vertical drop (at a right angle to the floor). If you drop it at an angle, the string has to be longer! The "normal equation" is just math's way of cutting the string perfectly straight down.

Result 4: why the SVD exists. How do we know EVERY matrix is just Rotate-Stretch-Rotate? Because if you take any weird machine and run it forwards then backwards (the matrix times its backwards self), it turns into a perfectly balanced symmetric matrix! And we already know symmetric matrices have perfect right-angled un-turning arrows. By finding the special arrows of this forwards-and-backwards machine, we unlock the secret stretching axes of the original weird machine.

Result 5: the condition number bounds error amplification. Why does a big condition number mean your errors explode? Imagine a machine that multiplies your height by 1000, but shrinks your width by 1000. If you accidentally write down your width as 1 millimeter too big, the machine will multiply that tiny 1mm mistake by 1000! Your tiny typo becomes a gigantic 1-meter mistake in the final answer. The condition number is exactly this ratio: the biggest stretch divided by the smallest shrink, warning you how badly your typos will blow up.

Oracle lab

The oracle computes eigenvalues and singular values two genuinely independent ways.

For a symmetric matrix: the Jacobi rotation method iteratively zeroes off-diagonal entries with plane rotations, while the characteristic polynomial route (for 2 by 2) solves the characteristic equation directly. One is iterative and geometric, the other algebraic.

For a general matrix: singular values from the eigenvalues of the matrix times its backwards self against the Frobenius identity the sum of squared singular values equals the Frobenius norm squared and the product identity the product of singular values equals the determinant's absolute value.

def test_gram_schmidt_produces_an_orthonormal_basis():
    import random
    rng = random.Random(1907)
    for _ in range(200):
        n = rng.randint(2, 4)
        vecs = [[rng.uniform(-4, 4) for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(n)]
        out = gram_schmidt(vecs)
        assert is_orthonormal(out, tol=1e-7) is True
        # the span is preserved when the inputs were independent
        if len(out) == n:
            for v in vecs:
                # v must be reconstructible from the orthonormal basis
                recon = [0.0] * n
                for q in out:
                    c = dot(v, q)
                    recon = [a + c * b for a, b in zip(recon, q)]
                assert all(abs(a - b) < 1e-6 for a, b in zip(recon, v))
    # a dependent vector is dropped
    out = gram_schmidt([[1.0, 0.0], [2.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]])
    assert len(out) == 2
    assert is_orthonormal(out) is True


def test_qr_reconstructs_the_matrix():
    import random
    rng = random.Random(1965)
    checked = 0
    for _ in range(150):
        rows, cols = rng.randint(2, 5), rng.randint(2, 3)
        if cols > rows:
            continue
        a = [[rng.uniform(-4, 4) for _ in range(cols)] for _ in range(rows)]
        try:
            q, r = qr(a)
        except ValueError:
            continue
        # Q has orthonormal columns
        qcols = [[q[i][c] for i in range(rows)] for c in range(cols)]
        assert is_orthonormal(qcols, tol=1e-7) is True
        # R is upper triangular
        for i in range(cols):
            for j in range(i):
                assert abs(r[i][j]) < 1e-9
        # Q R reproduces A
        for i in range(rows):
            for j in range(cols):
                v = sum(q[i][k] * r[k][j] for k in range(cols))
                assert abs(v - a[i][j]) < 1e-7
        checked += 1
    assert checked > 50
    try:
        qr([[1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 4.0]])
        assert False, "dependent columns must raise ValueError"
    except ValueError:
        pass

def test_power_iteration_on_symmetric_matrices():
    import random
    from math import sqrt
    rng = random.Random(1933)
    checked = 0
    for _ in range(200):
        x, y, z = (rng.uniform(-5, 5) for _ in range(3))
        a = [[x, y], [y, z]]
        tr, det = x + z, x * z - y * y
        disc = tr * tr - 4 * det
        vals = sorted([(tr - sqrt(disc)) / 2, (tr + sqrt(disc)) / 2], key=abs, reverse=True)
        if not dominant_exists(vals, tol=1e-3):
            continue
        # Convergence is governed by |lambda2 / lambda1|. When that ratio is
        # close to 1 the iteration crawls, so skip those rather than loosen
        # the accuracy demanded of the ones we do test.
        if abs(vals[1] / vals[0]) > 0.8:
            continue
        lam, v = power_iteration(a, iterations=2000)
        assert abs(lam - vals[0]) < 1e-5, (vals, lam)
        # it really is an eigenpair
        av = mat_vec(a, v)
        for i in range(2):
            assert abs(av[i] - lam * v[i]) < 1e-5
        checked += 1
    assert checked > 100

def test_eckart_young_optimality():
    import random
    rng = random.Random(1936)
    a = [[rng.uniform(-4, 4) for _ in range(4)] for _ in range(5)]
    sig = singular_values(a)
    for k in (1, 2, 3):
        err = approx_error(a, k)
        tail = sqrt(sum(s * s for s in sig[k:]))
        assert abs(err - tail) < 1e-6, (k, err, tail)
        # no random rank-k rival beats it
        for _ in range(300):
            left = [[rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(k)] for _ in range(5)]
            right = [[rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(4)] for _ in range(k)]
            cand = [[sum(left[i][t] * right[t][j] for t in range(k)) for j in range(4)]
                    for i in range(5)]
            num = sum(a[i][j] * cand[i][j] for i in range(5) for j in range(4))
            den = sum(cand[i][j] ** 2 for i in range(5) for j in range(4))
            if den < 1e-18:
                continue
            f = num / den
            diff = [[a[i][j] - f * cand[i][j] for j in range(4)] for i in range(5)]
            assert frobenius(diff) >= err - 1e-9
    # k = 0 gives the zero matrix, so the error is the whole norm
    assert abs(approx_error(a, 0) - frobenius(a)) < 1e-6


def test_condition_number_and_numerical_rank():
    from math import sqrt
    # an orthogonal matrix is perfectly conditioned
    c, s = 0.6, 0.8
    rot = [[c, -s], [s, c]]
    assert abs(condition_number(rot) - 1.0) < 1e-6
    # a deliberately ill-conditioned matrix
    ill = [[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1e-8]]
    assert condition_number(ill) > 1e7
    # a singular matrix has infinite condition number
    assert condition_number([[1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 4.0]]) == float("inf")

            Misconceptions
              

            • "Every matrix can be diagonalised." Defective matrices cannot. \begin{pmatrix}1&1\0&1\end{pmatrix} has one eigenvalue of multiplicity 2 and only one eigenvector direction. The SVD, by contrast, always exists.
              • 

            • "Eigenvalues are always real." Only guaranteed for symmetric (more generally Hermitian) matrices. A rotation has complex eigenvalues, which is exactly how it encodes turning.
              • 

            • "Eigenvalues and singular values are the same thing." They coincide in magnitude only for symmetric positive semi-definite matrices. In general singular values are non-negative and always exist; eigenvalues may be complex or insufficient in number.
              • 

            • "A small determinant means ill-conditioned." Scaling a matrix changes the determinant by a scaling factor and leaves the condition number unchanged. The condition number is the right measure, and it is a ratio of singular values.
              • 

            • "Solve least squares by forming the matrix times its backwards self." It works, and it squares the condition number — losing twice as many digits. QR or the SVD is the right route when accuracy matters.
              • 

            • "Classical Gram-Schmidt is fine." It loses orthogonality catastrophically in floating point. Modified Gram-Schmidt is much better, Householder better still, and this is one of the clearest cases where two algebraically identical algorithms behave completely differently.
              • 

            • "Power iteration always converges." It needs a strictly dominant eigenvalue and a starting vector with a component along it. Eigenvalues plus 3 and minus 3 give no convergence at all.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Eigenvalues of a non-symmetric matrix can be wildly sensitive to perturbations. The classic example is a large Jordan block, where a change of a tiny microscopic amount in one entry moves the eigenvalues by a small amount depending on size — for a size of 16 that is a change of 0.1. Non-normal matrices have ill-conditioned eigenvalues even when the matrix itself is well conditioned, and that is why pseudospectra exist.
            

            Computing eigenvalues via the characteristic polynomial is a numerical disaster beyond tiny sizes: polynomial roots are extremely sensitive to coefficients (Wilkinson's polynomial is the standard horror), so real algorithms never form it. They use QR iteration instead.
            

            The SVD via the matrix times its backwards self — the route used here for clarity — squares the condition number, so small singular values are computed poorly. Production implementations use Golub-Kahan bidiagonalisation precisely to avoid forming the matrix times its backwards self.
            

            Numerical rank is a judgement, not a fact. Where exactly the singular values "fall off a cliff" depends on your tolerance, and for data with no clean gap the question simply has no sharp answer.
            

            Finally, PCA assumes variance is what matters and that the structure is linear. A dataset lying on a curved manifold will be badly served, and features on different scales will be dominated by whichever has the largest units — which is why standardising before PCA is not optional.
            In your real work
            The SVD is the universal tool. Recommender systems factor a ratings matrix by truncated SVD; latent semantic analysis does the same to a term-document matrix; image compression keeps the top singular values. Every one of those is Eckart-Young being applied.
            

            PCA is dimensionality reduction everywhere in machine learning, and the singular values tell you honestly how many dimensions your data really has — which is the Packet C question of M30, answered numerically.
            

            Eigenvalues decide stability: a discrete system a system stepping forward settles exactly when every eigenvalue has magnitude below 1, which is M40's convergence criterion for Markov chains, and the same test governs whether a control loop or a physics integrator is stable. Power iteration on a link matrix is PageRank.
            

            And the condition number is the number to compute before trusting any numerical answer. If the condition number is around 100 million in double precision, you have about eight correct digits left no matter how good your library is — which is why ill-conditioned regressions give unstable coefficients, and why regularisation (ridge regression adds a regularisation term, lifting the smallest singular value) is the standard fix.
            Flash cards
            What is an eigenvector, in one sentence?
            A direction the matrix does not turn, only scales, by the factor called the eigenvalue.
            When can a matrix be diagonalised?
            When it has a full set of independent eigenvectors. Distinct eigenvalues guarantee this; repeated ones may or may not.
            State the spectral theorem.
            A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, so the matrix equals a rotation, a scaling, and an inverse rotation.
            What does the SVD say geometrically?
            Every matrix is a rotation, then a scaling along perpendicular axes, then another rotation.
            What is the relation between singular values and the eigenvalues of A transpose A?
            The singular values are the square roots of those eigenvalues, and the right singular vectors are their eigenvectors.
            State the Eckart-Young theorem.
            Truncating the SVD to the largest k singular values gives the best rank-k approximation, with spectral error equal to the next singular value.
            What is the condition number and what does it tell you?
            The ratio of largest to smallest singular value. It bounds how much a relative error in the data is amplified in the answer.
            Why avoid forming A transpose A for least squares?
            Because it squares the condition number, losing twice as many digits. Use QR or the SVD instead.
            When does power iteration fail?
            When there is no strictly dominant eigenvalue, or the starting vector has no component along the dominant eigenvector.
            Why is the SVD a better rank test than counting pivots?
            Pivot counting needs an exact zero test, which is meaningless in floating point. Singular values degrade gracefully, so you can see how close to rank-deficient a matrix is.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why a stretched sheet has special directions, you can prove that a symmetric matrix has orthogonal eigenvectors and point to the step that uses symmetry, you can state the SVD and explain why every matrix has one, you can say what the condition number bounds and why it beats the determinant as a measure of near-singularity, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you would compute a condition number before trusting a numerical result.
