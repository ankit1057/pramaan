The story

In the spring of 1951, David Huffman was a doctoral student in electrical engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. His professor, information theory pioneer Robert M. Fano, assigned the class a term project: either take a difficult final exam or find the most efficient mathematical binary code for transmitting letters with unequal frequencies. Huffman spent months filling reams of scrap paper with mathematical proofs, trying and failing to construct codes from the top down.

Just as he was about to give up and study for the final exam, Huffman was hit with a flash of insight: instead of attempting to divide all symbols into two halves from the top down (Shannon-Fano coding), he should build the tree from the bottom up! He took the two symbols with the lowest probabilities, combined them into a single composite symbol, and repeated the process until only one root remained. Huffman's greedy bottom-up algorithm proved to be mathematically optimal, yielding shorter average bit lengths than any known compression technique. Today, Huffman coding remains embedded inside JPEG images, MP3 audio, and DEFLATE compression (ZIP and GZIP).

Say it simply

A greedy algorithm makes the choice that looks best at the immediate moment, without worrying about future consequences. Sometimes being greedy works like magic: if you want to attend as many events as possible in a day, always choosing the event that finishes earliest leaves the most time open for future events. However, greedy choices can fail catastrophically: if you want to make change for 6 cents using coins of 1, 3, and 4 cents, taking the largest coin (4) forces you to use three coins (4 + 1 + 1), whereas two 3-cent coins (3 + 3) is the true optimal answer!

Say it precisely

A problem is good for a greedy algorithm if making the best immediate choice right now never messes up your choices later. We call this the "greedy-choice property". You don't ever need to undo your work or change your mind!

How do we prove a greedy trick actually gives the perfect answer? We use an imagination game called the Exchange Argument: 1. Imagine someone gives you the perfect, magic answer. 2. You look at their first choice. Is it the same as your greedy choice? 3. If it's different, you prove that swapping their choice for your greedy choice keeps the answer just as good, or even makes it better! 4. Since you can swap all their choices for yours without ruining the perfect score, your greedy way must be just as perfect.

Why it's true

Let's play the Exchange Argument game with Scheduling. You want to go to as many fun rides at the theme park as possible. The greedy trick is: always pick the ride that finishes earliest!

Let's say the Magic Perfect Schedule chose a different first ride than you. The ride they chose must end later than yours, because your greedy trick literally picked the earliest ending ride possible. If you swap their ride for yours, you actually get out of the ride earlier! That means you have even MORE free time to go to the rest of the rides on their schedule. Swapping to your greedy choice only made things better, never worse.

Since this works for the first ride, it works for the second, the third, and so on. You never run into a trap by picking the earliest finishing ride, meaning the greedy trick gives you the maximum number of rides possible!

But when does greedy fail? Let's look at the Backpack Problem. You have a small backpack that holds 5 pounds. You see a giant gold bar weighing 4 pounds worth 400 dollars, and three smaller gold bars weighing 2 pounds each but worth 250 dollars each. A greedy trick says: "Take the most valuable bar!" So you grab the giant 4-pound bar (400 dollars). Now your backpack has 1 pound left, so you can't fit anything else. But if you weren't greedy, you could have taken two of the 2-pound bars (250 + 250 = 500 dollars)! Greedy fails here because grabbing the giant bar leaves awkward empty space in your backpack that you can't fill. (Unless you brought a saw to chop the gold bars into pieces, which is called the "Fractional Backpack Problem", where greedy actually works again!)

Oracle lab

The ground truth for greedy algorithms is verified against formal exchange invariants and dynamic programming bounds. For fractional knapsack, greedy selection by value-per-pound ratio provides an upper ceiling for any backpack packing challenge. For Huffman coding, assigning the shortest Morse-like codes to the most frequent letters matches the absolute theoretical compression limit of information theory. Whenever an algorithm makes an irrevocable choice at each moment, we test that local perfection translates reliably into global success.

By iteratively combining the two nodes with the lowest frequencies, characters with high frequency (e.g. 'e', 't') receive short 1-to-2 bit codes, while rare characters receive longer codes. We measure and assert that no code word is a prefix of any other code word, guaranteeing unambiguous instant decoding over arbitrary transmitted bitstreams.

    assert len(codes) == 6

    assert len(codes["a"]) <= len(codes["f"])

    # Prefix-free check
    for c1, s1 in codes.items():
        for c2, s2 in codes.items():
            if c1 != c2:
                assert not s2.startswith(s1)

          Misconceptions
            

          • "A greedy algorithm always finds the optimal solution." Greedy algorithms only work when the problem possesses optimal substructure and the greedy-choice property. In problems like 0/1 Knapsack, TSP, or general Coin Change, greedy produces suboptimal answers.
            • 

          • "You can prove a greedy algorithm works by trying a few test cases." Empirical test cases can easily miss pathological counterexamples. Proving greedy correctness requires a formal mathematical induction or exchange argument.
            • 

          • "Huffman codes use fixed-length bit sequences." Huffman codes are variable-length prefix-free codes: frequent characters receive short bit strings (1 or 2 bits) while rare characters receive longer bit strings.
            • 

          • "Dijkstra's algorithm is dynamic programming." Dijkstra is fundamentally a greedy algorithm: it permanently settles the vertex with minimal tentative distance at each step without ever revising settled vertices.
            • 

          Where it breaks
          Greedy algorithms fail catastrophically on non-matroid NP-hard problems like the Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP) or Vertex Cover. A greedy TSP heuristic (visiting the nearest unvisited city) often gets trapped into taking an extraordinarily expensive final edge to return home, producing tours significantly worse than the optimal route. Because greedy logic never looks ahead to consequences down the road, it can easily grab a tiny reward now only to pay a painful penalty later.
          In your real work
          Data compression engines (ZIP, GZIP, zlib) use Huffman coding in their DEFLATE pipeline. Operating system CPU scheduling (Shortest Job First / Earliest Deadline First) uses greedy heuristics. Video streaming codecs (H.264, HEVC) use greedy rate-distortion optimization (RDO) to allocate quantization bits across macroblocks.
          Flash cards
          What is the Greedy-Choice Property?
          The property that a globally optimal solution can be arrived at by making locally optimal (greedy) choices without ever backtracking.
          What are the core steps of an Exchange Argument proof?
          Assume a perfect master solution differs from the greedy choice, swap the differing choice with the greedy pick, and show that the new plan is just as good or better.
          Why does the greedy choice fail on the 0/1 Knapsack problem?
          Because items cannot be fractionally divided to fill remaining capacity, leaving empty slack that lowers total value.
          What does it mean for a code to be prefix-free in Huffman coding?
          No valid character code word is a prefix of any other code word, enabling unambiguous instant decoding without delimiter symbols.
          In Interval Scheduling, what attribute should you sort by to maximize activities?
          Earliest finish time (end time), because finishing early leaves maximal remaining time open for subsequent activities.
          What algebraic structure guarantees that a greedy algorithm produces an optimal solution?
          A Matroid, which satisfies hereditary independent set axioms and the independent set exchange property.
          Stop when
          You can construct an exchange argument proof, build a Huffman prefix-free tree from scratch, and solve the circular gas station circuit in O(N) time.
