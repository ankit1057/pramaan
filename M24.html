The story

Hema's grandfather owns a field beside the river. The three straight sides he can measure with a rope. The fourth side is the river, and the river wanders.

The patwari wants an area. Grandfather shrugs: it cannot be done, the edge is not a shape.

Hema disagrees. She walks the straight edge driving sticks in every ten paces, and at each stick she walks out to the river and measures. Now she has a row of widths. She treats the field as a row of rectangles, ten paces wide, each as tall as its stick's measurement. She adds them up.

"Too big," says grandfather, pointing to a place where the river bulges in and her rectangle sticks out over the water.

So she does it again with sticks every five paces. The rectangles are narrower and they hug the bank more closely. The total comes down a bit. Then two paces. The total comes down again, but less. Then one pace, and it barely moves at all.

The numbers are settling.

Here is the surprise: the field has a definite area even though its edge is a river, and she has found it without ever knowing the shape. She did not measure the area. She measured a trend — the limit of a sum of things she could measure — exactly as Priya found speed from a shrinking window in M22.

And then grandfather, who was a surveyor, tells her the part that still astonishes people. There is a shortcut. If you know a function whose rate of change is the width of the field, you never have to add up a single rectangle.

Say it simply

Integration answers two questions that look unrelated and turn out to be the same one.

The area question. What is the area under a curve? Slice it into thin rectangles, add them up, and take the limit as the slices shrink. That is the definite integral, written the integral of f from a to b.

The reversal question. Given a rate, what was the quantity? That is the antiderivative or indefinite integral: find F with F prime is f.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says these are the same operation. To find the area, find an antiderivative and subtract its values at the ends:

the area is F of b minus F of a

That is the shortcut Hema's grandfather knew. An infinite summing process collapses into one subtraction — provided you can find F, which is the hard part and the reason most of this module is a toolbox of techniques.

Say it precisely

Antiderivatives

An antiderivative is just playing a movie backwards. If a movie shows the speedometer of a car, the antiderivative tells you how far the car has traveled. When we find an antiderivative F of x, we always add "plus C" at the end. Why? Because knowing the car's speed tells you how far it drove today, but it doesn't tell you what the odometer said when it started! The plus C is the starting amount.

Techniques

Sometimes figuring out the backwards movie is hard, so we have tricks:

  • Substitution (Changing Currency): If a puzzle is too hard in dollars (x), we switch it to rupees (u). But you must remember to change the exchange rate (dx to du) AND the boundaries of the map (the limits of the integral).
  • Integration by Parts (Trading): If you are stuck with two things multiplied together, this trick lets you swap one piece of the puzzle for a hopefully simpler piece. It's like un-doing the Product Rule of derivatives.
  • Partial Fractions (Un-baking a Cake): If you have a horrible, complicated fraction, this trick breaks it apart into two or three simple, bite-sized fractions that are easy to swallow.

The definite integral

If you want the exact area of an odd shape, slice it into thin vertical strips. Pretend each strip is a perfect rectangle. Add them all up. The thinner the slices, the closer you get. If you make the slices infinitely thin, you get the definite integral.

Rules of the slices: - If you integrate backwards (from b to a), the area turns negative. - If you stay in one spot (from a to a), the area is zero. - You can chop a big area into two smaller areas and add them together.

The Fundamental Theorem

This is the biggest secret in all of calculus.

Part 1: If you are painting a fence, the rate at which your total painted area grows is exactly equal to the height of the fence right where your brush is. (The derivative of the area is the function itself!) Part 2 (The Shortcut): If you want to know how much water went into a bucket between 1:00 and 5:00, you could measure every single drop (the infinite slices). OR, you could just read the water meter at 5:00, read it at 1:00, and subtract them! the area is F of b minus F of a

Area

The integral naturally measures signed area. If the graph dips below the ground (the x-axis), it counts as "negative dirt" being dug out. If you want the area between two curves, just subtract them: Top curve minus Bottom curve.

Numerical integration

Sometimes the shortcut formula is impossible to find. We have to go back to measuring slices, but we want to be smart about it: - Trapezoid Rule: Instead of flat rectangles, connect the tops with straight tilted lines. It's pretty good! - Simpson's Rule: Connect the tops with curved lines (parabolas). It's incredibly accurate because most things in nature are curved, not straight.

A pinch of history

Over 2000 years ago, Archimedes figured out how to find areas by packing shapes inside other shapes. Much later, brilliant Indian mathematicians like Madhava found ways to add up infinite slices of curves. Finally, in the 1600s, Newton and Leibniz realized that finding the tilt of a curve (derivatives) and finding the area under a curve (integrals) were exactly the same coin, just flipped upside down. It changed the world forever.

Why it's true

Result 1: The Speed of Area (FTC Part 1)

Imagine a curtain opening across a stage. How fast is the visible area of the stage growing? If the curtain opens by a tiny width h, the new area revealed is a tall, thin sliver. The area of that sliver is roughly its height times h. So, the Area divided by h is exactly the height! As the sliver gets infinitely thin, the "speed of the area" becomes exactly the height of the curtain right there.

Result 2: The Shortcut (FTC Part 2)

If you have two different speedometers that always show the exact same speed, the two cars must be driving right next to each other (or one is just a constant distance C ahead of the other). Since the "slice-by-slice area" and the "antiderivative" both have the exact same speed (the height of the function), they must be the exact same thing, just shifted by C. So evaluating F of b minus F of a perfectly cancels out the C and gives you the exact area.

Result 3: Integration by Parts (The Growing Carpet)

Remember the Growing Carpet from the Product Rule? New Area = (Length times new width) + (Width times new length). Integration by parts is just rearranging this! (Width times new length) = New Area - (Length times new width). It lets you swap a hard piece of carpet for an easier piece.

Result 4: Why Simpson is Super Accurate

The Trapezoid rule uses straight sticks to measure a curvy hill. It misses the bulges. Simpson's rule uses flexible, curvy sticks (parabolas) to measure the hill. If the hill is shaped like a parabola, or even a cubic curve, Simpson's flexible stick perfectly matches the shape of the hill, making the error exactly zero! For anything else, it still hugs the curve much tighter than a stiff, straight stick.

Oracle lab

The oracle computes definite integrals two genuinely independent ways.

The analytic route uses the Fundamental Theorem: evaluate a known antiderivative at the two endpoints and subtract. It never adds up a rectangle.

The numerical route uses a fine composite Simpson rule: it only ever evaluates f at points and combines the values with fixed weights. It has never heard of an antiderivative.

If the Fundamental Theorem were false, or an antiderivative wrong, these would disagree. The demo also checks the symmetry properties and the additivity of the integral. The mutant below evaluates the antiderivative as F(a)-F(b), reversing the limits — a sign error that is invisible until you compare against something real.

Theory predicts the order of each numerical rule: the trapezoid error falls like h^2 and Simpson's like h to the fourth. So halving the step should quarter the trapezoid error and divide Simpson's by sixteen.

Where reality disagrees: the Simpson ratios stop being 16 at the finest step shown, which is why the assertion above only checks the first four. The reason is the same trade-off as M22's derivative: once the truncation error drops near the rounding error of the summation itself, further refinement buys nothing and the measured ratio becomes noise.             Misconceptions
              

            • "The constant of integration is a formality." It is the whole truth that antiderivatives are a family, not a function. It disappears only in a definite integral, where it cancels in the subtraction.
              • 

            • "the integral of one over x is log x." It is log of absolute x. Dropping the absolute value makes the formula wrong for every negative x, where one over x is perfectly well defined.
              • 

            • "The integral is the area." It is the signed area. the integral of sine from 0 to two pi is zero although the curve encloses an area of 4. To get enclosed area you must integrate absolute f or split at the zeros.
              • 

            • "In a substitution you can leave the limits alone." The limits belong to the old variable. Substituting u is g of x requires integrating from g of a to g of b; keeping a and b silently computes a different integral.
              • 

            • "Simpson's rule is fourth order, so I can check that on any function." Not on a polynomial of degree 3 or less, where it is exact and its error is identically zero. The experiment showed this explicitly.
              • 

            • "Every elementary function has an elementary antiderivative." Most do not. e to the minus x squared, sine x over x and one over log x have no antiderivative expressible in elementary terms — a theorem of Liouville, not a failure of ingenuity.
              • 

            • "A finer grid always gives a better numerical integral." Only until rounding error in the summation dominates, exactly as with numerical differentiation.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            The Fundamental Theorem as stated needs f continuous. For functions with jumps you must split at the discontinuities, and for wilder functions the Riemann integral itself gives up — the indicator of the rationals has lower sum 0 and upper sum 1 at every partition, so it is not Riemann integrable at all. That failure is what motivated Lebesgue's theory, where that function integrates perfectly well to 0.
            

            Improper integrals — infinite limits, or an integrand that blows up inside the range — are defined by a further limit and may diverge. the integral of one over x to infinity diverges while the integral of one over x squared to infinity converges, and the exercise's pole check exists because integrating across one over x minus 1 at x is 1 is meaningless without care.
            

            Symbolically, integration is genuinely harder than differentiation. Differentiation is a mechanical algorithm that always succeeds; integration is a search, and Liouville's theorem says the search often has no answer in elementary terms. This asymmetry is why automatic differentiation is easy and automatic integration is not.
            

            Numerically, the error formulas assume the relevant derivative is bounded. For a function with a kink, a spike or an endpoint singularity, Simpson's h to the fourth promise evaporates and uniform grids perform badly; adaptive quadrature, which refines only where the function is difficult, is the practical answer.
            In your real work
            Integration is accumulation, and accumulation is everywhere. Total bytes transferred is the integral of bandwidth; total energy drawn is the integral of power, which is exactly what a battery-usage report computes from sampled current; distance is the integral of velocity, which is how a step counter or dead-reckoning routine works from accelerometer samples.
            

            The numerical rules here are the actual implementation: sensor data arrives as samples, not formulas, so you are always doing a Riemann sum or a trapezoid rule whether you call it that or not. Knowing that the trapezoid rule is second order tells you how sampling rate trades against accuracy — halving the sample interval quarters the error — which is a real battery-versus-precision decision on a device.
            

            Smoothing a noisy signal with a moving average is a sliding integral. The area-between-curves computation is how you quantify the gap between two latency distributions or two cumulative curves. And the fact that most functions have no closed-form antiderivative is why numerical quadrature is not a fallback but the normal case.
            Flash cards
            State both parts of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
            Part 1: the derivative of the integral from a to x of f is f(x). Part 2: the integral from a to b equals F(b) - F(a) for any antiderivative F.
            Why does the power rule for integration need a special case at n = -1?
            Because the rule divides by n+1, which is zero there. The logarithm fills that gap.
            What happens to the limits when you substitute u = g(x) in a definite integral?
            They become g(a) and g(b). Leaving them unchanged computes a different integral.
            Where does integration by parts come from?
            Integrating the product rule and rearranging. It is the product rule read backwards.
            Does the integral give you the area under a curve?
            It gives the signed area. For enclosed area you must integrate the absolute value, or split at the zeros.
            What are the error orders of the trapezoid and Simpson rules?
            Order h^2 for the trapezoid, order h^4 for Simpson, whose error involves the fourth derivative.
            Why can you not measure Simpson's error order on a cubic?
            Because Simpson is exact for every polynomial of degree at most 3, so the error is identically zero and reveals nothing.
            Give three elementary functions with no elementary antiderivative.
            e^(-x^2), sin(x)/x, and 1/log(x). This is Liouville's theorem, not a lack of cleverness.
            Why does the Fundamental Theorem's proof need the Mean Value Theorem?
            To conclude that two antiderivatives differ by a constant, since a function with zero derivative on an interval must be constant.
            What is the practical meaning of integrating a sampled signal?
            Accumulation: total energy from power samples, distance from velocity samples, bytes from bandwidth samples. You are computing a Riemann sum.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child how to find the area of a field with a river for one edge, you can state and sketch the proof of both parts of the Fundamental Theorem and say where continuity and the MVT are used, you can carry out substitution and by parts on a definite integral without mishandling the limits, you can say why Simpson's order cannot be measured on a cubic, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you can explain why numerical quadrature is the normal case rather than a fallback.
