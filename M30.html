The story

Ravi's shop sells spice blends, but only two of them. Packet A is two spoons of chilli and one of turmeric. Packet B is one spoon of chilli and one of turmeric.

A customer wants a blend with five spoons of chilli and three of turmeric. Ravi works it out: two of A and one of B gives five chilli and three turmeric. Done.

Another customer wants seven chilli and four turmeric. Three A and one B. Done.

Ravi starts to believe he can make anything. Then a new supplier arrives with Packet C: three spoons of chilli and two of turmeric.

He is delighted — more choice. His daughter looks at it for a moment and says Packet C is useless. One A plus one B is already three chilli and two turmeric. C is nothing new.

Ravi objects that surely three packets give more options than two. She asks him to name a blend he can make with C that he could not make before. He cannot.

Then she asks the sharper question. "Is there any blend you cannot make?" Ravi tries to find one and keeps failing — every target he picks, some combination of A and B reaches it.

Here is the surprise, and it is two surprises at once. Adding a third option added nothing, because C was already reachable. And two options turned out to be enough for everything. The number that matters is not how many packets are on the shelf — it is how many of them are genuinely independent. That count is called the dimension, and almost all of linear algebra is the study of it.

Say it simply

A vector space is a set where you can add things and scale them, and the usual rules hold. The examples that matter are $\mathbb{R}^n$, matrices, polynomials and functions.

The span of some vectors is everything you can reach by scaling and adding them. Vectors are linearly independent when none is reachable from the others — no redundancy, like Ravi's Packet C. A basis is a set that is both independent and spanning: the smallest list that still reaches everything. Every basis of a space has the same size, and that size is the dimension.

A linear map respects the structure: $T(x+y)=T(x)+T(y)$ and $T(cx)=cT(x)$. Every such map is a matrix, and every matrix is such a map.

The central theorem is rank-nullity: what a map flattens, plus what it keeps, equals what it started with. Everything else in this module — solvability, elimination, decompositions — is that accounting identity in different clothes.

Say it precisely

Vector spaces and subspaces

A vector space over $\mathbb{R}$ is a set $V$ with addition and scalar multiplication satisfying: associativity and commutativity of addition, a zero vector, additive inverses, $1v=v$, and the two distributive laws together with $a(bv)=(ab)v$.

A subspace is a subset that is itself a vector space. The test is three conditions: it contains $\mathbf{0}$, it is closed under addition, and it is closed under scalar multiplication. (Closure under both operations implies the zero vector, provided the set is non-empty.)

Span, independence, basis, dimension

The span of $v_1,\dots,v_k$ is the set of all linear combinations $\sum c_iv_i$. It is always a subspace.

The vectors are linearly independent when $\sum c_iv_i=\mathbf{0}$ forces every $c_i=0$; otherwise dependent, and then at least one is a combination of the others.

A basis is an independent spanning set. Every vector has a unique expression in a given basis. All bases of a finite-dimensional space have the same size, the dimension.

In $\mathbb{R}^n$: any $n$ independent vectors form a basis, more than $n$ vectors are always dependent, and fewer than $n$ never span.

Linear maps

$T:V\to W$ is linear when $T(ax+by)=aT(x)+bT(y)$. Fixing bases, $T$ is represented by a matrix whose $j$-th column is $T$ applied to the $j$-th basis vector.

The kernel (null space) is $\ker T=\{v: T(v)=\mathbf{0}\}$, a subspace of the domain. The image (range, column space) is $\{T(v)\}$, a subspace of the codomain. $\operatorname{rank}(T)=\dim(\operatorname{im} T)$ and $\operatorname{nullity}(T)=\dim(\ker T)$.

Rank-nullity theorem. For $T:V\to W$ with $\dim V=n$:

$$\operatorname{rank}(T)+\operatorname{nullity}(T) = n$$

$T$ is injective iff $\ker T=\{\mathbf{0}\}$ iff nullity 0; surjective iff rank $=\dim W$.

Row rank equals column rank, so "the rank" is unambiguous.

Elimination and echelon form

Row echelon form: all-zero rows at the bottom, and each leading entry (pivot) strictly to the right of the one above. Reduced row echelon form (RREF) additionally requires every pivot to be 1 and to be the only non-zero entry in its column. The RREF of a matrix is unique, and the number of pivots is the rank.

For $Ax=b$, form the augmented matrix and reduce. Pivot columns correspond to basic variables; free columns give free variables, one per dimension of the kernel. The system is inconsistent exactly when a pivot appears in the augmented column.

LU decomposition and pivoting

Gaussian elimination without row swaps factors $A=LU$ with $L$ unit lower-triangular and $U$ upper-triangular. With row swaps it becomes $PA=LU$ for a permutation $P$. Once you have it, solving $Ax=b$ is two triangular solves, at $O(n^2)$ each, against $O(n^3)$ for the factorisation — so one factorisation serves many right-hand sides.

Partial pivoting chooses the largest-magnitude candidate as the pivot at each step. This is not fussiness: without it, a small pivot produces huge multipliers, and rounding errors are amplified in proportion. The classic example is

$$\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$$

with tiny $\varepsilon$, where naive elimination loses the second equation entirely.

Why you rarely compute an inverse

Solving $Ax=b$ by LU costs about $\frac{n^3}{3}$ operations; forming $A^{-1}$ costs about $n^3$ and then the multiply costs $n^2$ more — and the result is less accurate, because the explicit inverse introduces extra rounding. For a sparse $A$ the inverse is usually dense, which can be catastrophic in memory. The rule is: solve, do not invert.

Change of basis

If $B$ is the matrix whose columns are a new basis, written in the old coordinates, then a vector's new coordinates $[v]_{\text{new}}$ satisfy $v = B[v]_{\text{new}}$, so $[v]_{\text{new}} = B^{-1}v$. A linear map with old matrix $A$ has new matrix

$$A' = B^{-1}AB$$

Matrices related this way are similar; they are the same map seen from different angles, which is why they share determinant, rank, trace and eigenvalues — the subject of M31.

A pinch of history

Elimination is ancient: the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art (by about the first century CE) solves linear systems by exactly this procedure on a counting board, and Gauss used it in 1810 for least squares on astronomical data. The name "Gaussian elimination" is, as usual, later and partly misattributed.

The abstract axioms came much later. Grassmann's Ausdehnungslehre (1844) was far ahead of its time and largely ignored; Peano gave the modern axioms in 1888; and the definition only became standard after Banach and others in the 1920s. So the computation preceded the concept by about two thousand years.

Alan Turing's 1948 paper "Rounding-off errors in matrix processes" introduced the LU factorisation in its modern form and the condition number, founding numerical linear algebra.

Why it's true

Result 1: every spanning set contains a basis, and Packet C was redundant.

  1. Suppose $v_1,\dots,v_k$ span $V$ but are dependent, so $\sum c_iv_i=0$ with some $c_j\neq0$.
  2. Solve for $v_j$: it is a combination of the others.
  3. So any vector written using $v_j$ can be rewritten without it, and the remaining $k-1$ vectors still span $V$.
  4. Repeat until the set is independent. It still spans, so it is a basis.

That is exactly Ravi's daughter's argument: $C = A + B$, so anything using $C$ can be rewritten using only $A$ and $B$.

Result 2: the rank-nullity theorem.

  1. Let $\dim V = n$ and choose a basis $u_1,\dots,u_k$ of $\ker T$, so $k=\operatorname{nullity}(T)$.
  2. Extend it to a basis $u_1,\dots,u_k,w_1,\dots,w_{n-k}$ of $V$ — always possible in finite dimensions.
  3. Claim: $T(w_1),\dots,T(w_{n-k})$ is a basis of $\operatorname{im} T$.
  4. Spanning: any $T(v)$ expands $v$ in the basis; the $u_i$ terms map to zero, leaving a combination of the $T(w_j)$.
  5. Independence: if $\sum a_jT(w_j)=0$ then $T\left(\sum a_jw_j\right)=0$, so $\sum a_jw_j\in\ker T$, so it is a combination of the $u_i$. But the $u$'s and $w$'s together are independent, forcing every $a_j=0$.
  6. Hence $\operatorname{rank}(T)=n-k$, and $\operatorname{rank}+\operatorname{nullity}=n$.

Step 5 is the heart of it, and it is where the extension in step 2 earns its keep. Intuitively: the map flattens a $k$-dimensional subspace to nothing and faithfully carries the remaining $n-k$ dimensions across. Nothing is created or destroyed, only flattened.

Result 3: why partial pivoting matters. Take

$$\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}$$

whose true solution is near $x\approx1$, $y\approx1$. Without pivoting, the multiplier is $\frac{1}{\varepsilon}$, enormous. The second equation becomes $\left(1-\frac1\varepsilon\right)y = 2-\frac1\varepsilon$. In floating point, when $\frac1\varepsilon$ dwarfs 1 and 2, both sides round to $-\frac1\varepsilon y = -\frac1\varepsilon$, giving $y=1$ — fine — but back-substitution computes $x = \frac{1-y}{\varepsilon}$, a cancellation of nearly equal numbers divided by something tiny, and the answer is garbage. Swapping the rows first makes the multiplier $\varepsilon$, tiny, and everything is stable.

The failure is not because the matrix is ill-conditioned: for $\varepsilon=10^{-20}$ this system is perfectly well conditioned, and its exact solution is insensitive to the data. The algorithm is what is unstable, and pivoting fixes the algorithm.

Result 4: solving beats inverting. $A^{-1}b$ requires forming $n$ columns of the inverse, each itself a solve, then an $n^2$ multiply: roughly three times the work of one LU solve, with more rounding steps. The identity $A^{-1}b$ is a definition, not an instruction.

Oracle lab

The oracle computes the rank two genuinely independent ways.

Elimination reduces the matrix to row echelon form and counts pivots — an iterative, numerical procedure.

Combinatorial search takes the columns and finds the largest subset that is linearly independent, testing independence by whether a non-trivial combination can be made to vanish. It never performs an elimination on the whole matrix.

If row rank did not equal column rank, or if elimination miscounted, the two would disagree. The demo also verifies rank-nullity by computing an explicit kernel basis, and checks $A=LU$ by multiplying the factors back. Experiment lab
Theory predicts that partial pivoting is not a refinement but a necessity: on the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ naive elimination should lose all accuracy as $\varepsilon\to0$, while pivoted elimination should stay exact — even though the problem itself is perfectly well conditioned. That distinction between a hard problem and a bad algorithm is measurable, so we measure it.


Theory also predicts that LU factorisation pays for itself across many right-hand sides, and that forming an explicit inverse costs more and is less accurate. We count operations and measure residuals.

Where reality disagrees: the naive solver returns $x=0$ at $\varepsilon=10^{-18}$ — not a slightly wrong answer, a completely wrong one — while the pivoted solver returns 1 to full precision on the identical problem. That is the clearest possible demonstration that stability is a property of the algorithm, not of the problem. A well-conditioned system solved by an unstable method gives garbage, and no amount of extra precision in the input helps. Note also what pivoting does not fix: if the matrix itself is nearly singular, the problem is ill-conditioned and every algorithm will struggle, because the true answer is genuinely sensitive to the data. Telling those two situations apart requires the condition number, which is M31.

def test_one_factorisation_many_solves():
    import random
    rng = random.Random(42)
    n = 5
    a = [[rng.uniform(-5, 5) for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(n)]
    l, u, perm = lu_decompose(a)
    for _ in range(40):
        x_true = [rng.uniform(-5, 5) for _ in range(n)]
        b = [sum(a[i][j] * x_true[j] for j in range(n)) for i in range(n)]
        x = lu_solve(l, u, perm, b)
        for got, want in zip(x, x_true):
            assert abs(got - want) < 1e-6
    try:
        lu_decompose([[1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 4.0]])
        assert False, "a singular matrix must raise ValueError"
    except ValueError:
        pass

    # A perfectly invertible matrix whose FIRST pivot is zero. Elimination
    # without a row swap cannot start; with partial pivoting it is trivial.
    swapper = [[0.0, 1.0], [1.0, 0.0]]
    l2, u2, perm2 = lu_decompose(swapper)
    for i in range(2):
        for j in range(2):
            lhs = sum(l2[i][k] * u2[k][j] for k in range(2))
            assert abs(lhs - swapper[perm2[i]][j]) < 1e-12
    x = lu_solve(l2, u2, perm2, [3.0, 7.0])
    assert abs(x[0] - 7.0) < 1e-12 and abs(x[1] - 3.0) < 1e-12

    # A tiny leading entry: still factorable, and the solve stays accurate.
    tiny = [[1e-18, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0]]
    l3, u3, perm3 = lu_decompose(tiny)
    x = lu_solve(l3, u3, perm3, [1.0, 2.0])
    assert abs(x[0] - 1.0) < 1e-6 and abs(x[1] - 1.0) < 1e-6


def test_pivoting_rescues_a_well_conditioned_problem():
    b = [1.0, 2.0]
    # at a moderate eps both methods are fine
    for eps in (1e-2, 1e-4):
        assert residual_2x2(eps, b, naive_elimination_2x2(eps, b)) < 1e-10
        assert residual_2x2(eps, b, pivoted_elimination_2x2(eps, b)) < 1e-12
    # at a tiny eps only pivoting survives
    eps = 1e-18
    naive = naive_elimination_2x2(eps, b)
    piv = pivoted_elimination_2x2(eps, b)
    assert abs(piv[0] - 1.0) < 1e-9, "pivoting finds x near 1"
    assert abs(naive[0] - 1.0) > 0.5, "the naive method does not"
    assert residual_2x2(eps, b, piv) < residual_2x2(eps, b, naive)
    # and the cost comparison favours solving over inverting
    for n in (5, 10, 50):
        assert inverse_cost(n) > solve_cost(n)
        assert inverse_cost(n) >= 3 * solve_cost(n)

            

            Misconceptions
              

            • "More vectors means a bigger span." Ravi's Packet C added nothing. Only independent vectors enlarge a span, and beyond the dimension no new vector can.
              • 

            • "A subspace is any subset closed under addition." It must also contain the zero vector and be closed under scalar multiplication. A line not through the origin fails, and so does the first quadrant (not closed under negation).
              • 

            • "Rank is the number of rows, or the number of non-zero rows of the original matrix." It is the number of pivots after elimination. Rows can be dependent without looking it.
              • 

            • "Row rank and column rank are different things." They are always equal, which is why "the rank" is well defined — and why the demo's two methods must agree.
              • 

            • "$A^{-1}b$ is how you solve a system." It is a definition, not an algorithm. Solving by LU is about three times cheaper and more accurate, and for sparse matrices the inverse may not even fit in memory.
              • 

            • "If the answer is wrong, the problem must be ill-conditioned." The experiment's matrix is perfectly well conditioned and the naive algorithm still returned zero instead of one. Stability of the algorithm and conditioning of the problem are independent.
              • 

            • "Pivoting is an optimisation." It is a correctness requirement in floating point. Without it, elimination can lose every significant digit.
              • 

            Where it breaks
            Rank is discontinuous, and that makes it treacherous numerically. A matrix of rank 2 perturbed by $10^{-16}$ has rank 3 by any exact test, so every implementation needs a tolerance — and choosing that tolerance is the problem. The honest tool is the singular value decomposition, where you look at where the singular values fall off a cliff, which is M31.
            

            Partial pivoting is stable in practice but not in theory: the growth factor can be as large as $2^{n-1}$, and matrices achieving it exist (though they essentially never arise). Complete pivoting is provably better and rarely worth the cost.
            

            LU requires a square matrix. Non-square systems — least squares with more equations than unknowns, or underdetermined ones — need QR or the SVD, and the right notion of "solution" changes from exact to best-fit.
            

            The abstract theory also assumes finite dimension throughout. Rank-nullity in its stated form fails for infinite-dimensional spaces, where a map can be injective without being surjective even from a space to itself — the shift operator on sequences is the standard example, and that is where functional analysis begins.
            In your real work
            Every system of linear constraints is this module. A layout engine solving for view positions, a least-squares fit of a sensor calibration, a physics solver resolving contact forces — all reduce to $Ax=b$, and all of them should call a solve routine rather than forming an inverse.
            

            Rank is the honest measure of how much information a dataset carries: a feature matrix whose rank is far below its column count has redundant features, which is exactly Packet C, and it is why collinear predictors break a regression. Dimension is the quantity that embeddings and PCA are about — M31 builds the tools.
            

            The pivoting result is the practical heart of the module. If you ever write elimination by hand — in a shader, in an embedded solver, in a quick script — omitting the row swap is a real bug that produces plausible-looking garbage. And the "solve, do not invert" rule is the single most reliable piece of numerical advice in this track: numpy.linalg.solve over inv, every time.
            Flash cards
            What makes a set of vectors a basis?
            It is independent and it spans. Every vector then has a unique expression in it, and all bases of a space have the same size.
            Why did adding a third spice packet change nothing?
            Because it was already in the span of the other two. Only independent vectors enlarge a span.
            State the rank-nullity theorem and its intuition.
            Rank plus nullity equals the dimension of the domain. The map flattens a subspace to zero and carries the rest across faithfully.
            What are the three conditions for a subspace?
            It contains the zero vector, and it is closed under addition and under scalar multiplication.
            What does the rank of a matrix count?
            The number of pivots in its echelon form, equivalently the dimension of its column space, equivalently of its row space.
            When is a linear system inconsistent?
            Exactly when the reduced augmented matrix has a pivot in the right-hand-side column.
            Why does partial pivoting matter?
            A small pivot produces enormous multipliers that amplify rounding error. Pivoting keeps every multiplier at most 1 and makes elimination stable.
            Is an inaccurate answer always a sign of an ill-conditioned problem?
            No. A well-conditioned problem solved by an unstable algorithm gives garbage. Conditioning is a property of the problem, stability of the method.
            Why prefer LU to computing an inverse?
            LU costs about a third as much, is more accurate, and one factorisation serves many right-hand sides. The explicit inverse of a sparse matrix is also usually dense.
            What does a change of basis do to the matrix of a linear map?
            It conjugates it: A becomes B inverse A B. Similar matrices are the same map in different coordinates.
            Stop when
            You can explain to a child why a third spice packet was useless, you can prove rank-nullity by extending a kernel basis and say which step needs the extension, you can reduce a matrix by hand and read off rank, kernel and consistency, you can state why pivoting is a correctness issue rather than a refinement, you pass the boss exercise without hints, and you reach for solve rather than inv without thinking about it.
